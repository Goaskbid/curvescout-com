Why this ride

726 km from Tutamandahostel to Cuenca, about 9 h 48 of riding. On the way it passes Cotopaxi, Latacunga, Chimborazo Reserve and Riobamba. The highest point is about 3,900 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Roadbook

  1. Quito start

    Quito sits almost on the equator and almost two miles up, and the two facts together explain a lot. At about 2,850 metres the air is thin, the sun is fierce, and the days are mild whatever the calendar says, so the city lives in a permanent early spring. It stretches for kilometres along a narrow Andean valley, with Pichincha volcano rising on the western side and, on clear mornings, the snow cone of Cotopaxi and other peaks visible to the south and east.

    This is the starting point for the Avenue of the Volcanoes, the name the German explorer Alexander von Humboldt gave to the double line of peaks that runs south along the Ecuadorian highlands, and a good ride begins here. Leaving the city takes patience, since traffic is heavy and the avenues are crowded with buses. Once you are on the Panamericana heading south, the road climbs and falls through green valleys and past farms and flower greenhouses, as Ecuador is one of the world's major rose exporters.

    Practical points count at this altitude. Engines lose power in thin air, and carburetted bikes can feel flat, while fuel-injected ones cope better. Weather changes fast, with sun and a cold downpour within an hour, so pack proper waterproofs and a warm layer, since the nights are cold. Quito has fuel stations and workshops across the city, and you should fill up before heading into the mountains, as stations thin out in the high country. Take care with the sun, which at this latitude and altitude burns quickly, and leave valuables out of sight when parking.

    Food in Quito is hearty, with locro de papa, a thick potato and cheese soup, and empanadas de viento, fried pastries with cheese and sugar. Try the fruit juices, many of them from fruits unknown elsewhere, such as naranjilla and tree tomato. The Mitad del Mundo monument, about 25 km north of the centre, marks the equator, though the real line is a few hundred metres away. The city's altitude can cause headaches in the first days, so drink water, go slowly and keep the first ride modest.

  2. Cotopaxi scenic

    Cotopaxi is the volcano every Ecuadorian child can draw: a near-perfect cone of 5,897 metres, capped with glacier, rising out of high grassland an hour or so south of Quito. It is one of the highest active volcanoes in the world, and it last erupted in 2015, sending ash over the surrounding villages and closing the national park for months. The park has since reopened, though access can be restricted whenever the mountain gets restless, so ask locally before you commit to the day.

    Machachi is where you fill the tank and eat.

    The riding is part of the pleasure. From the Panamericana, a paved road leads up into the park through plantations of pine and then onto the páramo, a cold, windswept tableland of tussock grass, lupins and low scrub, where wild horses graze and Andean foxes and, if you are lucky, a condor cross the sky. The surface is paved at first and turns to cobbles or gravel nearer the lake of Limpiopungo, a shallow lagoon at about 3,800 metres, ringed with reeds and birds. A rough road continues up toward the car park at roughly 4,600 metres, below the refuge, if conditions allow.

    Altitude is the thing to respect. Engines lose power in thin air and so do riders, and a headache, nausea or breathlessness is common if you rush up from Quito, which already sits at 2,800 metres. Stay hydrated, move slowly and turn back if you feel unwell. Weather is moody: the summit often hides in cloud, with the clearest hours in the early morning, and rain, hail and cold can arrive any time. The dry months, roughly June to September, give the best chance of a view of the cone, and it is worth asking about entry rules at the gate.

  3. Latacunga scenic

    Latacunga lies at about 2,750 metres in the high Andean valley that the explorer Alexander von Humboldt called the Avenue of the Volcanoes, and the name is hardly an exaggeration. On a clear morning the snowy cone of Cotopaxi, nearly 5,900 metres high, hangs above the plain to the east, with Iliniza and other peaks to the west. The town has a slightly worn, dignified look, with many buildings made from pale volcanic stone and plazas shaded by old trees.

    Volcanoes have shaped the place in more ways than the scenery. Cotopaxi's eruptions have damaged Latacunga several times, and the town was rebuilt using the volcanic stone, which is why it looks the way it does. Local culture mixes Indigenous traditions with Spanish colonial heritage. The Mama Negra festival in the autumn is a riot of music, costumes and processions, and it brings the whole community into the streets. The Saturday market in nearby Saquisilí is famous for its colour and its trade in everything from livestock to herbs.

    From Latacunga the riding is excellent. The Pan-American Highway runs north and south in the valley, with views of volcanoes at every turn. Westward, the Quilotoa loop climbs into the highlands past villages and to a spectacular crater lake. At altitude, the air is thin and cold, so expect your bike to feel slightly flat and nights to be chilly. Rain and cloud often hide the peaks in the afternoon, so go early.

    Latacunga is a sizeable town and has fuel stations on the main road, plenty of cafes, markets and guesthouses. Pujilí, nine kilometres to the west, is smaller but has fuel and a lively Sunday market. Try chugchucaras, a rich dish of fried pork, corn and plantain that Latacunga is known for, and bring warm layers and sun protection, since the sun is strong at altitude.

  4. Chimborazo Reserve scenic

    Chimborazo is a dormant volcano 6,263 metres high, the highest mountain in Ecuador, and it holds a remarkable record. Because the earth bulges at the equator, the summit is the point on the planet's surface farthest from its centre, further out than Everest, a fact that sounds like a quiz answer but feels real when you stand in the thin air at four thousand metres and look up at the glaciers. The mountain is also revered by the Andean peoples who live around it, as a powerful presence, and the scientist Alexander von Humboldt tried to climb it in 1802 and reached about 5,800 metres, the highest anyone had been at that time.

    The reserve around it is a stretch of high moorland, called páramo, a windswept landscape of tussock grass, lichen and sand, and it is home to vicuñas, the wild relatives of the llama, which were reintroduced in the 1980s and now graze in small herds beside the road. The air is thin, dry and cold, with strong sun and sudden showers. A paved road reaches over four thousand metres, and refuges higher up, which climbers use as a base, can be reached by gravel track. Nearby towns include Guaranda, a hill town of twenty-two thousand to the west, and Guano, famous for its woollen carpets, to the east, and the cities of Ambato and Riobamba are on the main road and have fuel and services.

    The riding to the reserve is an experience. The road climbs from the valleys through fields and eucalyptus plantations to the open páramo, with big sweeping bends, long views, and little traffic except for the occasional bus, truck or llama herder. Bikes lose power in thin air, so be prepared for slower acceleration, and altitude sickness is a real danger, causing headaches, nausea and fatigue; ascend gradually, drink water and take it easy. The weather swings: you may have sun in the morning and hail at noon, with fog hiding the summit, which is often clouded in.

    There is an entry procedure at the reserve gate, and the best views come early, when the sky is clear. Carry warm clothes, gloves and a windproof layer, because it can be near freezing even on an equatorial day. Fill the tank at Ambato or Riobamba, since there is nothing near the mountain, and eat a hot meal of soup and potatoes in a village on the way down.

  5. Riobamba scenic

    At roughly 2,750 metres above sea level, Riobamba is a high-altitude city that wears its height lightly. It sits in a wide basin in the central Andes of Ecuador, surrounded by volcanoes, and on clear mornings Chimborazo, the tallest mountain in the country at about 6,263 metres, stands in the distance with its snowy summit catching the first light. Because the Earth bulges at the equator, Chimborazo's summit is the farthest point on the planet's surface from its centre, which makes it a tempting claim for the title of the world's highest mountain.

    The city has a calm grandeur. Its streets are wide and laid out on a grid, the result of a rebuild after the earthquake of 1797 destroyed the original town. Ecuador's first constitution was drawn up here in 1830, so Riobamba likes to call itself the cradle of the nation. Markets fill the town on Saturdays, with farmers and weavers from the villages bringing produce, wool and animals in from the highlands, and the colours of the Indigenous skirts and hats are a pleasure to see.

    Riding in the Ecuadorian Andes is a lesson in altitude. The road north to Ambato and Quito runs along the Avenue of the Volcanoes, and the road west towards Chimborazo climbs quickly above the treeline into a barren high plateau called the paramo, where vicunas graze and the wind is cold. The air is thin, so engines lose power and riders can feel headaches or breathlessness, so go easy on the climbs and drink plenty of water. Tarmac is generally good on main roads, but cattle and dogs can wander out, and fog and rain arrive without warning.

    Riobamba is large enough to have everything you need, with fuel, workshops, hotels and restaurants all easy to find. Try hornado, roast pork served with potatoes, and the local ice cream made from fruit juice and ice, known as helado de paila. Early mornings give the clearest mountain views, so plan to be out before the clouds settle.

  6. Cuenca finish

    Cuenca stands at about two and a half thousand metres in the southern Andes of Ecuador, where the air is thin and cool, and the rivers run down from the high moorland of El Cajas toward the Amazon basin and the Pacific. It is the third city of the country, a place of red-tiled roofs, whitewashed walls and wrought-iron balconies that was granted World Heritage status in 1999 for its well-preserved colonial grid, laid out in the sixteenth century on the site of an Inca settlement. The Tomebamba river runs along the southern edge of the old town, and its banks are lined with old houses with balconies hanging over the water.

    The cathedral, known as the New Cathedral, rises above the main square with blue and white domes that are visible from almost everywhere in the town. It was built over many decades in the late nineteenth century and was never fully completed. Cuenca is also associated with a well-known product, the so-called Panama hat, which is in fact an Ecuadorian invention woven from the toquilla palm and exported from the port of Panama to workers building the canal. The best hats are still woven in the region around Cuenca, and several workshops in town let you watch them being finished and blocked.

    The riding in and out of Cuenca is demanding in the best way. The roads climb and drop through Andean valleys with steep slopes, tight bends and sudden changes of weather, and the surface varies from fresh asphalt to potholed patches. To the west, Cajas National Park rises above four thousand metres, with lakes, peat and rocky ridges, and the road crossing it can be cold, wet and foggy even when the city is sunny. Pack warm clothing and waterproofs. Altitude can affect you and your engine, so ride sensibly and carry water.

    As a city of nearly three hundred thousand, Cuenca has fuel stations, workshops, markets and plenty of places to eat, including the local specialities of mote pillo, a dish of hominy and eggs, and roasted guinea pig, known as cuy. Mornings are clear and crisp, and afternoons often bring rain. The air smells of woodsmoke, cut flowers from the flower market and warm bread. It is a delightful place to finish a long ride from the high volcanoes, or to start one.

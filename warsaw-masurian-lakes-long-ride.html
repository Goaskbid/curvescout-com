Why this ride

Lake country, long distance and a calmer Poland. Out of Pułtusk and back again: 491 km, roughly 8 h 28 in the saddle. The stops in between: Mikołajki lakes, Giżycko and Mrągowo roads.

  2. Mikołajki lakes swim

    Mikołajki sits on a narrow neck of land between Lake Mikołajskie and Lake Tałty, and just south of it the huge, shallow expanse of Śniardwy opens out, the biggest lake in Poland. Everything here is water. The glaciers that scoured this part of the north-east left a maze of lakes joined by channels and rivers, and the town grew up as a fishing village where two of them touch. Today it calls itself the sailing capital of Masuria, and in July the harbour is a thicket of masts.

    The best reason to swim here is that the lake is so unhurried. Water is shallow near the shore, warm by August, and clear enough that you see your feet. Public bathing spots are scattered around the town, along with the lakeside promenade and the old bridges that open for the passing yachts. A bronze whitefish king sits near the water, a bit of cheerful local mythology about a monarch of the fish who wore a crown, and children climb on it without apology. Fish is the thing to eat. Smoked eel, fried perch and pike-perch turn up on every menu, and the smoked varieties are sold from stalls near the quay.

    Just a few kilometres away lies Łuknajno, a small lake that is a biosphere reserve and one of the best places in Europe to see mute swans in large numbers. It is a protected area, so you look from the edge and leave it alone. Cormorants, herons and cranes are common all across the region, and in the evening the call of a crane over reeds can stop a conversation.

    The roads in the area are good, flat to gently rolling and often lined with old lime and chestnut trees that arch over the tarmac. They are lovely, but they are also full of tractors, delivery vans and holiday traffic on summer weekends, so treat them as a pleasant cruise rather than a challenge. Watch for deer at dusk and for speed cameras in the villages, where fifty means fifty. Mikołajki is large enough to have fuel stations and shops, and Mrągowo, a larger town about nineteen kilometres off, is the fallback. Come in June or early September if you can, when the lake is warm but the boardwalks are not yet shoulder to shoulder.

  3. Giżycko food food

    Giżycko was once Lötzen, a small East Prussian town, and the name change tells you most of what happened to this region. It lies between two large lakes, Niegocin and Kisajno, on a narrow strip of land that has been fortified since the mid-nineteenth century. The star-shaped Boyen Fortress on the Niegocin side was built in the 1840s to guard the eastern border of Prussia. It never fought a great siege, which is perhaps why so much of it has survived, and you can walk along the earth ramparts and gate buildings at no great cost to your time.

    The other landmark is the canal. A short waterway with a swing bridge connects the two lakes, and the bridge is turned by hand when boats pass, a bit of old machinery that still works and still holds up the traffic for a while. If you arrive at that moment, take it as a free pause. Sailors and canoeists fill the harbour all summer, and the town has a lively waterfront with ice cream stands and fish restaurants.

    With almost thirty thousand people Giżycko is the busiest place on this ride, which is exactly why it is the right place to eat. Look for the local lake fish, zander or pike-perch with potatoes, or a bowl of sour rye żurek served in bread if the weather turns grey. Beer is cheap, and dumplings are everywhere. There are supermarkets, petrol stations on the main roads in and out, and a bike can be left in town without worry in the daytime.

    The roads around town are flat and wooded, and the surface is variable. Fresh asphalt runs into patched seams, and bends tighten without much warning where a road follows a lake shore. Speed cameras and village limits are real, so stay within the signs. The Masurian summer is short and warm, September is calmer, and in mid-winter the lakes freeze hard enough for ice-sailing. If you have time for a short detour, Wolf's Lair, Hitler's wartime headquarters, lies in the forest near Kętrzyn, about half an hour away. It is a sombre ruin of concrete bunkers, and it sits very oddly among the birdsong and the ferns.

  4. Mrągowo roads scenic

    Mrągowo, the old Sensburg, is a lakeside town of about twenty-two thousand people in the heart of Warmia and Masuria, and the roads around it are the reason the ride exists. They are the kind that Poland does without trying: narrow country tarmac running between avenues of old lime, maple and ash, rising and dipping across moraine hills that the last ice age dumped here. Between the trees you catch flashes of small lakes, and in late afternoon the light comes in low through the leaves in a way that makes you slow down for no reason at all.

    The town itself curls around Lake Czos, with a promenade, a beach, and an amphitheatre that hosts a big country music festival each summer. Yes, country music, in Poland, in the middle of the lakes. It has run for decades and draws a crowd that arrives by camper and tent. Outside festival time the place is quiet, with an old centre of modest size and some restored buildings from the Prussian period, when the region belonged to Germany and the town had a different name. Much of that history is gone, rewritten by the border changes of 1945.

    For riding, think of the roads as relaxed rather than technical. The bends are mostly gentle, with the occasional tight one where a road drops to a lake or turns sharply in a village. Surface quality changes from smooth new asphalt to older patched sections, and you should expect gravel at the edges and the odd pothole after winter. Tractors are everywhere, and in a village the limit is fifty at any hour, with cameras and police who enjoy the rule. Deer and foxes come out at dusk, and storks nest on poles in the villages through summer.

    Mrągowo is big enough for everything you need: fuel stations on the edge of town, grocery shops, pizzerias, kebab counters and a few places that serve decent Polish plates. If the weather is fine, buy something from a bakery and take it to the lake. If it rains, which it will sometimes, the roads get slick under the leaf litter, so slow down in autumn and watch the shaded corners.

Why this ride

From Torquay it runs 452 km to Warrnambool; allow about 6 h 18 of riding. It goes by way of Lorne, Apollo Bay, Cape Otway and Twelve Apostles. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Roadbook

  1. Torquay start

    Torquay is where the Great Ocean Road begins, or ends, depending on which way you ride. It is a town of about thirteen thousand people on the edge of Bass Strait, and it has a reputation that has little to do with motorcycles: this is the capital of Australian surfing. Bells Beach, a few minutes down the coast, hosts one of the world's oldest surf competitions, held every Easter, and the town gave birth to brands such as Rip Curl and Quiksilver that have since gone global. Surf World, the museum in town, tells the story with boards, photographs and a lot of sun-bleached nostalgia.

    Coming back to Torquay at the end of a day on the coast is a pleasant thing. The town has everything a rider needs: fuel at several stations, supermarkets, cafes, and motels and holiday flats in all sizes. Its main street has surf shops and bakeries, and the esplanade lets you wander along the beach with a coffee and watch the surfers in their wetsuits. Jan Juc, a few kilometres away, is another surfing village that is quieter and more residential.

    The riding into Torquay depends on the direction you travel. From Anglesea the road runs through low coastal scrub and heath, with the sea on one side and a few breaks in the vegetation where beaches appear. It is a fine road on a good day, but the traffic is thick at weekends, and the surface can be sandy where drifts cross it. Coming from Melbourne, the main road passes Geelong and the long suburban fringe, which is not exciting but is fast. The key is to avoid peak traffic on Friday evenings and Sunday afternoons.

    Weather is changeable, as in all of Victoria, and the wind is a factor. The water is cold, even in summer, and the surf breaks come from big Southern Ocean swells. Sunset over the bay, with the light turning pink on the cliffs, makes a decent end to a ride. Have a meal, take the gear off and leave the road until tomorrow.

  2. Lorne scenic

    Lorne is where the Great Ocean Road stops being a seaside drive and starts behaving like a mountain road. The town sits on Louttit Bay with the Otway Ranges standing right behind it, steep and thick with eucalypt, tree ferns and the odd waterfall. About twelve hundred people live here, a number that swells enormously in the Australian summer holidays, when Melbourne decamps to the beach and the main street fills with surfboards and ice cream.

    For a rider, Lorne is the natural place to eat. The strip along Mountjoy Parade has bakeries, cafes and fish and chips, and the pier is a good place for a coffee with a view of the bay. There is a fuel station in town, which matters because the road beyond is long, and the nearest other sizeable town back along the coast is Anglesea. Weekends and school holidays are busy, and parking a bike is easier than parking a car, though a sand-covered bay can be tricky underfoot.

    The Great Ocean Road itself was built between 1919 and 1932 by returned soldiers from the First World War, as a memorial to those who did not come home. They worked with picks, shovels and wheelbarrows on a cliff face that drops straight into Bass Strait, and you can feel the difficulty in how the road clings to the rock. West of Lorne the bends tighten around Cape Patton, and the sea shows up and vanishes with every corner. Speed limits here are enforced seriously, and tour coaches are slow, so patience is part of the deal.

    Inland from the town the Otway rainforest has real depth. Erskine Falls is a short drive away, and the forest holds mountain ash, myrtle beech and ferns that look like they belong in the Jurassic. Koalas sleep in the trees, kookaburras laugh at dawn, and the air smells of wet bark and salt. Early morning is the best time to see the coast before the traffic builds, and autumn is a lovely season when the summer crowds have gone but the weather often stays mild.

  3. Apollo Bay scenic

    Apollo Bay is where the Great Ocean Road stops being a headline and starts being a town. It sits in a curve of bay on the south coast of Victoria, with the forested Otway Ranges standing directly behind and Bass Strait rolling in from the front. A population of about fifteen hundred swells in summer when holidaymakers arrive, and you notice the change in how busy the main street and the foreshore are.

    The famous road itself was built by returned soldiers after the First World War, starting in 1919 and finishing in 1932, as a memorial to the dead, and that history is easy to forget when you are focused on the next bend. Between Lorne and here the road clings to the cliffs and the views drop away to surf beaches. Inland from Apollo Bay, the ride changes character: you climb into the Otways, where tall mountain ash, tree ferns and damp green air replace the open sea, and the road narrows and twists through the shade.

    This is a good place for the practical things. There is fuel in town, which matters because services thin out toward Cape Otway and beyond, and there are plenty of cafes, bakeries and fish and chip shops along the main street, with a bay view if you sit outside. The harbour is a working fishing port, and the smell of salt and diesel is part of it.

    Nearby is plenty to see. Cape Otway Lighthouse, built in the 1840s, is one of the oldest on the Australian mainland. Koalas live in the gum trees by the road around Kennett River, and you can often spot them without a walk. Weather matters: the Southern Ocean produces rain, wind and sudden cold even in summer, so carry a waterproof layer. Early morning is the best time to ride, before the coach traffic gets going, and weekdays are calmer than weekends.

  4. Cape Otway scenic

    The lighthouse at Cape Otway has been guiding ships through Bass Strait since 1848, and it is the oldest surviving lighthouse on the Australian mainland. The cape is the point where the Otway Ranges meet the sea, and it is where the Southern Ocean and Bass Strait collide, which makes the water among the roughest in the world. Hundreds of ships were wrecked along this stretch, and the lighthouse was built because too many had gone down with their cargo and crew.

    To reach it you leave the Great Ocean Road and take a side road of about twelve kilometres through the forest, a pretty ride through tall eucalypts and manna gums. This is koala country, and you will often see them in the trees beside the road, sitting in forks like furry bags. The surface is paved, narrow and often busy with tour buses and rental cars, so keep your speed down, and watch for wildlife on the road, especially at dawn and dusk.

    The wider region is a mix of rainforest and heath. Mountain ash, among the tallest flowering trees on earth, grows in the gullies, with tree ferns and mosses beneath, and the soil is rich enough to hold glow-worm colonies in places like Melba Gully. Closer to the coast, the vegetation is wind-bent and low, and the cliffs are limestone and sandstone. Whales visit offshore from roughly June to September, and from the cape you may see southern right whales with calves.

    Apollo Bay, about eighteen kilometres away, is the nearest town and the place for fuel, food and rooms, since the cape has only a small cafe and a few cottages. The weather can change fast, and cold southwesterlies are common, so layers matter. The best light for the coast comes in the late afternoon, when the sun drops over the sea. There is an entry fee for the lighthouse precinct, which funds its upkeep. Pay it, climb the tower if the weather is clear, and take in the long view over the strait.

  5. Twelve Apostles scenic

    Everyone calls them the Twelve Apostles, which is generous. There were never twelve, and the original name was the Sow and Piglets, a rather less holy image that tourism officials decided to retire. Today about eight limestone stacks stand in the surf off Port Campbell National Park, and one collapsed in 2005 in front of a crowd of onlookers. The cliffs are made of soft limestone, and the Southern Ocean is working on them constantly, so the view is never the same twice.

    The stacks form because waves cut caves into headlands, the caves become arches, and the arches fall to leave isolated pillars. Eventually the pillars fall too. It is a process that is visible in a lifetime, and this stretch is called the Shipwreck Coast for a reason. A short drive west, Loch Ard Gorge marks the site where the clipper Loch Ard struck the cliffs in 1878, and only two young people survived, Tom Pearce and Eva Carmichael.

    For riders, the Apostles are the highlight of the Great Ocean Road, and also its most crowded point. The viewing platforms are reached by a walkway from a big car park, and the midday crowds can be thick with tour buses and selfie sticks. Go at sunrise or an hour before sunset, when the stone glows and the visitors are fewer. The road here is mostly straight and open, running across wind-bent coastal heath, and gusts from the southwest can hit hard on the exposed stretches. A windproof layer is essential even in summer, since the temperature can drop abruptly.

    The nearest big services are at Port Campbell, a small seaside village a few kilometres along the coast, while Timboon and Cobden inland are quieter dairy towns that also have fuel. Cobden is known for cheese and Timboon for whisky made at a local distillery. Watch for little penguins, which nest along parts of this coast, and for wildlife on the road at dusk, when wallabies and kangaroos are most active. Let the ocean do the talking.

  6. Port Campbell scenic

    Port Campbell is a small coastal town of Victoria's southwest, set into a narrow gap in the limestone cliffs and facing the wild Southern Ocean. It is the base for the most famous part of the Great Ocean Road, and its little harbour and beach are protected by a headland, so the town feels sheltered while the coastline on both sides is raw. The nearest bigger places, Timboon and Cobden, are inland and a long way off in the paddocks, so the town has to serve everyone who comes along the coast with fuel, food and a bed.

    The sea has been carving this coast for millions of years. The soft limestone stands in cliffs of sixty metres or so, and the waves cut caves, arches and stacks, of which the Twelve Apostles, about twelve kilometres east, are the best known. They are fewer than twelve now, as stacks fall every few decades. The collapse of London Bridge in 1990, a double-arched rock formation, left two tourists stranded on the outer part and rescued by helicopter. Nearby, Loch Ard Gorge is named after a clipper that wrecked here in 1878, when two young survivors, Tom Pearce and Eva Carmichael, were washed into the gorge and lived. The coast was feared by sailors and is called the Shipwreck Coast.

    For a rider, the road is as good as the scenery and not as simple as it looks. The Great Ocean Road was built by returned soldiers after the First World War and dedicated as a memorial to those who died, and it winds along the cliff tops with long views and broad bends. Here the road is straighter than the section further east near Lorne, and wind is the main trouble, as the Southern Ocean blows hard and cold, sometimes sideways. Rain showers come fast and the weather can change within an hour, so carry a layer even in summer. Tourist coaches and camper vans are common near the Apostles, and speed limits are strictly enforced.

    The best time to ride is early morning or late afternoon, when the light turns the cliffs gold and the crowds thin. Winter has a drama of its own, with big seas, but the air is cold. Port Campbell has a fuel station, a bakery and fish and chips on the foreshore, and it is a good place to refuel before riding on toward Warrnambool.

  7. Warrnambool finish

    Warrnambool is the western gateway to the Great Ocean Road, or the end of it, depending on which way you ride, and it is a solid, comfortable regional city of about thirty thousand, where the dairy farms of south-west Victoria meet a cold, wild coast. The town spreads along the shoreline, with long beaches, a breakwater and good sea views, and the centre has all the practical comforts of a place that does not depend entirely on tourism.

    The coast here is known as the Shipwreck Coast, and with good reason. Dozens of vessels went down on these cliffs and reefs in the nineteenth century, and the Flagstaff Hill Maritime Village in Warrnambool tells their story. The most famous wreck is the Loch Ard, which sank in 1878; only two young people survived, a ship's apprentice named Tom Pearce and a passenger, Eva Carmichael, and the gorge where they came ashore is a short ride from the road further east. Also important is the whale story. Southern right whales come to Logans Beach, just east of the town centre, between about May and October to give birth, and a viewing platform lets you watch them from the cliff. It is one of the best land-based whale-watching spots in the country.

    The riding around Warrnambool is more relaxed than the twisting section near Lorne. Roads run through flat farmland with long straights and wide views, and the wind off the Southern Ocean can be fierce, so a firm grip on the bars is wise. Tower Hill, a collapsed volcano a few kilometres west, has a lake inside the crater and walking tracks where you may spot emus, koalas and kangaroos. The old town of Koroit has handsome Irish-influenced buildings and a long history of potato farming.

    Fuel is easy here, along with food of all kinds, from fish and chips on the foreshore to cheese from the dairy farms nearby. The weather is changeable, and cold fronts can bring heavy rain, so bring a waterproof layer.

    If you have time, the road east toward the Twelve Apostles is one of the great coastal rides in the world, and the light in the late afternoon is superb.

Zvenigorod and New Jerusalem

West of Moscow through the Istra valley: two monasteries, pine forest and the quietest roads within an hour of the city.

Why this ride

Roadbook

  1. Istra start

    Istra is a Moscow Oblast town of about thirty-three thousand on the river of the same name, an hour or so west of the capital depending on how badly the Volokolamsk highway has behaved that day. Its great claim is the New Jerusalem Monastery, which stands on a bluff above the water and looks like a stage set for an entire other country. Patriarch Nikon founded it in the 1650s with the idea of recreating the holy places of Palestine in the Russian countryside, and the huge rotunda of its Resurrection Cathedral was modelled on the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. The river was even renamed the Jordan, and nearby hills got names to match.

    The monastery's later story is a reminder of how close the front came. In the Battle of Moscow in late 1941, German forces reached this area, and in December, as they fell back, they mined and blew up the cathedral. What you see today is the product of decades of restoration, and the soaring tented roofs and glazed tile decoration still look different in different light. Even if you do not go inside, ride past and look back at it from the road along the water.

    The reservoir behind the town was created in the 1930s as part of the water system feeding the capital, and the roads around it wander through birch and pine woods, dacha villages and open fields. Surfaces are mixed. Main roads are wide and decent, and the lanes toward the shoreline can be patched, potholed and sandy in the shoulders, so keep your speed honest. Weekend traffic from Moscow is heavy, with long convoys heading to country houses, so a Sunday afternoon return is a very different thing to a Tuesday morning start. Look out for speed cameras, which are common on the main roads, and for dogs that wander the village streets.

    Practically, Istra is large enough for fuel stations on the main roads and plenty of cafes serving soup, pelmeni and tea. Snegiri, about ten kilometres off, is smaller but sits on the same approaches, and for anything unusual you will probably need to head for Moscow itself. Give yourself an early start, take the side roads when you can, and leave time for the monastery walls at golden hour.

  2. New Jerusalem Monastery scenic

    The monastery at Istra is a replica of Jerusalem, and the man who built it never meant it as a joke. Patriarch Nikon, the most powerful churchman in seventeenth-century Russia, founded New Jerusalem in 1656, and he set out to recreate the holy places of Palestine in the Russian countryside. The great Resurrection Cathedral copies the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, with a vast rotunda over a representation of the tomb, and the river nearby was renamed the Jordan, the hills around it given the names of Tabor and Olivet. For a while it was one of the most ambitious building projects in the country.

    Nikon fell out with Tsar Alexei, and was stripped of his office and exiled, and the monastery was left unfinished for years. The architecture that followed is lovely in a restless way, with towers, tiled stoves and decorated walls. In December 1941, during the battle for Moscow, retreating German troops blew up the cathedral and the bell tower, leaving a smoking ruin. The restoration took decades, and the rebuilt rotunda is a quiet testimony to the effort. Inside, the cathedral is a vast, echoing space, and from the walls you can see the winding river and the fields beyond.

    For a rider coming out of Moscow this is a natural half-day, close to the capital but far enough to feel like a different world. The roads in the area run west of the city through woodland, small towns and old villages, and the surface, as in most of the Moscow region, can be uneven, with patched asphalt and the odd hole, so it pays to keep an eye on the road ahead. Weekend traffic is heavy, and the main roads through Istra can be slow, so set off early. In the morning, the walls are quiet and the light on the stone is lovely.

    Istra is a town of over thirty thousand people and has everything you need: fuel, cafes, shops and a few places to sit with a view of the walls. It makes sense to eat here rather than looking for something near the monastery, where the options are limited and expensive. Check opening times for the cathedral before you come, since the monastery is a museum and has its own schedule, and carry something to cover your head and shoulders if you plan to enter the church.

  3. Zvenigorod scenic

    Zvenigorod is one of the old towns of the Moscow region, set on a high bluff above the Moskva River about fifty kilometres west of the capital, and it has long been a favourite place for city people to escape to. Its Russian nickname, the Russian Switzerland, is generous, but the wooded hills, the river bends and the onion domes among the birches do make a gentle, harmonious landscape. About sixteen thousand people live here today, and the place has fuel, shops, cafés and a quiet, provincial air.

    The town's story begins in the fourteenth century, when it was the seat of a princely appanage, and a fortress stood on the hill called Gorodok. The Dormition Cathedral there, built around 1399, is one of the oldest surviving stone churches in the region and contains remnants of medieval frescoes attributed to the school of Andrei Rublev. A few kilometres away, the Savvino-Storozhevsky Monastery, founded in 1398 by the monk Savva at the invitation of Prince Yuri, is a handsome walled complex with white-stone churches, bright towers and a long role in the history of Russia's tsars, who favoured it as a retreat.

    In the nineteenth century, Zvenigorod became a summer resort, and artists and writers came to stay. The painter Isaac Levitan worked here in the 1880s and left landscapes that made the hills famous, and Anton Chekhov served as a district doctor in 1884 and 1885, treating local people while he wrote. Their names are still tied to the town, with memorial museums and walks.

    For a rider, the roads in the area are gentle and scenic, with smooth country lanes winding through birch forest and meadow, fields of rye and small villages with wooden houses. Mozhaisk Highway is busy, particularly at weekends when Muscovites head for their dachas, so go early. Summer is warm and green, autumn is golden, and winter is cold and snowy, and the surface can be icy. Fill up before you go, enjoy a plate of pelmeni or a bowl of soup in a café, and walk the monastery walls in the evening.

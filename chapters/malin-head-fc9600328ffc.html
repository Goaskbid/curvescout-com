Malin Head

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The headland has a name that most people know without knowing why: Malin is one of the sea areas in the shipping forecast, and the weather reports for it are read out on radio across the British Isles. Standing here in a gale, you understand why.

The ride up has been long. The final stretch from Carndonagh winds across a bare, windswept landscape of low hills, bog and stone walls, with tight bends, narrow lanes and little traffic outside summer. The surface is generally fine but can be wet and loose with sand and gravel, and the wind is the main danger, especially on the exposed headland, where gusts can knock a bike sideways. Come in the evening in summer for long light, or on a clear winter day for the sharpest air, but dress for weather in any season.

At the very tip, a rock called Banba's Crown marks the most northerly spot, and an old stone signal tower, built in 1805 against the threat of Napoleon, stands on the headland. Later, Lloyd's of London used it to watch shipping. The cliffs and coves nearby have names like Hell's Hole, a deep, noisy chasm where waves boom into the rock. Scenes from a recent Star Wars film were shot on the headland, so some visitors come for that. On dark winter nights, the northern lights are sometimes visible over the sea, since Malin is one of the best places on the island for them.

Fuel is a point to plan. There is little at the head itself, so fill up at Carndonagh, sixteen kilometres away, or at Buncrana, twenty-eight kilometres, which is a larger town. Carndonagh has a pub, shops and an old high cross, a carved stone cross from the seventh century that stands beside the church. A pint and a bowl of soup there is the traditional reward.

Stay for as long as the wind lets you. The sea here is wide, cold and endless, and the sense of arriving at an edge is strong.

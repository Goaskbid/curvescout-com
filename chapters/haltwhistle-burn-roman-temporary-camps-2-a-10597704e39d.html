Haltwhistle Burn Roman temporary camps 2 a

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Haltwhistle Burn is a small stream that cuts down toward the South Tyne just east of Haltwhistle, and the name is one of those wonderful northern English mouthfuls that seems to have been in use forever. Here, beside the burn, the Romans left a cluster of temporary camps, several overlapping enclosures that show how often armies returned to the same convenient ground. Alongside them lies a fort on the Stanegate, the military road that predates Hadrian's Wall. The camps are low earthworks in pasture, best appreciated in slanting light and with a little imagination.

Haltwhistle, about two kilometres away, bills itself as the centre of Britain, a claim that rests on some measuring of the island's extremes and is mostly good for a laugh and a postcard. It is a small town with a long main street, a petrol station, a couple of bakeries and pubs, and a railway station on the line between Newcastle and Carlisle. For anything bigger, such as a large supermarket or a garage that can fix a puncture, the nearest option is Hexham, about twenty-two kilometres east.

The riding here is gentler than on the high stretch of the Military Road a little farther north, and a lot quieter. The Stanegate itself survives in places as a lane, running along the south side of the valley, with the Tyne glinting below and the wall's line visible on the ridge across the water. What you get is rolling farmland, hedges, small woods, hay meadows in June, and a fair amount of rain. Cattle grids and sheep are the main hazards, plus mud on the road after farm vehicles pass.

There is a different kind of history in the valley too: coal and lead were mined in the nearby hills, and the railway came in the nineteenth century, linking this empty-feeling country to the industrial towns on both coasts. Old stone barns and farmsteads are scattered through the fields, many of them built with stone robbed from the Roman remains. It is an old English habit, reusing what the Romans left.

A pleasant way to spend an hour is to take a walk beside the burn, listening to the water and the birds, and then ride back along the valley road to Haltwhistle for a pie and a cup of tea before moving on.

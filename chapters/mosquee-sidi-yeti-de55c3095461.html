Mosquée Sidi Yeti

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Djerba is the sort of island where you can ride for an hour and never see anything higher than a date palm. The mosque of Sidi Yati sits on the flat interior, a short run from Erriadh, which is the nearest proper town, with Midoun and the capital Houmt Souk a little further out. Everything here is low, white and thick-walled, and the mosques follow the same rule: buttressed, squat, almost fortress-like, with small windows and none of the soaring minarets you might expect from the mainland.

There is a reason they look defensive. Djerba has been raided and occupied by a long list of visitors, from Normans and Spaniards to corsairs, and the island's old Ibadi community built its places of worship to be both prayer halls and refuges. Ibadism is a branch of Islam distinct from Sunni and Shia, and Djerba is one of the few places where it still shapes everyday life. The island's traditional settlement pattern, scattered farmsteads and village mosques spread across the countryside rather than packed into one centre, earned a UNESCO listing in 2023, and this is exactly the landscape that listing is about.

For the rider it means unhurried going. The roads are narrow, mostly sound, and crossed by scooters, donkeys, the occasional cart and dogs who have opinions about motorbikes. Olive groves and palms close in on both sides, with whitewashed walls marking property lines, and corners are gentle bends rather than anything you would call a challenge. Speeds stay low because the island demands it, and that suits the place. Midday heat in summer is heavy, so go early, or in the softer light of late afternoon when the lime wash turns the colour of warm butter.

Practicalities are easy. Erriadh, Midoun and Houmt Souk are all big enough to have fuel, and cafes serving mint tea or strong coffee are never far. Erriadh itself is worth a slow pass, since its walls are covered in murals from an art project that turned a quiet village into an open-air gallery. If you arrive by the Roman causeway or the ferry from the mainland, remember that Djerba has a long history as a place of trade, with a Jewish community at Hara Sghira that is among the oldest in North Africa. Take your helmet off at the mosque and look at how the buttresses are shaped. It is plain architecture, but it is plain architecture that has lasted.

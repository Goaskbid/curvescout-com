Watchtowers WP 8/11 to 8/28 including fort

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The Romans did not much care for the shape of the land when they drew this frontier. Around Osterburken the Upper Germanic Limes stops following valleys and ridges and simply runs in long, stubborn straight lines across the Bauland, the rolling limestone country between the Odenwald and the Jagst. The watchtower sites numbered in this stretch were once timber, then stone, posts spaced so that each could see its neighbours, strung along a palisade and ditch that marked where the province ended and the unconquered north began.

Osterburken itself is the reason to stop. It held a cohort fort with an annexe bolted on later, and the town grew a Roman museum around the excavated remains. The prize there is the collection of altars set up by beneficiarii, the soldiers posted to police roads and collect tolls, who thanked Jupiter in stone at the end of their tours. There is also a great Mithras relief found in the area, a carved bull-slaying scene that says a lot about what frontier soldiers believed in when the winters came. You do not need to be a history buff to enjoy it; it is the kind of museum you finish in an hour with your helmet under your arm.

The riding around here is gentle and honest. Roads run between wheat, rapeseed and dark blocks of beech forest, rising and falling over low ridges with long sightlines and very little traffic outside the commuter hours. The Kirnau stream cuts a small, green valley through the town, and the lanes toward Adelsheim and Seckach have that easy rhythm where you sit in third gear and let the bike breathe. In early summer the fields smell of hay and the light at evening goes golden across the plateau. In autumn, morning fog lies in the hollows and the tarmac stays damp under the trees well into the day, so be gentle on the first corners.

Osterburken is a proper small town with fuel, bakeries and a station, so it is a sensible place to refill both bike and rider before heading off along the frontier line. Adelsheim, a few kilometres away, has a pleasant old centre if you want a second coffee. The Limes itself is mostly invisible now, a low bank in the woods or a reconstructed tower here and there, so treat the ride as a game of spotting traces rather than expecting walls. That is half the fun: once you know the line was ruler-straight, you start to see it in field boundaries and forest tracks everywhere.

The West Hammar Marshes

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The marshes of southern Iraq lie where the Tigris and Euphrates come together on their way to the Gulf, a vast network of reed beds, lagoons and channels that was, until recent decades, home to one of the oldest ways of life on earth. The West Hammar Marshes are the westernmost, south of the city of Nasiriyah, and this stop lies about fifteen kilometres from the small town of Al Fuhud. UNESCO inscribed the Ahwar of Southern Iraq in 2016, along with the ancient Sumerian cities of Ur, Uruk and Eridu, which stood on the edge of this same wetland.

The Marsh Arabs, or Ma'dan, have lived here for thousands of years, building houses and guesthouses of woven reeds with arched roofs, moving about in long, narrow boats poled through the channels, and raising water buffalo. In the early 1990s, the government of Saddam Hussein drained most of the marshes, partly to punish people who had sheltered rebels and partly to open the land, and the wetland shrank to a fraction of its size. After 2003, local people broke the dams and the water returned, and much of the marsh has recovered, though droughts and upstream dams on the rivers still threaten it. It is an extraordinarily important place for birds, with millions of migrating waterfowl in winter, as well as the endangered Basra reed warbler, and there is a distinct smell of water, mud and reeds.

I should be honest that this is not a typical ride. Travel in southern Iraq needs careful planning, with advice about security, permits and checkpoints, and most visitors travel with a local guide. The main road from Basra to Nasiriyah crosses flat desert and farmland with heavy truck traffic and long, straight sections, and the heat from June to September is extreme, often above fifty degrees. Spring and autumn are better, and winter days are mild and sometimes rainy.

Fuel is available on the main road, and in towns such as Nasiriyah and Al Fuhud, but in the marsh villages you rely on boats and local hospitality. If you are invited into a reed guesthouse, expect sweet tea, flatbread and perhaps fish from the water. Always check the latest official travel advice and local conditions before you plan to go.

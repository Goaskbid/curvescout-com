Joganjiyama Kofun

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Not every great tomb is a mountain. Joganjiyama Kofun is one of the smaller, humbler mounds in the Furuichi cluster in southeastern Osaka, a patch of grass and trees tucked between houses, a rice field's worth of land that happens to be fifteen hundred years old. The ride labels it by a nearest town, Kashihara, but you are really in the Kawachi plain, where the Yamato kings built the huge keyhole-shaped tombs that give Japan's early centuries their name. Together with the Mozu group near Sakai, the Furuichi tombs were inscribed on the World Heritage list in 2019.

The shape is the point: a round mound joined to a square one, from above a keyhole, and the whole thing once ringed with clay figures called haniwa and a moat. Most of that is long gone. What you see today is a green mound with a fence, a signboard and an unexpected hush. Walk around the edge and notice how the neighbourhood has curved itself around it, streets bending out of respect, bicycles parked against the railings.

The riding here is city riding, to be honest. Small roads, narrow lanes, traffic lights, schoolkids on bicycles at three in the afternoon. This is a place for walking and looking, and your bike's job is to get you between mounds. Fuel is easy, since Habikino, Fujiidera and the larger suburbs are all just minutes away, and every other corner has a vending machine for a cold canned coffee.

If you want the grand scale, go and see the nearby tomb of Emperor Ojin, which is far bigger. What this small mound gives you instead is the sense that the big ones were part of an ordinary landscape of lesser tombs, built for family members and retainers, and that ordinary people have lived beside them for centuries. In autumn the leaves turn, and in April the surrounding streets are pink with cherry blossom, which is the best time to come.

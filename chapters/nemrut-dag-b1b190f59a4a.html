Nemrut Dağ (area 38.0,38.7)

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Nemrut Dağ is a mountain with a king on top. In the first century BC, Antiochus I of Commagene, ruler of a small kingdom between the Roman and Parthian empires, ordered a tomb-sanctuary built on a summit 2,134 metres high, and had it crowned with a tumulus of crushed stone about fifty metres high. On terraces to the east and west sat colossal limestone statues, perhaps eight to nine metres tall, of himself, gods who mixed Greek and Persian traditions, and guardian lions and eagles. Earthquakes and time have toppled the heads, which now lie in rows on the ground, staring out at the mountains.

Antiochus claimed descent from both Alexander the Great and the kings of Persia, and the sanctuary expresses that mixture of East and West. Inscriptions on the terraces describe his intentions and the rituals for his cult. Nowhere else does a small kingdom's ambition feel so large. The site has been a UNESCO World Heritage property since 1987, and sunrise and sunset are the most popular times to visit, when the heads glow in the low light and the range of the Taurus mountains fades into layers of blue.

Getting there is half the pleasure. The approach from Sincik or Kahta climbs through a hard, bare landscape of rock and scrub, and the last section to the summit car park is steep, with hairpins. Part of the way up can be rough, and the final stretch to the terraces is on foot over loose stones. The mountain is high enough that snow lies across the road from late autumn until spring, and the summit is generally accessible only from about May to October. Wind at the top is cold even in July, so carry a jacket.

Fuel is available in Kahta and Adıyaman, the nearest larger towns, and Sincik has a small selection. On the way, look for the Roman bridge at Cendere, built in the second century with a pair of columns for each emperor, and the burial mounds at Karakuş and Arsameia. Small guesthouses near the summit cater for those who want to see both sunset and sunrise.

Plan your summit visit around the weather and expect crowds at dawn in high summer.

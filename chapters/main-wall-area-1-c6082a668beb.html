Main Wall Area 1

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The Danevirke is the longest and most important archaeological monument in northern Europe, a network of earthworks and walls that stretches across the neck of the Jutland peninsula, and the Main Wall is its backbone. At this spot, close to the village of Dannewerk near Schleswig, you are standing on a grassy ridge that looks like nothing much until someone tells you it was built to stop armies. It runs for kilometres through fields and woods, and over a stretch of centuries it was raised, repaired and extended by Danish kings, from the early Viking age into the twelfth century.

The landscape explains why the wall is here. The peninsula narrows between the marshy valleys of the Treene and Rheide on the one hand and the long inlet of the Schlei on the other, leaving a land bridge only a few kilometres across. Anyone travelling overland between the Danes in the north and the Saxons and Franks in the south had to pass this gap, and the Danevirke closed it. A later brick section, usually credited to King Valdemar in the 1100s, is among the earliest medieval brick structures in the region, and a good example of how the building style came north. The monument was inscribed by UNESCO in 2018 together with Hedeby, the Viking trading town nearby.

For a rider, the interest is in how quiet and gentle the surrounding roads are. Small lanes follow the line of the wall through arable land, with gentle curves and fine views over flat farmland and the occasional wood. The surface is mostly fine but narrow, and there are few places to pull in on the busy stretches, so choose your stopping point with care. Traffic is light except on the main road to Schleswig. A museum near the wall explains the history with models and excavation finds, and walking paths along the earthwork give you the sense of scale that a road cannot.

Dannewerk itself is tiny, so for fuel and food head into Schleswig, about five kilometres away, where there are stations, supermarkets and a nice old town by the Schlei. The area smells of grass and wet earth, and the wind is rarely absent. Come on a clear spring day when the larks are singing above the ramparts, and you will find the whole thing remarkably peaceful for a place that was designed around fear.

Church of Sta. Clara and Visigothic Art Co

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Most people come to Mérida for the Romans and leave without noticing that the city carried on being important long after the legions went home. The little church of Santa Clara, tucked into the old centre a few steps from the main squares, is where that later chapter is kept. It houses a collection of Visigothic art, mostly carved marble from the sixth and seventh centuries: friezes, pilasters and altar fragments decorated with vines, rosettes and geometric knots, much of it pulled out of Roman buildings and recut for Christian use. If the Roman theatre is the big gesture, this is the quieter and stranger one.

Mérida was founded in 25 BC as Emerita Augusta and became the capital of the Roman province of Lusitania. Under Visigothic rule its bishops were among the most powerful in the peninsula, and the city built churches on a scale that rivalled the great sees. Much of what survives was reused again by the Moors, who took Mérida in the early eighth century and built the Alcazaba beside the river, using the same recycled stone. So the sculpture in Santa Clara is a good example of how nothing in this region was ever wasted, only rearranged.

As a stop for riders, it works best as a slow morning in town before the heat builds. The streets in the centre are narrow and partly pedestrianised, so leave the bike in one of the garages near the river or on the edge of the old town and walk in. Mérida has around fifty-six thousand people, with petrol stations on every approach road, supermarkets, a lively café scene around the Plaza de España and plenty of bars serving Extremaduran specialities such as migas, cured Iberian ham and the strong local cheeses. The ride then heads east toward the hills around Guadalupe, where the straight roads of the plain give way to longer, curving climbs through cork and chestnut.

A word on timing. Extremadura summers can top forty degrees, so go early, and check opening hours before you rely on any museum visit, since small collections like this often close for a long midday break and on Mondays. In spring and autumn the light is soft and clear, and the stone in the church looks as warm as honey.

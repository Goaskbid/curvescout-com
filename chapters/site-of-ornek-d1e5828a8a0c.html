Site of Ornek

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Ornek is a place where the Silk Road becomes tangible in the grass. It lies in the foothills of the Talas Alatau, at the western end of the Tian Shan, in southern Kazakhstan near the border with Kyrgyzstan, and it was a settlement of the Turkic peoples who dominated these steppes between roughly the eighth and twelfth centuries. Its earthen walls, the outline of its citadel and the lines of houses are visible as low mounds, and scattered around are balbals, carved stone figures with faces, raised by the Turks to honour the dead. Ornek, like nearby Kulan and Kostobe, was inscribed as part of the Silk Roads corridor running from Chang'an to the Tian Shan.

The landscape is the thing. Behind the site the mountains rise steeply, snow-capped for much of the year, and in front the land rolls away in wave after wave of grassland towards the Chu and Talas valleys. In spring the steppe is green and splashed with poppies and tulips, which grow wild here; by late summer it turns gold and dry. Horses and sheep graze freely, and you may see shepherds on horseback, a scene that has not changed much since the Silk Road caravans passed through.

Riding here means good tarmac in the valley and rough tracks everywhere else. The highway between Taraz and Bishkek runs along the valley floor, and from it gravel roads lead up into the foothills; the final approach may be little more than a dirt track. Distances are long, villages are small and services are sparse, so a dual-sport bike and some self-reliance make sense. Weather changes fast near the mountains, with afternoon storms in summer and cold nights at any time of year.

There are no towns of any size immediately nearby; Taraz, the regional capital, is the nearest city with reliable fuel, shops and accommodation, and smaller villages along the main road have basic shops and occasionally a fuel station. Carry extra fuel and water if you plan to explore. Local hospitality is generous; you may be invited for tea, bread and kumys, fermented mare's milk, which is an acquired taste but an important part of steppe culture.

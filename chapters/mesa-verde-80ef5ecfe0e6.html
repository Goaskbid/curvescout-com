Mesa Verde

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde sit in the folds of a high green tableland south of Cortez, and the first thing that surprises you is how far you still have to ride after the entrance gate. The park road climbs onto the mesa top and then keeps going for a good while, twisting along the canyon rims at a speed limit that will feel slow and turns out to be right, because the edges are close and the deer are careless. Treat it as a gentle, scenic hour rather than a spirited one.

The people who built here were Ancestral Puebloans, farmers who lived on these mesas for centuries and who moved their homes into the sandstone alcoves in the 1200s. By around 1300 they had left for good, heading south toward the Rio Grande and Arizona. Cliff Palace, the largest of the dwellings, was found by local ranchers in 1888, and the area became a national park in 1906. Several of the big sites are visited on ranger-led tours that need a ticket, so it is worth checking ahead rather than arriving at the gate on a whim. The mesa-top villages and pit houses, which you can walk to on short paths, tell the older half of the story and are often quieter.

For a rider the practical points are simple. Summer afternoons bring thunderstorms off the La Plata Mountains, and the mesa top can be cooler than the valley by a good margin, so a liner earns its place on the bike. Late May and September to early October give the best mix of light and quiet.

What stays with you is the silence. Piñon and juniper smell sharp after rain, the canyons fall away in layers of tan and rust, and you realise that this was a crowded, ordinary neighbourhood once. Fire has also shaped the mesa in recent decades, leaving stands of pale dead trunks among new growth, which gives the ride an odd, open, windswept look. Give it half a day if you can, and take a jacket you do not need.

Holly Beach

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Holly Beach has the odd distinction of being a beach town without much of a town. It is a long ribbon of sand along the Louisiana Gulf coast, backed by a scattering of raised beach houses and camps on stilts, and it has been called the Cajun Riviera by people with an affectionate sense of humour. The sand is brown, the water is warm and cloudy, and the horizon is full of oil platforms. It is not a postcard, and it is all the more honest for that.

The coast here has taken a beating. Hurricanes Rita in 2005 and Laura in 2020 both struck this stretch hard, tearing out buildings and rewriting the shoreline, and the community has rebuilt more than once. Many of the houses you see are new, raised high, and bright. The people who come here are mostly regional visitors, fishing families, and a few birdwatchers who know that the spring and autumn migrations along this flyway bring thousands of songbirds down onto the chenier ridges and the beach.

Louisiana 82 runs along the shore, and it is a different kind of ride from anything in the hills. The road is flat and straight, with the sea on one side and marsh on the other, and the interest is in the details: pelicans skimming the surf, a heron in a roadside ditch, the sudden whiff of salt and mud. Wind is the main factor, and a strong crosswind can make it hard work. Look out for sand drifting across the surface after a blow, and for wildlife on the road at dawn and dusk.

Services are thin. There are a few small shops and snack places that open for the beach season, but do not count on fuel here. Cameron is the nearest settlement, around fourteen kilometres east, and it is itself small; Sulphur and Lake Charles, a good distance north, are the reliable places for fuel and a proper meal. Take water and sun protection, and fit insect repellent in the saddlebag. Spring and autumn are the best seasons, with milder weather and the bird migrations. Summer is hot and sticky, and the tropical season from late summer onward means keeping one eye on the forecast.

Segment Río de La Sal - Pampa del Inca (area -26.4,-69.7)

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Out here the Atacama stops being a postcard and becomes a working landscape: pale gravel plains, dry washes, and a horizon that stays the same colour for an hour at a time. The Río de la Sal gets its name honestly. Whatever water the Andes send down this way picks up minerals on the trip, and what survives to the surface leaves white crusts along the banks. Pampa del Inca, the broad flat beside it, is the kind of name you only get where travellers have been crossing the same ground for centuries.

That is the point of the place. This is a piece of the Qhapaq Ñan, the Inca road system that ran from the highlands of Cusco down the spine of the continent, and the stretch through the Atacama region is one of the Chilean sections recognised as part of the World Heritage listing. The Inca did not build here to admire the view. They needed a dependable line across a desert where the next water could be two days away, so the track follows what little there is: dry valleys, the odd spring, flat ground for walkers and llama trains.

For a rider the reward is scale and silence, so plan around the lack of everything else. The nearest proper town is Diego de Almagro, about 37 km away, with some 18,000 people, so that is where you top up the tank and buy water and food before heading in. Do not count on anything out here. Carry more fuel than the map says you need, a litre or two of water beyond your own thirst, and a phone with the route saved offline, because signal is a rumour. Gravel and sand can show up without warning on tracks like this, so ride it as an adventure road, not a motorway.

Mornings and late afternoons are the best hours. Midday light flattens everything into glare, while low sun pulls out the ridges and the colour in the rock, and the air smells of dust and warm stone. The wind picks up in the afternoon and carries grit, so keep your visor clean. The altitude here is considerable even though the ground looks flat, and the nights turn cold quickly. A spare layer is cheap insurance.

The town you pass through on the way takes its name from Diego de Almagro, the Spanish conquistador who led an expedition south from Peru into Chile in the 1530s and came through this same high, dry country. His men suffered badly in the crossing, and many say that experience helped sour the first Spanish impression of Chile. Standing on the pampa, it is not hard to see why.

Ancient Dam Of Ma Rib Dam Of Aljufaynah

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Not every part of the Ma'rib story belongs to the famous main dam. The Jufaynah dam, or Dam of al-Jufaynah, is one of the smaller works in the system that the Sabaeans built across the valleys of the region, and it shows that the irrigation of the oasis was not a single structure but a network. Many wadis flow into the plain here, and the people of ancient Saba learned to channel each of them, building diversion walls, canals and distribution boxes so that the flood could be shared among fields.

Less is written about this site than about the main dam, and it is better to be honest about that than to embroider. What can be said is that it belongs to the same Sabaean tradition of dressed stone and mud-brick works, that it sits within a few kilometers of Ma'rib, and that its purpose was to hold and direct floodwater to farmland. The care taken in the masonry and in the water control follows the same logic as the larger dam, and together the structures reveal a society that organized labor and law around water.

The landscape is arid and open, with low stony hills, dry watercourses and scattered date palms where the water still reaches. The ground is pale, the sky bright and the air hot, and after rare rain the wadis flood suddenly and brown water fills channels that have been dry for months. The line between flood and drought is sharp here, which is the reason the dams mattered.

Today the area is dangerous. Ma'rib has been a front line in Yemen's war since 2014, with fighting, mines and a heavy military presence around the city and the surrounding hills. Foreign governments advise against any travel, and a rider thinking of the roads here should wait until conditions are safe and information is reliable. It is not a place to improvise.

In peaceful times, the sites around Ma'rib were reached on a short drive from the town, which offered fuel, tea and simple meals. A local guide who knew the dams and the tracks between them was a valuable companion, and a hat and plenty of water were essential.

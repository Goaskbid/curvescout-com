Liverpool - Maritime Mercantile City

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Liverpool turned its back on the land and faced the sea, and the waterfront shows it. The Pier Head is dominated by three great buildings, the Royal Liver Building with its two clock towers topped by mythical Liver Birds, the Cunard Building and the Port of Liverpool Building, known as the Three Graces, built in the early twentieth century when the city was one of the richest ports in the world. Behind them the docks, warehouses and merchants' houses of the old town tell the story of a city that grew from trade in cotton, sugar, tobacco and, painfully, enslaved people.

That last point matters. Liverpool's wealth in the eighteenth century was closely tied to the transatlantic slave trade, and the International Slavery Museum on the waterfront deals with the subject directly. UNESCO listed the Maritime Mercantile City in 2004, praising it as a key place in the development of global trade and port technology, and removed it from the list in 2021 after new waterfront development, a decision that divided local opinion. The Albert Dock, built in 1846 with cast-iron columns and brick warehouses, remains a handsome reminder of the city's heyday.

Liverpool is also the city of the Beatles, of football and of a distinctive accent, and humour is part of the culture. The ferry across the Mersey, immortalised in a song, still runs from the Pier Head to Birkenhead and Wallasey, and the Mersey tunnels carry traffic beneath the river. Riding in the city centre is a matter of one-way systems, bus lanes and cobbled dock roads, with heavy traffic at rush hour, so choose a quieter time. The road along the waterfront offers fine views, and the surfaces are generally good, though the damp can make cobbles and dock-side setts slick.

Liverpool has fuel stations on the main approaches and everything else in the centre, and parking for motorcycles is available in designated bays, so use them. Try scouse, a stew of lamb or beef and potato that gave the city's people their nickname, and finish with a pint in one of the old Victorian pubs. Allow at least a couple of hours for the waterfront, and visit the Three Graces at dusk, when the lights come on and the river shines.

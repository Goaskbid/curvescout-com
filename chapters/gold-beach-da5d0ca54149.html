Gold Beach (area 49.3,-0.6)

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

On the morning of 6 June 1944 the British 50th Northumbrian Division came ashore along this low, sandy coast between La Rivière and Arromanches, and almost everything you see from the dunes at Ver-sur-Mer is shaped by that day. Gold Beach was the middle of the five Allied landing zones, with Juno to the east and Omaha to the west, and it is the quietest of them to ride today: a string of small seaside villages, wide flat sands at low tide and a coast road that ambles rather than races.

Ver-sur-Mer is where the British Normandy Memorial now stands on the slope above the shore, a long colonnade carrying the names of the men under British command who died in the campaign. It is a sobering, beautifully made place, and the view from it over the beach makes the geography of the landing obvious in a way no map does. Nearby, Company Sergeant Major Stanley Hollis of the Green Howards won the only Victoria Cross awarded for actions on D-Day itself. A few kilometres west, the concrete caissons of the Mulberry harbour still sit in the sea off Arromanches like broken teeth, and the German battery at Longues-sur-Mer keeps its guns pointed at the Channel.

The riding here is gentle. The D514 follows the coast through Courseulles, Ver and Asnelles, with roundabouts, speed bumps and plenty of summer traffic, while the lanes inland towards Creully run between open wheat fields and stone farm villages with high walls. Wind off the Channel is a constant companion and can push a light bike around on the exposed stretches. Early morning or a late September evening is the time to come, when the coach parties have gone and the light goes soft and silver over the sand.

For anything more than a coffee, Bayeux is the natural base, thirteen kilometres inland, with fuel, supermarkets and the tapestry that tells an earlier cross-Channel invasion in the other direction. Courseulles-sur-Mer has fuel too, plus oyster stalls on the harbour and plenty of cafés. Ver itself is small, but there is usually somewhere open in season for a crêpe and a cider, which feels like the right way to sit for a while and look at the water.

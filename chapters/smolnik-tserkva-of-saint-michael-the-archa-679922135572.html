Smolnik-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Archa

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

In the Bieszczady mountains of south-eastern Poland, close to the border with Ukraine, a wooden church stands on a gentle rise above the San valley at Smolnik. It is dedicated to St Michael the Archangel and built in the style of the Boyko people, with three log sections stepping up from east to west under shingled roofs. Since 2013 it has been part of the World Heritage Site of Wooden Tserkvas of the Carpathian Region in Poland and Ukraine.

The church tells a story of absence. Before the Second World War, the Bieszczady were home to Boyko and Lemko communities, mostly Greek Catholic or Orthodox, who built these churches in their villages. In the years after the war, especially during Operation Vistula in 1947, they were deported or resettled, and many villages vanished entirely, leaving only cemeteries, orchards gone wild and the occasional church. The Smolnik tserkva survived and was later used as a Roman Catholic church.

The landscape around Smolnik is wild by European standards. Beech and fir forests cover the slopes, and above them the poloniny, high grassy ridges, offer views into Ukraine and Slovakia. Wolves, lynx, brown bears and European bison live here, and you may see deer or foxes along the roads at dawn and dusk. The rivers are clear and cold, and the air smells of resin and hay in summer.

The Great Bieszczady Loop is what draws most bikes here, and road 896 through Smolnik and Lutowiska is part of the approach to it. The roads are narrow, winding and generally well surfaced, with few towns and light traffic outside the summer holidays. The section from Ustrzyki Górne to Wetlina and Cisna is especially beautiful. Watch for wildlife, logging trucks and occasional gravel on corners.

Services are sparse. Lutowiska, five kilometres away, is a small village with limited facilities; Ustrzyki Dolne, twenty-five kilometres north, has reliable fuel, shops and banks. Fill up before heading into the mountains. Simple guesthouses and mountain huts offer accommodation and food: pierogi, soups and local trout. The Bieszczady have a reputation as Poland's last frontier, a place for people seeking solitude, and an evening by a campfire with only the sound of the forest is the region's real gift.

Fosse n°5

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Douvrin, Billy-Berclau and Wingles form a little triangle of brick towns at the northern edge of the Lens coalfield, and the old pit known as Fosse n°5 sits among them. It is not a famous site and does not need to be: its value lies in being one of the many pieces that make up the mining landscape UNESCO listed in 2012, a network of pits, housing estates, slag heaps, railways and public buildings stretching for over a hundred kilometres across the north of France. Seen from the saddle, the area is a patchwork of cités, fields, canals and newer industrial estates, with the occasional terril breaking the skyline.

The history here is about people as much as coal. Workers came from all over, first from the French countryside and Belgium, later from Poland, Italy and North Africa, and the mining towns became some of the most diverse places in France. Polish churches, Italian surnames and couscous on the menu at local cafés are part of that story. The pits gave work and shelter but also brought disasters, strikes and silicosis, and the communal memory of all that is still strong.

The roads around Douvrin are flat and practical. The Canal d'Aire runs close by, and following it gives you long, easy stretches with water on one side and old industrial buildings on the other. Elsewhere, expect towns blending into each other, roundabouts every few hundred metres, and the A21 and the N47 carrying heavy traffic. Old cobbles remain in places, and the cité streets often have speed humps and parked cars on both sides. It is not a region for knee-down riding; it is a region for reading the landscape at a moderate pace. Evening light is kind to the brick, and a dry day makes all the difference here.

Douvrin, Wingles and nearby La Bassée have fuel, bakeries and supermarkets, and Lens, with its striking Louvre-Lens museum built on a former pit site, is a short hop to the south. A coffee and a croissant at a local bar, a look at the pit buildings and the surrounding housing, and you will have absorbed more of the basin's character than any guidebook could give you. Ask an older local about the mine, and you may well get a story worth hearing.

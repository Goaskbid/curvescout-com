Namur

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Namur stands at the meeting of two rivers, the Sambre flowing in from the west and the Meuse running north to south, and above the confluence a huge citadel stretches along a rocky spur. The Walloon capital has been a fortress town since Roman times, and the citadel as you see it is a layered thing: medieval walls, a Dutch-era rebuild, and extra works added by the engineers who followed Vauban's methods. It is among the largest fortifications in Europe, and you can ride a long way up the hillside road to the top, where a panoramic view sweeps over the red roofs and the two river valleys.

Down in town the pace is easy. A university, a big cathedral and a cluster of cafes around the old squares make Namur feel lived in rather than staged. The painter and printmaker Félicien Rops, born here, has a museum in the centre. Locals are proud of their position as the gateway to the Ardennes and the Meuse valley, and quietly resent being overlooked for Brussels and Bruges.

The ride south is the reason most bikers stop. The road along the Meuse to Dinant winds under limestone cliffs, with the river on one side and rock walls on the other, passing Profondeville and the castle gardens at Freÿr. It is a gentle, flowing road in good condition, perfect for a Sunday, though it fills with traffic, cyclists and coaches at weekends. In spring the wooded slopes are fresh green and in autumn they turn copper; summer brings haze and tour boats on the water.

For fuel and food, Namur is an ideal break: more than a hundred thousand people, stations on all the ring roads, and a well-stocked riverside area where you can park the bike and grab a croque or a plate of frites. Walloon cooking leans toward hearty dishes, including rich stews and local beers.

Give yourself time for the climb to the citadel, and if you can, ride the loop early on a weekday morning, before the day trippers arrive.

Shobuike Mounded Tomb

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Some mounded tombs have a name, a story and a signpost. Shōbuike has a pond. The tomb stands in Kashihara, in the flat, tilled country of the Nara basin, a large earthen mound from the late sixth or seventh century, and it is one of the big monuments of the period when the burial customs of the Yamato rulers were changing from the gigantic keyhole tombs of earlier centuries to smaller, more carefully built mounds with stone chambers. Scholars have suggested different candidates, as is common for these tombs.

What a visitor sees is a large grassy mound with a path around it, a moat with water lilies in summer, and rice fields beyond. It is a calm, rural spot, with the sound of frogs in the evening and, in the early morning, mist on the water. Some excavated finds from tombs of this period include stone chambers lined with massive slabs, wooden coffins lacquered with a dry method, and objects like mirrors and beads, which are held in museums in Kashihara and Asuka. These museums are a good place to see what the tombs held, and they open at set times.

For riders, the area is a quiet network of lanes between towns. The main road through Kashihara is busy, with trucks and buses, but once you leave it, the side roads are narrow and calm. Surfaces are generally good, though edges can be soft and drainage channels deep, and farm vehicles are common in the planting and harvest seasons. Kashihara has fuel, shops and restaurants in abundance, and the neighbouring town of Sakurai, with sixty thousand people, is a base for more.

The seasons shape the experience. Early summer flooding of the paddies makes the fields look like mirrors, reflecting the mountains, and autumn brings the colour of harvest, while spring is a mix of blossoms and haze. Midsummer is hot enough to make riding gear a burden.

It is worth stopping for a while at the water's edge, as egrets and herons are frequent visitors, and the whole setting is a pleasant reminder that these monuments were designed to be seen across water and fields. Take a bottle of water, since there are few vending machines in the countryside.

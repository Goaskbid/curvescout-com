Kalai Hulbuk

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Hulbuk was the capital of a small principality of the Samanid era in what is now southern Tajikistan, and it is one of the most evocative ruins in the Khatlon region. Its location is on the plains south of Vose, near the Kyzylsu, within sight of the Afghan border country. Archaeologists dug out a palace complex of mud-brick and fired brick from the ninth to eleventh centuries, with remarkable carved terracotta panels and a ceramic tradition that left glazed bowls with sharp, graceful script. Some of this is displayed in a small museum on site, and part of the citadel has been reconstructed so you can see how the high rooms were arranged.

What you will notice first is the scale of the mound, rising from flat cultivated land, and the silence around it. The Khatlon plains are warm and dry, with cotton fields, poplar windbreaks, and herds of sheep being walked along canals. In spring the hills behind are green and carry wildflowers, and by July the ground goes pale and cracked.

Riding here is an adventure of a different sort from the Pamir Highway. Roads between Kŭlob, Vose and the villages are paved but unevenly, with sections of broken asphalt, patches, and potholes that can be deep. Livestock, donkey carts, and overloaded cars move at their own pace, and drivers overtake with optimism. It is not a place for night riding. Spring and autumn are the best seasons, because summers are very hot and winter sometimes brings snow and ice on the hills toward Kŭlob.

Vose, about eight kilometres away, is a town of more than twenty thousand people and the logical place to find fuel, bread, tea and a simple meal. Kŭlob, about twenty-five kilometres off, is larger and has more services. Fuel quality can be variable in Tajikistan, so top up where you see a busy pump, and carry extra if you head into the countryside. Locals are curious and generous, and an invitation to tea is more likely than not, so leave a little time in the day. Ask permission before you photograph people, and keep to the paths on the mound, since the walls are fragile.

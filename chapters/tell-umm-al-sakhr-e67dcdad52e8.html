Tell Umm Al Sakhr

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Tell Umm al-Sakhr is a mound on the plain south of Samarra, one of the countless tells of Mesopotamia, hills made of the remains of old villages, each built on the ruins of the last. Samarra is also a name in the story of prehistory: early pottery found nearby in the twentieth century, painted with geometric patterns and birds and fish, gave its name to the Samarra culture of the sixth millennium BC, one of the first farming societies of the Tigris valley.

What I can say is what the landscape is. The Tigris flows through a wide, flat basin here, with irrigated fields along its banks and open desert behind, and the horizon is long. Date palms and willows grow along the water, and in spring the plain turns briefly green, with wildflowers among the stones. Samarra, about eleven kilometres away, is the nearest town, with some 158,000 people, and it has the fuel and shops you would expect.

Like the rest of the Samarra area, this is a place to approach with care. Security conditions in this part of Iraq have varied over the years, and access to archaeological sites often depends on permission and local advice. There are no facilities at the mound, no shade and no water, so anyone who goes should carry what they need and travel with someone who knows the ground.

For anyone interested in the deep past, the sense of scale is humbling. Beneath your feet may lie the layers of many centuries, from farmers to caliphs, and the ground, strewn with broken pottery, tells a story that no signpost can. The wind is usually the loudest thing, and the sky, huge and pale, closes the view.

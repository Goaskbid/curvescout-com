Frontiers of the Roman Empire

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The Wall comes into its own here, at the Irthing gorge, where the line runs along a high shelf above the river and you can see why the Romans picked it. Birdoswald fort, known to them as Banna, sits right on that edge, with a long view down the valley and the ground falling away to the water.

What is unusual here is the sequence you can read in the ground. In the west the first Wall was not stone but turf, built in the 120s, and parts of that turf rampart were later replaced in stone when the engineers got round to it. At Birdoswald you can still follow the changeover, and the fort's granaries and the long Wall running east and west make the fort one of the better places to understand how the system hung together. Down by the river, the abutments of a Roman bridge show where the Wall crossed the Irthing, and the river itself has since wandered off and left them stranded, which gives you a quiet lesson in how long two thousand years is.

On a bike the approach is a country lane with a few blind corners, narrow enough that you need to listen for anything coming the other way, and the surface can be patchy after winter. It is a good road for a steady, unhurried pace and a bad one for being clever. Come in the morning if you can, before the car park fills and while the dew still sits on the fort walls, and bring a layer, because the wind off the moor is rarely shy.

Gilsland has a long history as a modest spa village, and Sir Walter Scott is said to have met his future wife here in the late eighteenth century, a bit of local lore the village is fond of. Fuel is a different matter: this is a thinly settled corner, so fill the tank in Brampton or Haltwhistle, both a few kilometres off, or in Carlisle if you want a wider choice. The inscription as a World Heritage site came in 1987, and it covers the whole frontier rather than one fort.

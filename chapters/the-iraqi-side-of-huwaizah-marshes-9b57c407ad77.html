The Iraqi side of Huwaizah Marshes

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The marshes of southern Iraq are one of the oldest inhabited landscapes on earth, and Huwaizah, on the Iranian border east of the Tigris, is the largest of the surviving wetlands. Here the river spreads out into reed beds, open lagoons and channels, and for centuries the Ma'dan, the Marsh Arabs, have lived on floating islands of piled reeds, in houses woven from the same plant.

Their houses, mudhifs, are guest halls built from bundled giant reeds bent into arched ribs, and some stand large enough to have the look of a cathedral. Water buffalo wade through the shallows and are the family's wealth, supplying milk and cream, and the people travel in long, narrow boats called mashoufs, poled through the channels. The way of life continues a tradition that many link to ancient Sumer, whose cities lay at the head of the Gulf not far away.

The marshes were nearly destroyed. In the 1990s, the government of Saddam Hussein drained large areas, building dykes and canals to punish the people who lived there and to remove a refuge for rebels, and most of the wetlands dried to salt crust. After 2003 communities broke the dykes and water returned, and part of the marsh revived. UNESCO listed the Ahwar of Southern Iraq in 2016 as a site of both natural and cultural importance.

Be clear that this is not a casual riding destination. Travel in this region depends on security conditions and permits, roads are limited and there are few services away from towns such as Basra, Amarah or Nasiriyah, and fuel and water need to be arranged well in advance. Heat in summer reaches fifty degrees and the water level varies with drought and upstream dams. Anyone drawn to the story should check official advice and think of it as a place to learn about rather than a route.

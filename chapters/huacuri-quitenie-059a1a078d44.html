Huacuri Quitenie

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Somewhere in the creosote flats north of Charcas, in San Luis Potosí, the land that the Wixárika people call Wirikuta begins to feel different, even to a visitor who knows nothing of its meaning. The horizon widens, the mountains of the Sierra de Catorce rise to the east, and on a clear morning the whole desert seems to hum. Huacuri Quitenie is one of the sacred places along the Wixárika pilgrimage route to Wirikuta, where pilgrims stop to pray and leave offerings on their way to Leunar, the hill they call Cerro Quemado, where the sun was born.

The pilgrimage itself is one of the oldest living spiritual traditions in North America. Groups of Wixárika, led by a mara'akame, a shaman, travel from their communities in the mountains of Jalisco and Nayarit, a journey of several hundred kilometres that once took weeks on foot. Today many travel by truck or bus, but they still stop at the sacred springs and rocks, taking part in rituals of confession, purification and renewal. The desert is not wilderness to them; it is a living map of their gods and ancestors.

Nature here is tough and surprisingly rich. Barrel cacti, yucca and agave dot the gravel plains, along with the small, button-like peyote that hides in the shade of shrubs. Hawks circle overhead, jackrabbits bolt across the track, and at night the stars are extraordinarily bright. Summer brings heat and sudden afternoon thunderstorms that can turn dry arroyos into torrents in minutes; winter nights can drop below freezing.

For a rider, the important thing is self-sufficiency and respect. Roads are few, mostly unpaved, often sandy or rocky, and signage is minimal; a dual-sport bike and good navigation help. Charcas, a former mining town of about thirteen thousand people, twenty-odd kilometres away, is the nearest place with reliable fuel, shops and food. Bring water and spare fuel for exploring. Do not pick plants, do not disturb offerings and do not intrude on ceremonies; ask a local guide or community member if you want to learn more. Ridden slowly and quietly, this is some of the most haunting country in Mexico.

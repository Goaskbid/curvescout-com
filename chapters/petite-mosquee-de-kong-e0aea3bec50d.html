Petite Mosquée de Kong

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Kong once had many mosques, and the small one is the better way to understand the town. Smaller than the Great Mosque, built in the same mud-and-timber tradition, it suggests how Islam here was not just a matter of grand monuments but of neighbourhoods, each with its prayer place and teacher. The Dyula, the Muslim merchants who made Kong a trading power in the eighteenth century, carried both goods and learning along the old routes from the Niger bend to the forest, and the town became a place where scholars copied books and taught the Quran.

The country around is wide, warm savanna, with red laterite roads, scattered shea and néré trees and fields of yam, millet and cotton. Riding up from the south, you notice the change from the humid forest belt to drier, more open land, and the light becomes harder and the horizons larger. The roads are usually adequate on the main routes and rough on side tracks, with potholes, speed bumps in the villages, and heavy trucks to watch for. The best time is the cooler dry season from November to February, though harmattan dust can dim the sun, and the rains from May to October make tracks muddy and rivers high.

To the east lies Comoé National Park, one of the largest protected areas in West Africa, where savanna merges into gallery forest along the Comoé river and elephants, hippos, buffalo, many antelope species and a wealth of birds make their home. It is easy to see why the park and the old Muslim town were put together in one journey, as both are about a way of living with this land. The mosques of Kong, with their rough mud surfaces, seem to grow from the same soil as the savanna itself.

For practical purposes, Kong is small and services are basic. Take fuel from a bigger town beforehand, such as Ferkessédougou or Korhogo, and carry water and food. A visit to the mosque should be arranged politely, preferably with a local person who can introduce you, and with modest dress, covered shoulders and knees. Ask before taking photographs, avoid prayer times, and consider leaving a small contribution towards the upkeep of the building. The yearly replastering of the mud is paid for by the community.

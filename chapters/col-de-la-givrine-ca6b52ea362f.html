Col de la Givrine

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The Col de la Givrine tops out at 1,228 metres, which is hardly a heroic height, but the pass has a character all its own. It lies on the Jura ridge between Saint-Cergue and the French resort of Les Rousses, and it is one of those places where the border is a few paragraphs of paperwork and a different bakery. Bring your passport or ID card, because the checkpoint is real, even if you are often waved through.

The land around the col is classic high Jura: long, undulating pasture dotted with spruce, dry-stone walls, dark forest on the slopes and the occasional chalet with a sweep of roof covering a farmhouse and barn in one. The name Givrine hints at frost, and with good reason, because cold air collects in the hollows and winter comes early. In snowy months, cross-country skiers glide across these meadows, and the pass is a well-known gateway to the trails that thread through the border area.

The riding is pleasant rather than dramatic. From Saint-Cergue the road rises in smooth bends, crosses the ridge, and then drops along the French side toward Les Rousses and the lake of the same name. The surface changes subtly when you cross, and French speed limits and cameras are not decorative. In summer the road is busy with cyclists and weekend traffic, and on foggy days the dark wall of forest feels close enough to touch.

Do not count on fuel at the col. There are a couple of places to eat and drink nearby, but for petrol you need Saint-Cergue, Gland or Nyon on the Swiss side, or Les Rousses and Morez across the border. Les Rousses is a lively mountain town with shops and restaurants, and has a long tradition of cheese and smoked sausage. The Jura here produces Comté on the French side, and if you pick up a wedge it will survive the ride in a tank bag.

The ridge also marks a subtle change in weather. Clouds often pile up against the Jura from the lake side, so the Swiss slope can be gloomy while the French side is sunny, or vice versa. It is worth stopping at the top for a moment just to let your eyes adjust to the sky, which seems larger here than the trees would suggest.

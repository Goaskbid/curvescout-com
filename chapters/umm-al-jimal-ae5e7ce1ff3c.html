Umm Al-Jimāl

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Umm al-Jimāl is a black town in a yellow desert. Built almost entirely of dark basalt, it rises from the plain of northern Jordan, near the Syrian border, about fifteen kilometres east of Mafraq. People lived here for centuries, from the Nabataean period through Roman, Byzantine and early Islamic times, and what survives today is an extraordinary rural town of houses, churches, water reservoirs, barracks and streets, many buildings still standing two or three storeys high. Because wood was scarce, the builders used long basalt slabs to make floors and roofs, supported on corbels and arches, and some of the stairways leading to the upper floors still cling to the walls. In 2024 UNESCO listed the site, recognising it as an exceptional example of a farming and trading town on the edge of the desert.

The name means mother of camels, a reference to the caravans that once passed through. Wandering among the ruins, with the dark stones absorbing the heat and the silence broken only by birds and the wind, you get a powerful sense of daily life in a town that faded slowly rather than being destroyed. Look for the Latin and Greek inscriptions, the crosses carved on lintels of the churches, and the huge cisterns that collected the scarce winter rain.

The Hauran, the volcanic plateau that stretches north into Syria, is a landscape of black basalt boulders, wheat fields and scattered villages. Bosra, just across the Syrian border, has a Roman theatre of the same dark stone, but the frontier is not a place to approach casually. To the southeast, the desert castles of the Umayyads, such as Qusayr Amra with its frescoes, are within reach along the desert highway.

The roads are straight, wide and mostly empty outside the towns, with long sightlines across the plain. Watch for trucks heading to and from Iraq and Saudi Arabia, sheep and dogs, and sudden potholes and speed bumps in villages. Wind and dust can be strong, and the heat in summer is relentless. Come between October and April.

Mafraq, the nearest town, has fuel, shops and simple restaurants. Bring water, a hat and sunscreen, and accept any invitation to tea; Jordanian hospitality is legendary. Check the travel advice for areas near the Syrian border before you go.

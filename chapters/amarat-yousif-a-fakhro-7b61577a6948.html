Amārat Yousif A.‘Fakhro

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Muharraq, the island where this house stands, is a flat knob of coral and sand just off the north coast of Bahrain, and in the age of pearls it was one of the richest places in the Gulf. The Fakhro family were among the merchants who made money in that trade, and their house, the Amarat Yousif Fakhro, is part of the Pearling Path, a group of buildings in the old town that UNESCO inscribed in 2012 as a testimony to the island economy that depended on pearls for centuries.

To understand why the house matters, think of how the business worked. Every summer, fleets of wooden boats left Muharraq for the oyster beds offshore, and the men on board dived by hand to the sea floor, held their breath for a minute or two, and came up with oysters in a basket. Merchants on land advanced money to the captains, bought the pearls, and sold them on to dealers in Bombay and beyond. The big merchant houses grew out of this credit system, and their buildings show it: tall rooms for receiving guests, strong storerooms, and a layout that mixed family life with business.

Look at the materials. These houses are built of coral stone, held together with lime and plastered in gypsum, with carved teak doors imported from India, and often topped with wind towers, open shafts that catch the breeze and funnel it downwards. Narrow lanes, shaded by the walls, wind between them in a way that keeps the interior cool without any machinery. In the heat of an August afternoon the difference is something you can feel on your skin. The houses are slowly being restored, and a few are open as museums, although times vary and are worth checking.

Riding on the island is slow, with narrow one-way lanes, parked cars and children playing football, and the best way to see the Pearling Path is to leave the bike and walk. The wider roads around Muharraq carry heavy traffic, and Bahraini drivers are quick. Fuel is cheap and easy to find in Muharraq and Manama, a few minutes away. Eat at a small cafe in the old town, order karak tea or Arabic coffee, and try a plate of machboos, the spiced rice and meat dish that is the Gulf's version of a national meal. Winter, from November to March, is the pleasant season, since summer humidity can be hard to take.

Gardani Khisor Settlement

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Khisor, or Hissar, is a word that turns up across Central Asia for a fortress, and Gardani Khisor is one of those lonely fortress-hills of the upper Zeravshan valley in Tajikistan. It stands a few kilometres from the river, west of the little town of Ayní, in country where the plain narrows and the Fan Mountains start to rear up to the south. I can tell you the setting better than the ruin, because there is not much published on the site and what stands is mostly broken walls and mounds, but the pattern is familiar. The Sogdians and their successors put small forts on rocky knolls to watch the river road and the side valleys, and the villagers below kept orchards and flocks within shouting distance of safety.

Take a look at what sits around the fort. Terraced fields climb the slopes, walnut and apricot trees shade the lanes, and the air smells of dust, wood smoke and crushed thyme when the wind turns. Snow lingers on the high peaks into early summer, while down in the valley the afternoons can be properly hot. Birdwatchers will find wheatears, choughs and the occasional eagle riding the thermals along the rock faces.

The riding here is mountain work, and it asks for patience. Between Dushanbe and Ayní you either cross the Anzob Pass or run through its long tunnel, and beyond that the road along the Zeravshan alternates between decent asphalt and a patchwork of repairs, loose stone after rain, and the occasional rockfall. Trucks grind along in low gear, and cows and goats treat the road as shared pasture. The best months are late May to June and again in September and October. Spring floods can undermine the bank, and winter is a different story entirely.

Ayní, about seventeen kilometres from here, is the nearest place with a market, tea houses and informal fuel stalls, but supply in this part of the country is unreliable, so fill your tank in Dushanbe or Penjikent and carry a can. Drinking water from the villages is generally safe only if boiled or treated, so bring a filter or tablets. Hospitality is a strong tradition, and refusing the tea and flatbread that are offered at a village gate is a hard thing to do gracefully, so build a margin into the day.

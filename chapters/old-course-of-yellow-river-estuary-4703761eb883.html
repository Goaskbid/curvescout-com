Old Course of Yellow River Estuary

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

The Yellow River is the most restless big river on earth, and this is one of the places it used to leave the land. Over the centuries it has carried so much silt out of the loess plateau that it keeps building its own floor, rising above the plain, bursting its banks and changing course. In 1855 it broke out in Henan, gave up its long southern route to the Yellow Sea and swung north to the Bohai Gulf instead. The channel that ended here, on the low coast of Shandong, is one of the old mouths of that restless history, and the land around it is still being made by mud.

Do not expect mountains or corners. This is delta country: dead-flat, wide-skied, with raised embankments, drainage channels, reed beds, tamarisk scrub and salt-crusted flats that shimmer in the afternoon. The roads are straight and long, often with trucks serving the oil wells and chemical plants of the Dongying area, and the riding is about distance and light rather than technique. Early morning and the hour before sunset are the times to be here, when the reeds turn copper and the haze flattens out. Wind off the sea can be steady and tiring, and in summer the heat sits on the tarmac.

What makes the place worth the detour is the birdlife. The delta wetlands are a major stopover on the East Asian flyway, and the protected reserve here and at the present river mouth holds huge numbers of migrating and wintering waterbirds. Spring and autumn are the seasons to come, and on a good day the sky over the reed beds is busy with geese, cranes, gulls and waders. Cranes and storks are among the species the reserve is known for, though you will want binoculars and patience, not a motorbike engine, to see them.

The data for this point gives no named town nearby, which tells you how empty it feels, so plan fuel and water before you arrive. Fill up in a city on the way, and carry a little extra if you can, because filling stations thin out on the delta roads. Gates and access rules around protected land can change, and some tracks near the mouth are closed to vehicles, so treat the final approach as something to check locally. Come to look at a coastline that is still arriving, grain by grain, from the other side of a continent.

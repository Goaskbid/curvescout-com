New Salem

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

New Salem is a hill town in the old sense: a scatter of farms and old houses strung along a ridge in the north-central part of Massachusetts, with no real centre and not much commerce. It sits just above the western shore of the Quabbin Reservoir, which is the thing that explains the landscape. In the 1930s the state flooded the Swift River valley to give Boston its water, and four towns, Dana, Enfield, Greenwich and Prescott, were disincorporated and cleared out. The reservoir that resulted is huge, roughly eighteen miles long, and the forest that surrounds it has been left to grow back with very little interference.

Why does that matter on a bike? Because this is some of the quietest, least developed country in the eastern half of the state. The roads wind through woods and across open fields, and traffic is light even on a summer Saturday. The surface can be patchy, with frost heaves, and the corners are the sort that appear around a stand of birch with no warning, so keep the pace honest. Route 202 and Route 122 give you the main axes, and the smaller roads such as the ones near the Quabbin gates bring you close to the water. Entry to the Quabbin reservation is by foot or bicycle at the gates, not by motor vehicle, so the water mostly stays out of reach from the saddle.

Nature has done well out of the flooding. Bald eagles were reintroduced around the Quabbin in the 1980s and have been nesting since, and the forests shelter deer, moose, bears and coyotes, so dusk is a time to be watchful. The air smells of leaf litter and woodsmoke in autumn, and the light on the water on a clear morning is worth waiting for.

Do not expect services here. Orange, the nearest larger town, has fuel and some food, and Athol up the road is bigger still, with more choice. Pack water if the day is hot, and treat New Salem as a stretch of road for thinking rather than for stopping. The old common with its white meetinghouse is the one place to pause, for the quiet and the view across a field that still looks very much as it did a century ago.

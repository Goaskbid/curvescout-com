Mount Amel Castles

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

It is a region of terraced slopes, olive groves, tobacco patches and stone villages, with the Hula valley falling away to the east and the long wall of Mount Hermon hanging over everything on a clear day. The nearest settlements are Ain Ebel on the Lebanese side and Qiryat Shemona and Metulla on the Israeli side, which tells you most of what you need to know about the place: this is a frontier, and the frontier shapes every road.

The castles in the label are the Crusader strongholds that once watched these ridges. Beaufort perched above the Litani gorge, Toron at Tibnin and Chastel Neuf at Hunin were all part of a chain built to hold the road between Damascus and the coast, and the same logic of high ground and long views is why the lanes here climb the way they do. The country between them is steep and twisty, with short, tight bends, rough patches of asphalt and sudden drops into dry wadis. Spring is the season to ride it, when the hillsides are green and the poppies are out; by August the slopes turn the colour of toast and the heat settles into the valleys.

I will be straight about the practical side. This is one of the most sensitive borders in the Middle East, and which roads are open, which are military zones and which you should not point a motorbike at changes with the news. Check current conditions and local advice before you plan anything here, and do not wander toward the fence for a photograph. On the Israeli side, Qiryat Shemona is a proper town of some twenty thousand people with fuel, supermarkets and cafes, and Metulla, the little border village at the tip of the Galilee panhandle, is good for a coffee and a quiet look at the Lebanese hills across the valley.

If you are here for history, bring a map and some patience, because the stones are scattered and often fenced off. Arrive with a full tank from the larger town, carry water in summer, and treat the day as a slow wander through a landscape that has been fought over for a thousand years and still has not quite settled down.

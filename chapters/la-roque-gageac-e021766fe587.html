La Roque Gageac

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

La Roque-Gageac is a village with a problem that most places would envy: it has no room. A single street of ochre houses runs along the foot of a limestone cliff, with the Dordogne on the other side, and everything else is piled up the rock above, with gardens, steps and old stone staircases clinging to the face. The cliff leans slightly outwards, which gives the whole place a precarious look, and there have been rockfalls in the past that have damaged houses. Even so, the people have stayed for centuries, and the village is among the most photographed on the river.

The setting also produces a microclimate. The cliff faces south and traps the sun, and shelters the street from the colder winds, so the temperature is noticeably milder than elsewhere in the valley. Palms, fig trees, bananas and bamboo grow in gardens, and a small exotic garden near the top holds more tropical plants than you would expect in the Dordogne. On a warm day the rock radiates heat, and the village smells of stone, river and lime blossom.

For centuries the river was the main road, and the flat-bottomed wooden boats called gabarres carried wine, salt, timber and barrels along the Dordogne. Today replicas of them take tourists for gentle trips from the village quay, looking up at the cliffs and the castles on the skyline, and it is a pleasant way to understand how the valley was used. You can also see traces of troglodyte dwellings in the rock, caves that were once homes and fortresses.

The ride along the D703 here is straightforward and pretty, with the river on one side and the cliff on the other, and the road is narrow and busy in high season. There is little room to pass, and the village street is tight, with parked cars and walkers filling the carriageway. Park outside if possible and walk in. The best light is in the late afternoon, when the sun lights up the cliff, and the best seasons are late spring and early autumn. In winter, the village is quiet, and some restaurants close.

Cafés and restaurants line the riverside, and a long lunch of duck or walnut cake in the sun is the proper response. For fuel, Sarlat-la-Canéda, about eight kilometres away, is the main source, and the smaller towns of Cénac and Domme have limited services. Fill up before you set off along the river.

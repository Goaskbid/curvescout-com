Gyeongju

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

For almost a thousand years, from 57 BC to AD 935 by the traditional count, Gyeongju was the capital of the Silla kingdom, and the city still behaves like a place that remembers it. The old centre is flat and green and quiet, with the great grass-covered burial mounds of Silla kings and queens rising among the houses and shops like a row of soft hills. Nothing in the city is allowed to climb too high, which keeps the skyline low and lets the mountains behind it stay visible.

The tombs are the signature. Daereungwon, the tumuli park, holds large mounds in rows, and one of them, Cheonmachong, the Heavenly Horse Tomb, can be entered, revealing the timber and stone layers of the construction and a display of gold crowns and ornaments. These crowns, slender and hung with jade, are among the finest of their age. Nearby the stone observatory Cheomseongdae, thought to date from the seventh century, stands in a field like a bottle, and Anapji, a pond with pavilions, is lit in the evenings and reflects the lights beautifully.

In the hills east of the city rise Bulguksa temple and the Seokguram grotto, built in the eighth century, with a granite Buddha gazing out toward the sea, and both are UNESCO sites. The road up Tohamsan to the grotto is narrow and winding, with fine views and a cool breeze, and it is a pleasant short ride, best before the tour buses arrive. Autumn brings red maples and ginkgo, and spring has cherry blossom in the park by Bomun Lake.

Gyeongju is also famous for Gyeongju bread, a small pastry filled with red bean paste, and for ssambap, rice with leaf wraps and a dozen side dishes. Fuel and meals are easy, and bike parking is straightforward outside the tomb area. Cycling is popular here, and rental shops line the streets, a sign of how gentle and walkable the town is. Spend a slow morning among the mounds and an unhurried evening by the pond.

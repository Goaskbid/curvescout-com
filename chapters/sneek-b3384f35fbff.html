Sneek

This is an existing place description, not a separately verified motorbike route. Seasonal, access and service information may have changed.

Sneek is the town the Frisians themselves treat as the capital of their lakes, and you understand why the moment you ride in: water on every side, masts poking over rooftops, and a skyline that is mostly sky. It sits in the middle of Friesland's low green grid, a few kilometres from the Sneekermeer, and the old centre is ringed by canals that still carry more boats than some roads carry cars. Its emblem is the Waterpoort, a brick water gate from the early seventeenth century with twin towers and a little bridge over the canal, once part of the town defences and now the picture on half the postcards in the province.

This is one of the eleven Frisian cities that the Elfstedentocht skating race links in the rare winters when the ice holds across the whole circuit, and locals talk about the last one, in 1997, the way other places talk about a famous cup final. In summer the town turns to sailing. Sneekweek, the big regatta on the Sneekermeer in early August, fills every quay, bar and campsite, and if you arrive that week expect bikes, boats and beer in equal measure. Ask for Beerenburg in any cafe, the herb-steeped Frisian gin that grandmothers swear cures colds, and try it after the riding is done.

The roads around Sneek are flat as a table and narrower than they look on the map. Dike tops and polder lanes run dead straight for kilometres, then kink sharply at a farm or a bridge, and the bridges matter: many of them swing or lift for boats, so a five-minute wait in the sun with the engine off is part of the rhythm here. The real opponent is wind. It comes off the IJsselmeer and across open fields with nothing to stop it, and a crosswind on an exposed dike can push a tall touring bike around more than any mountain hairpin. Watch for cyclists, who own these lanes by law and by habit, and for tractors dropping clay in autumn.

Practically, Sneek has everything: fuel on the ring roads, supermarkets, bakeries and terraces along the canals in the centre, so it makes a sensible base or a long lunch stop on a lakes loop. Park near the edge of the old town and walk in, because the centre is a tangle of one-way streets, pedestrian zones and cobbles that are friendlier on foot. Late afternoon is the hour to be here, when the light goes gold over the water and the sailing crowd drifts in to tie up.

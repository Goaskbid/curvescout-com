  1. 1. Velingrad

    Velingrad lies in a wide basin in the western Rhodope, with wooded ridges rising on every side and the high Rila range just across the valley to the north. It is a young town by Balkan standards: it was formed in 1948 by joining three older settlements, Chepino, Lade and Kamenitsa, and it takes its name from Vela Blagoeva, a teacher and socialist activist. What really defines it is water. Dozens of mineral springs bubble up here, hot and cool, and Velingrad styles itself the spa capital of the Balkans.

  2. 2. Batak

    Every Bulgarian schoolchild learns what happened here in 1876. When the April Uprising against Ottoman rule broke out, Batak joined it; when the rebellion collapsed, irregular troops surrounded the village and killed a large part of its people. Hundreds had sought refuge in the church of Sveta Nedelya and died inside it. Reports by foreign journalists and diplomats shocked Europe, and the outrage helped bring on the Russo-Turkish War that ended with Bulgarian independence. The church is now a memorial museum, low and dark, with the victims' bones in an ossuary and the marks of the violence left on the walls. It is not an easy visit, and it is not meant to be.

  3. 3. Dospat

    Up at about twelve hundred metres, close to the Greek border, a long lake with ragged arms fills the valleys of the western Rhodopes, and at its southern tip sits Dospat. The reservoir is one of the highest large bodies of water in Bulgaria, and on an early summer morning, with mist lying on it and not a boat moving, it feels more Scandinavian than Balkan. The town is small and mostly Pomak, Bulgarian-speaking Muslims whose villages are scattered across these hills, and the minaret of the mosque rises among the houses.

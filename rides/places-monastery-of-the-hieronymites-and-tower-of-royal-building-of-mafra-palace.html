  1. 1. Monastery of the Hieronymites and Tower of

    In Belém, the river Tagus widens toward the Atlantic, and the whole district has the feel of a farewell. This was where Portuguese ships left for India, Brazil and the spice islands, and where the returning ones anchored, laden with pepper, cinnamon and gold. A good deal of that wealth went into building. The Mosteiro dos Jerónimos, begun in 1501 under King Manuel I and paid for in part by a levy on the spice trade, and the Tower of Belém, finished in 1519, are the twin pillars of a style that takes its name from the king, Manueline, with carved ropes, anchors, armillary spheres and exotic plants in stone. Together they were listed by UNESCO in 1983.

  2. 2. Cultural Landscape of Sintra

    Lord Byron called Sintra a glorious Eden, and two centuries of romantics, kings and eccentric millionaires have tried hard to prove him right. The granite range rises abruptly west of Lisbon, catching Atlantic moisture so that its slopes are draped in mist and dense, damp forest of oak, cork, fern and exotic trees planted by nineteenth-century gardeners. On the peaks and in the woods stand the Moorish Castle, its walls snaking along the ridge; the Pena Palace, a riot of yellow and red towers built for King Ferdinand II in the mid-1800s; the Quinta da Regaleira, with its spiralling initiation well; and the exotic Monserrate palace. UNESCO inscribed the whole cultural landscape in 1995.

  3. 3. Royal Building of Mafra - Palace

    King João V of Portugal had a great deal of Brazilian gold and a vow to keep, and the result is the Royal Building of Mafra, a palace, basilica and monastery so large that it seems to belong to a different scale of town altogether. Construction began in 1717, reportedly in thanks for the birth of an heir, and the project absorbed enormous resources and tens of thousands of workers. The facade stretches for some two hundred and twenty metres, with the basilica in the centre and the royal apartments in the wings, the king's at one end and the queen's at the other, a long walk apart.

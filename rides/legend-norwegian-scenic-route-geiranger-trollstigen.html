Why this ride

Norwegian Scenic Route. fjord, waterfalls, hairpins. On the way it passes Norwegian Scenic Route Geiranger Trollstigen and Valldal. The highest point is about 1,200 m. Trollstigen is subject to seasonal closure - check it is open before you set off. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Norwegian Scenic Route Geiranger Trollstigen point 1

    This is the road to Dalsnibba, a mountain with the cheerful reputation of having the highest fjord view in Europe, at about 1,500 metres above Geirangerfjord. The side road is a toll road that climbs from the high plateau at Djupvasshytta in a series of bends to a car park and a viewing platform on the summit. The surface is paved, though the road is narrow and exposed, and it is generally open only in the snow-free months, from about late spring to early autumn. The summit can be in cloud even when the valley is clear, so be ready to turn back if the view is lost.

  2. 2. Norwegian Scenic Route Geiranger Trollstigen point 2

    From the summit of Dalsnibba you look straight down on the Geirangerfjord, and the first reaction is usually silence. The fjord lies about fifteen hundred metres below as a thin, dark, winding groove between the mountains, with a ship the size of a toy at the quay and the village a handful of roofs. It is one of the finest fjords in the world, and together with the Nærøyfjord it carries a UNESCO listing. The sides rise nearly vertical, and the waterfalls that pour from the rim have names that have gone round the world: the Seven Sisters, the Suitor opposite them, and the Bridal Veil.

  3. 3. Norwegian Scenic Route Geiranger Trollstigen point 3

    Geiranger is a small village at the head of the fjord, with a harbour, a church, a few shops and a number of hotels, and for a place with so few permanent residents it handles an astonishing number of visitors. In summer several cruise ships may be anchored at once, each one far larger than the village itself, and a stream of coaches and cars winds down the hairpins to see it. Despite this, Geiranger has a calm, almost domestic feel when the ships have gone, and the evening light on the water is lovely.

  4. 4. Norwegian Scenic Route Geiranger Trollstigen point 4

    The Eagle Road, Ørnevegen, is the one that leaves Geiranger to the north, and the viewpoint at Ørnesvingen is where it earns its name. At this bend the road swings round the edge of a steep slope with the whole Geirangerfjord laid out below, the village at its head and the mountains stepping away on both sides. It was built in the middle of the twentieth century, and the eleven hairpins that climb out of the valley are a fine piece of mountain engineering, with the gradient kept steady enough for the cars and buses of the day.

  5. 5. Near Valldal

    Gudbrandsjuvet is a narrow gorge where the river Valldøla forces its way through a slot in the rock, only a few metres wide in places, with water that has polished the stone into smooth curves and potholes over thousands of years. It sits in the upper Valldalen, between the fjord village of Valldal and the climb to Trollstigen, and the scenic route has built a set of steel walkways and platforms that let you stand directly above the roaring water. Look down, and the force of it is startling.

  6. 6. Near Åndalsnes

    Eleven hairpins stacked on a near-vertical wall of rock, with a waterfall thundering down beside them: that is Trollstigen, the Troll's Ladder, one of the most famous roads in the world. Opened in 1936 after eight years of construction, it climbs from the Isterdalen valley to a plateau at 858 metres, with steep gradients and bends so tight that buses and campervans must take them in several attempts. The Stigfossen waterfall plunges down beside the road, and on a misty day the whole scene looks like a set from a fairy tale.

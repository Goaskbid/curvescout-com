Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. The Hague

    The Hague is where the Dutch government sits, but not where the capital is, which is a characteristic Dutch compromise. Amsterdam is the capital, but the parliament, the ministries, the royal working palace and the embassies are all here, and the city has an air of quiet formality. The Binnenhof, a cluster of medieval buildings around a courtyard, is the centre of Dutch politics, and the Mauritshuis next door holds Vermeer's Girl with a Pearl Earring and some of the finest Dutch paintings in the world. Close by, the Peace Palace, a vast, fairy-tale neo-Gothic building finished in 1913, houses the International Court of Justice. The city has long been the seat of international law.

  2. 2. Utrecht-Domplein

    Dig under the Domplein in Utrecht and you hit a Roman fort. That is not a figure of speech: when the city was rebuilt after the great storm that brought down the nave of the cathedral in 1674, the foundations were already sitting on the foundations of something much older. The Romans built a fort here around the middle of the first century, on a low sandy rise beside the Rhine, and called it Traiectum, the crossing. When the legions left, the name stuck, and a thousand years of sound changes turned it into Utrecht.

  3. 3. Works along the IJ before Diemerdam

    Just east of Amsterdam, where the city's suburbs give way to water and polders, a narrow strip of land runs out along the old shore of the IJ and the IJmeer toward Muiden. The Diemerdam is an old dike, and the works along the IJ before it are part of the Dutch Water Defence Lines, the system of forts, dikes, sluices and inundation fields that the Dutch built over centuries to defend their cities by flooding the land around them. The Defence Line of Amsterdam, a ring of forts around the capital, was listed by UNESCO in 1996 and later joined with the New Dutch Waterline into a single site.

  4. 4. Culvert/Bridge Kromme Rijn

    A river that has lost its job is a curious thing to ride beside. The Kromme Rijn, the crooked Rhine, was once the main channel of the Rhine through the middle Netherlands, and the Romans used it as their northern frontier. They built forts along it at intervals, among them one at Vechten, just east of Utrecht, and another at Bunnik, and the river served as a road for supply boats. In the Middle Ages the Rhine silted up and shifted, and what is left is a gentle, winding stream with willows and farm bridges, in a landscape of meadows and moated estates.

  5. 5. Culvert near Offerhausweg

    The name tells you what is here, a culvert, a small tunnel for water under a path or a bank, and the humble nature of the place is its charm. The Netherlands is a country made of managed water, and this part of Utrecht, on the eastern edge of the city near the university campus of De Uithof, is cut by streams, ditches and old defensive works that were all designed to move water about, or to hold it back. A culvert is the kind of detail that engineers make a country work with, and that most travellers never see.

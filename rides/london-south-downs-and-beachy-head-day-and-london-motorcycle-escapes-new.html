Why this ride

A one-way ride of 496 km, Dorking to Ewell - roughly 9 h 50 in the saddle. On the way it passes Seven Sisters cliffs, Beachy Head and Lewes. The climbs: Ditchling Beacon and in Cobham. The passes are the reason to go.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Box Hill rider gate

    Box Hill is the Surrey Hills' best-known bump, and it earns its fame. The hill rises sharply from the valley of the River Mole to a crest of chalk grassland and ancient box trees, from which the land falls away in a wide view toward the South Downs. It is a National Trust property, and it has been a favourite day out for Londoners since the railway came to Dorking in the nineteenth century. The poet John Keats finished part of Endymion in the inn below the hill, and Jane Austen set a famous picnic scene on its slopes in Emma.

  2. 2. Ditchling Beacon

    Ditchling Beacon is the highest point on the South Downs in East Sussex, at 248 metres, and the way up from the north is steep enough to make a loaded bike grumble. The lane leaves the village of Ditchling and climbs the scarp in a series of narrow bends, with a gradient that is a serious test for cyclists, who treat it as a rite of passage, since the London to Brighton charity ride famously ends with this climb. A motorcycle takes it in its stride, but there is little room to pass, so patience is needed.

  3. 3. Seven Sisters cliffs

    Seven Sisters is the name of a row of white chalk cliffs that rise and fall like a wave along the Sussex coast, between the mouth of the Cuckmere river and Birling Gap. They stand about a hundred metres high, bright against the sea, and the view of them from the clifftop path to the east is one of the most photographed in southern England. The coast here is constantly being worn away, and every winter storm brings down another piece of cliff, so the profile you see is never quite the same.

  4. 4. Beachy Head

    Beachy Head is the highest chalk sea cliff in Britain, around 160 metres, and the place where the South Downs end in a white wall at the English Channel. Down on the shore a red-and-white striped lighthouse from 1902 looks tiny, and the old Belle Tout lighthouse sits on the next headland west, a squat white tower that was moved back from the cliff edge in 1999. Past it the cliffs run on in the long, undulating line of the Seven Sisters, now protected as part of the South Downs National Park.

    Read the complete Beachy Head write-up.

  5. 5. Lewes food stop

    Lewes is one of those small county towns where you can feel the layers of time on the street. A Norman castle, built soon after 1066, sits on a mound in the middle, and the main street, the High Street, climbs steeply toward it in a long curve of old brick, tile and timber. In 1264 Simon de Montfort defeated Henry III in a battle on the slopes outside the town, a moment often seen as the start of English parliamentary government, and the place has stayed a little rebellious ever since.

  6. 6. Back to Box

    Box Hill is the great chalk lump south of Leatherhead, where the River Mole cuts through the North Downs and leaves a steep slope of box trees, yews and wildflower grassland on one side. The box trees give the hill its name, and it is one of the few places in Britain where they grow wild. The National Trust looks after much of it. Down by the river at Burford Bridge, the poet John Keats finished his poem Endymion in 1817, and Jane Austen set the famously awkward picnic in Emma on the hill.

  9. 9. Brentford

    Brentford is where the River Brent and the Grand Union Canal meet the Thames, on the western edge of London, and for centuries it was the first important crossing west of the capital. It is a busy, mixed place of new riverside apartments, old pubs, warehouses and roads, with Kew Gardens just across the water and Syon Park, the London home of the Dukes of Northumberland, next door.

  12. 12. Near Stanford-le-Hope, on the way back

    Down where the Thames widens into its estuary, the Essex shore becomes a strange, flat, huge-skied place of marshes, sea walls, container cranes and old forts, and Stanford-le-Hope sits just back from it. The town is a commuter place of nearly thirty thousand people, but a few minutes' ride south the land opens up into creeks and mudflats where the river meets the sea, with ships gliding past towards London.

  14. 14. Back to New

    Ewell and Cheam are two of the old villages that London swallowed in the twentieth century, on the border between Surrey and the capital, with Nonsuch Park between them. That park holds the secret. In 1538 Henry VIII began building a palace here, meant to be so grand there would be none like it, and he named it Nonsuch. It was never finished in his lifetime and was demolished in the 1680s after Charles II gave it to a mistress who sold the materials to pay her gambling debts. The site was lost for centuries and was found again by archaeologists in the 1950s, and the park remains as a wide expanse of grass and trees, with the old mansion house of a later era at its edge.

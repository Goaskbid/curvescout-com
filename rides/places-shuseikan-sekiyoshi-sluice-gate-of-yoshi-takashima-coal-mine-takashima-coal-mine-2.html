Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Shuseikan / Sekiyoshi Sluice gate of Yoshi

    Water was the engine of Shuseikan, and the Sekiyoshi sluice gate is part of the story of how it was supplied. The factory complex that Shimazu Nariakira founded in the 1850s on the shore of Kagoshima Bay needed power for its machinery, and the answer was a canal that carried water from a river in the hills north of the city. The sluice gate controlling the flow still survives, a modest stone structure beside a stream, and it forms part of the World Heritage listing of Meiji industrial sites.

  2. 2. Sakitsu Village in Amakusa

    A small Gothic church with a spire stands right at the water's edge in Sakitsu, a fishing village tucked into an inlet on the southern coast of Amakusa's Shimoshima island. It looks almost Breton, a surprise in rural Kyushu, and the story behind it is one of the most moving in Japanese history. When Christianity was banned in the early seventeenth century, the people of Sakitsu kept their faith in secret for over two centuries, disguising prayers and objects as Buddhist or Shinto practice, and using things from their own fishing life, such as abalone shells whose inner patterns recalled holy images, as objects of devotion.

  3. 3. Takashima Coal Mine/ Takashima Coal Mine

    Out in the sea off Nagasaki, a low island called Takashima was the place where modern Japan dug its first Western-style coal shaft. In 1868, in the very year of the Meiji Restoration, the Scottish merchant Thomas Glover went into partnership with the Saga domain to sink a shaft here using steam-powered machinery from Britain. The mine later passed to Mitsubishi and became one of the pillars of the company's rise, fuelling steamships, factories and the rapid industrialisation of the country. Takashima is now part of the Sites of Japan's Meiji Industrial Revolution, listed by UNESCO in 2015.

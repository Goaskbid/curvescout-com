A 367 km round trip from Pythagóreio - roughly 6 h 20 in the saddle. The stops in between: Ephesus, Lydian Tumuli of Bin Tepe and back through Ephesus. It crosses from Greece into Türkiye - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Pythagoreion of Samos

    Pythagóreio is a small fishing harbour on the south-east coast of Samos, a Greek island that lies so close to Turkey that the strait between them is barely two kilometres wide at its narrowest. The village sits on the site of the ancient capital, which in the sixth century BC was one of the richest cities of the Aegean under the tyrant Polycrates. He built a great mole to protect the harbour, a temple of Hera that was among the largest of the Greek world, and a water tunnel that astonished the ancient world. The island was also the birthplace of Pythagoras, the philosopher and mathematician, and a statue of him stands on the waterfront.

  2. 2. Ephesus

    Ephesus once had a population of a quarter of a million, a harbour that brought in ships from across the Mediterranean, and a temple counted among the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. Today it has the best-preserved street plan of any Roman city in the east, and a crowd of visitors who walk down its marble roads each morning. The most photographed building is the Library of Celsus, whose two-storey facade, with its columns and statues of the virtues, was built in the early second century as a tomb and a library for a Roman governor, and rebuilt from the fallen stones in the twentieth century. The Great Theatre, carved into the slope of Mount Pion, held about twenty-five thousand people and is where, according to the Acts of the Apostles, the silversmiths of Ephesus rioted against Paul for threatening the trade in souvenir shrines of Artemis.

  3. 3. Lydian Tumuli of Bin Tepe

    Bin Tepe means a thousand mounds, and the name is only a mild exaggeration. On a low ridge north of the Gediz river plain, a few kilometres from the shore of Lake Marmara, the kings and nobles of ancient Lydia were buried under hundreds of earth tumuli, some of them enormous. Lydia, with its capital at Sardis, was the kingdom of Croesus, whose name became a byword for wealth, and the first state in the world to mint coins from electrum, the natural gold and silver alloy washed down from the mountains. The mounds date mostly from the seventh to the sixth centuries BC, and they were the funeral monuments of a rich, confident civilisation.

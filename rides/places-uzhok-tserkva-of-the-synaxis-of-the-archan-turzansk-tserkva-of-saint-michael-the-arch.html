Why this ride

On the way it passes Vihorlat, Turzańsk-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Arch and back through Vihorlat. It crosses Ukraine, Slovakia and Poland - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

The roads

Bieszczady Loop

It is 130 km from Vihorlat to Turzańsk-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Arch on Ševčenkova. Up about 430 m to 584 m, then 510 m back down the far side. Split it with a break around the middle.

The stops

  1. 1. Uzhok-Tserkva of the Synaxis of the Archan

    Start here.

    Just below the pass that carries the road and railway from Uzhhorod into Galicia, the village of Uzhok keeps one of the finest wooden churches of the Carpathians. The tserkva of the Synaxis of the Archangel Michael was built in the middle of the eighteenth century in the Boyko style, with three log chambers each topped by a tiered roof, rising in stages like a set of stacked hats, all covered in wooden shingles. It stands on a slope among old trees, with a separate bell tower nearby, and it is one of the sixteen wooden tserkvas of the Carpathian region in Poland and Ukraine listed by UNESCO in 2013.

    Read the complete Uzhok-Tserkva of the Synaxis of the Archan write-up.

  2. 2. Vihorlat

    97 km in.

    Vihorlat is a range of old volcanoes in the far east of Slovakia, close to the Ukrainian border, and its higher slopes are covered with some of the last primeval beech forests in Europe. The trees here have grown, died and rotted without human interference for a very long time, and the forest is part of the transnational UNESCO site of the Ancient and Primeval Beech Forests of the Carpathians and Other Regions of Europe, first listed in 2007 with sites in Slovakia and Ukraine and later extended across much of the continent.

    Read the complete Vihorlat write-up.

  3. 3. Turzańsk-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Arch

    228 km in.

    The wooden church of Saint Michael the Archangel in Turzańsk stands on a gentle slope in the foothills of the Bieszczady mountains, in the far southeast of Poland, near the borders with Slovakia and Ukraine, with its onion domes rising above the trees. It was built in the early nineteenth century by the Lemko or Boyko communities, the Ruthenian highlanders who once lived in these mountains, and it is one of the wooden tserkvas of the Polish and Ukrainian Carpathian region listed by UNESCO in 2013. Inside, an iconostasis of painted icons separates the nave from the sanctuary, and the church is still used for Orthodox services.

Why this ride

A 385 km round trip from Haltwhistle - roughly 6 h 38 in the saddle. On the way it passes Hadrian's Wall and vallum in wall mile 9, Campfield and back through Hadrian's Wall and vallum in wall mile 9. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Haltwhistle Burn 4 Roman temporary camp

    The Haltwhistle Burn runs south from the line of the Wall through a wooded gully, past the site of a small Roman fort on the Stanegate, the older frontier road. This label points to one of several temporary camps near it, the overnight earthworks of Roman armies on the move. Like others in the area, it shows as a low rectangle, visible in dry summers and under low light, and not much more. The Burn itself is more rewarding, a clear stream tumbling over stones between hazel and alder, and the walk beside it takes you to a quiet corner of the valley where the sound of traffic fades.

  2. 2. Hadrian's Wall and vallum in wall mile 9

    Nine Roman miles west of the wall's starting point at Wallsend, the frontier is already climbing out of the Tyne valley, and here on the edge of Newburn and Heddon-on-the-Wall you can still find it in the fields. Emperor Hadrian ordered the wall in AD 122, a stone barrier across the narrowest part of northern Britain from the Tyne to the Solway, and behind it the army dug the Vallum, a broad ditch flanked by earth mounds that marked the military zone from the civilian south. On this stretch the Vallum is often easier to read than the wall itself, a long shallow trough running through the grass, especially when the evening light comes in low and picks out every bump.

  3. 3. Campfield

    Out on the Solway coast west of Carlisle, the land flattens into salt marsh, peat bog and wide tidal sands, and the sky takes over. Campfield Marsh, near Bowness-on-Solway, is a nature reserve run by the RSPB, a mix of salt marsh, wet grassland and raised bog, and in winter it fills with tens of thousands of geese, barnacle and pink-footed, arriving from the Arctic, along with waders that feed on the mud at low tide. Across the firth, the town of Annan and the Scottish hills are visible on a clear day.

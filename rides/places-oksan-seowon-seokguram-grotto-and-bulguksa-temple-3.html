Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Oksan-seowon

    Oksan-seowon sits in a quiet valley northwest of Gyeongju, beside a clear stream that tumbles over smooth granite slabs. It was founded in 1572 to honour Yi Eon-jeok, one of the great Neo-Confucian scholars of the Joseon dynasty, who lived in retreat nearby, and it served as a private academy where young men studied the Confucian classics, prepared for the state examinations, and performed rites in honour of the master. The buildings are arranged according to the strict logic of Confucian architecture: a lecture hall, dormitories for students, a library and, at the back, a shrine for the rites. The architecture is restrained, with wooden halls, tiled roofs and open courtyards, designed to harmonise with the landscape. Oksan is part of the UNESCO listing of the Seowon, Korean Neo-Confucian Academies, inscribed in 2019.

  2. 2. Mt. Namsan Belt

    Namsan, the southern mountain of Gyeongju, is a modest granite ridge rising just under five hundred metres above the old capital of the Silla kingdom, and it is crammed with more Buddhist art than almost any mountain in Korea. For centuries the monks and craftsmen of Silla, which unified much of the Korean peninsula in the seventh century and ruled from Gyeongju until the tenth, carved Buddhas into its boulders, built stone pagodas on its ridges and placed temples in its valleys. UNESCO took in the whole mountain when it listed Gyeongju's historic areas, and walking its trails is like moving through an open-air museum among the pines.

  3. 3. Seokguram Grotto and Bulguksa Temple

    Bulguksa sits on the lower slopes of Tohamsan, a forested mountain above Gyeongju, and it is the most famous Buddhist temple in Korea. It was largely built in the eighth century under the Silla kingdom, at a time when Gyeongju was its capital and one of the largest cities in Asia, and tradition credits the work to the minister Kim Dae-seong. Stone staircases and terraces from that time still carry the wooden halls, which have been rebuilt after fires and wars, most destructively during the Japanese invasions of the 1590s.

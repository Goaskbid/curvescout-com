Why this ride

Roadbook

  1. Tver start

    Tver is the city that nearly became Moscow. In the fourteenth century its princes were Moscow's great rivals, and the two fought for the right to rule the Russian lands, until Moscow won and Tver was absorbed in 1485. Mikhail of Tver, who was killed by the Mongol Golden Horde, was later made a saint. What remains is a river city on the upper Volga, at the point where the Tvertsa joins it, roughly halfway between Moscow and St Petersburg, which made it a natural stop for anyone travelling between the two capitals.

    That role gave it a place in the history of the road. The route between Moscow and St Petersburg ran through Tver, and in the eighteenth century Catherine the Great ordered the city rebuilt after a great fire in 1763. The result is a neat classical centre of radial streets and pastel buildings, including the Imperial Travel Palace, built so the royal family had somewhere to stay on the way. The Tver merchant Afanasy Nikitin, who travelled to India in the fifteenth century and wrote of it, is remembered with a statue on the Volga embankment. The Volga here is still young, wide and calm, with willows on the banks.

    Riders heading from Solnechnogorsk up to Tver are following an old royal road, and the route is mostly fast, with a busy modern highway running alongside and smaller roads that wander through villages with wooden houses and onion-domed churches. Surfaces vary, and trucks are heavy on the main road, so take care in the rain. The best season is late spring to early autumn, with the forests at their best in September.

    Tver has some four hundred thousand people, so fuel, food and hotels are easy to find, and the villages around it, such as Sakharovo, have basics. Look for a cafe on the embankment, where you can watch the river traffic. Foreign riders should check current conditions and requirements before planning travel in Russia.

  2. Vasilyevo scenic

    Vasilyevo is a name that appears on the map of the Tver countryside many times over, and it is better treated as a way into the landscape than as a destination in itself. This one lies a few kilometres from Torzhok, on the old route between Tver and the north-west, in a land of low hills, wide fields and slow rivers, with the Tvertsa flowing through it on its way to the Volga. The villages are small, the churches are often half-ruined, and the sense of space is considerable.

    Torzhok is the real attraction. It is one of the oldest towns in the region, founded as a trading post on the border of Novgorod's territory, and it grew rich on the grain that was sent along the river to the north. Its churches and monasteries line the banks of the Tvertsa, and the Boris and Gleb Monastery has a collection of old towers and cathedrals. The town is famous for gold embroidery, a craft in which women stitched patterns of thread onto velvet and leather, and for a dish of minced chicken cutlets that was supposedly invented at an inn here and made famous by Pushkin, who passed through on his journeys between Moscow and St Petersburg. The writer's name is attached to several corners of the town.

    For riding, the approach is the old highway, the M10, which carries heavy traffic between Moscow and St Petersburg, and the better experience is on the smaller roads that run alongside it and through the villages. Surfaces vary a lot, from smooth new asphalt to rough, broken patches, and the lorries on the main road are a reminder to stay alert. In the evening, the light on the river and the fields is soft, and the birch woods glow in the late sun.

    Torzhok is six kilometres away and has everything a rider needs, with a population of nearly fifty thousand, plenty of fuel, a good choice of cafes and a historic centre worth a walk. Villages on this stretch have little in the way of services, so use the town for supplies. Late spring and early autumn are the pleasantest seasons, when the roads are drier and the mosquitoes are fewer, and in winter the road can be icy and the days are short.

  3. Torzhok finish

    Torzhok is an old trading town on the Tvertsa river, about sixty kilometres from Tver, and its name simply means the market. It was the grain gate of medieval Novgorod, a place where supplies from the Volga lands were assessed and taxed before moving north to the great city. That strategic role made it a target, and it was burned and sacked several times, once by the Mongol army in the thirteenth century. The town survived and grew, and in the eighteenth century it found a role on the road between Moscow and Saint Petersburg, hosting travellers who needed a change of horses.

    That road gave Torzhok some of its fame. Pushkin stayed here more than once, and the town is known for a dish of minced chicken cutlets named after the family of innkeepers called Pozharsky, who, according to the legend, served them to Tsar Nicholas I on a journey. Whether or not the story is true, the cutlets are on every local menu and are delicious, tender and crispy at once. The town is also famous for its gold embroidery, made with metallic thread on velvet or silk in elaborate designs, a craft that goes back centuries and was used for church vestments and slippers. The museum shows beautiful examples.

    Walking the old centre, you can see a number of historic churches and the walls of the Boris and Gleb Monastery, which has a cluster of white buildings and a tall bell tower overlooking the river. Torzhok has a gentle, lived-in air, with wooden houses, merchants' mansions and a riverside promenade where people stroll in the evening. The river is lined with willows, and in autumn the leaves float down the current.

    For a rider, the landscape here is a gentle one of low rolling hills, fields and woodland, with the Valdai Hills rising to the west. The old main highway passes close by, and traffic can be heavy and fast, with trucks, though quieter local roads lead into the countryside. Surfaces are variable, and potholes and frost heaves appear in places. The light in September, with gold fields and clear air, is a pleasure.

    Torzhok is large enough to have fuel, supermarkets and cafés, so it is a convenient place for a longer pause. The cutlets are the obvious choice for lunch, and some bakeries sell sweet pies. Mosquitoes in summer and snow in winter are the main inconveniences.

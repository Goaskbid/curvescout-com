Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Bristol

    Bristol sits at the head of the Severn estuary, which has the second-highest tidal range in the world, and its history is the history of ships. In 1497 John Cabot set sail from here in a small vessel called the Matthew and reached the coast of North America, and for centuries the merchants of the city grew rich on trade, much of it, as the city now admits, on the back of the slave trade. Isambard Kingdom Brunel left his mark: the Clifton Suspension Bridge, which crosses the Avon gorge in a single graceful span, opened in 1864 after his death, and the SS Great Britain, his great iron steamship, is now a museum in the dock where she was built.

  2. 2. City of Bath

    Bath is one of the few whole cities on the UNESCO list, and it earns it twice over. Underneath lie the Roman baths of Aquae Sulis, built around the only natural hot springs in Britain, where water still comes up at around forty-six degrees and the great pool, with its later colonnade above, steams in the cold. On top sits the Georgian city that eighteenth-century society built when Bath became the place to see and be seen, in honey-coloured local limestone: the Circus, the sweep of the Royal Crescent, the shops on Pulteney Bridge over the Avon and the long elegant terraces climbing the hills.

  5. 5. Blaenavon Industrial Landscape

    Blaenavon is a town on a hill that ought to be a mountain, with a shape and a story that suit the mountain. It stands high at the head of a valley in south Wales, at the edge of the Brecon Beacons, and in the late eighteenth century it became one of the great ironmaking centres of the world. The ironworks, built in 1789 and now preserved, shows how the furnaces, the casting house and the workers' cottages fitted together, and Stack Square, where the ironworkers lived, is still a place you can walk through. The whole landscape, with its quarries, tramroads, reservoirs and spoil heaps, was listed by UNESCO in 2000.

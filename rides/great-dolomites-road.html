Why this ride

A 76 km one-way ride from near Bolzano, about 1 h 14 of riding. On the way it passes Carezza, Passo Costalunga, Canazei and Passo Pordoi. The highest point is about 2,250 m. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Great Dolomites Road

For 23 km this is Great Dolomites Road, a road with a name of its own and a reason for it — expect company on a summer weekend. Early is better than fast.

Great Dolomites Road

The last of the trees go and the road runs out onto open ground. The summit sits at 2239 m, and what surrounds it is rock, weather and distance. Cold comes early up here and stays late. Ride it with something in reserve: the summit is not the end of the work, only the middle of it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Bolzano

    Bolzano lives with two languages and does so with a certain comfortable pride. It lies in a wide valley at the meeting of three rivers in South Tyrol, surrounded by steep slopes of vines and apples, with the pink limestone peaks of the Dolomites rising to the east. Until 1919 it belonged to Austria, and German is still the first language of most people in the province, though Bolzano itself is mostly Italian-speaking. Signs are in both, the cooking mixes dumplings and strudel with pasta and espresso, and the city feels like a conversation between two cultures.

  2. 2. Carezza

    Lago di Carezza has a reputation for being the most beautiful lake in the Dolomites, and in good light it earns it. It sits at about fifteen hundred metres in a forest of spruce below the jagged crest of the Latemar and the great towers of the Rosengarten, and its water is a deep, shifting emerald that changes from green to blue to turquoise depending on the sky and the angle of the sun. The German name is Karersee, and the Ladin name is Lec de Ciareja. Local people call it the rainbow lake, and there is a legend to match.

  3. 3. Passo Costalunga

    Passo Costalunga, known in German as the Karerpass, is the saddle at 1,752 metres that carries the road from the Bolzano side into the Val di Fassa. It is not a spectacular pass in itself, with its broad, wooded crossing and gentle slopes, but it sits at the foot of some of the most famous mountains in the Dolomites, and the view from the pass is one of the best things about it. To the south rise the Latemar, a ragged crest of pale rock, and to the north the Rosengarten, or Catinaccio, with its pink and orange towers.

  4. 4. Canazei

    Canazei is a small Ladin town at the head of the Val di Fassa, and the first impression is of towering rock. The Marmolada, the highest peak in the Dolomites at 3,343 metres, rises to the south-east with its glacier, while the cliffs of the Sella group fill the northern horizon, huge walls of pale dolomite that glow pink and orange at sunrise and sunset. The Dolomites were declared a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2009, and no one who stands here is likely to wonder why.

  5. 5. Passo Pordoi

    The Pordoi Pass is the highest on the Sella Ronda, at 2,239 metres, and the road from Arabba climbs it in thirty-three hairpins that are among the most photographed in the Alps. The road is wide, the surface is generally excellent and the bends are well graded, so it is one of the most enjoyable climbs in the Dolomites, a place where you can find a rhythm and let the bike dance. At the top you come out beneath the vertical cliffs of the Sella, with the Marmolada glacier shining to the south-east.

  6. 6. Arabba

    Arabba is a small village in the heart of the Dolomites, and a crossroads of passes. It sits at about 1,600 metres on the upper Cordevole river, at the foot of the Pordoi, the Campolongo and the Fedaia, and its position makes it the natural halfway point of the Sella Ronda, the circuit around the huge Sella massif. The mountains here are the pale, jagged towers that make the Dolomites famous, and the whole range was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009.

  7. 7. Passo Falzarego

    Falzarego is a pass that rewards the effort of the Dolomites, a bare grey saddle at 2,105 metres between Cortina d'Ampezzo and the high valleys of the Cordevole, ringed by pale, ruined-looking peaks that glow pink at sunset. The mountains here are sheer, jagged and strangely close, with the great cliffs of Lagazuoi and the Tofane on one side and the stacked towers of the Cinque Torri on the other. There is almost no vegetation at the top, only rock, scree and a few huts, and the sky feels very large.

  8. 8. Cortina d Ampezzo

    The peaks of the Tofane, Cristallo, Pomagagnon and Sorapiss surround it, and the whole area is part of the UNESCO Dolomites World Heritage Site. The town has about five thousand residents, and many more in the holidays, with elegant shops, bars and a long history as a mountain resort.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Asuka-dera Temple Site

    Asuka-dera is where Japanese Buddhism first took root in earnest. The temple was started in the late sixth century by Soga no Umako, the most powerful minister of his day, as the first full-scale Buddhist temple in the country, built with the help of craftsmen sent from the Korean kingdom of Baekje. Tile makers, bronze casters and architects crossed the sea, and the layout they used, with a pagoda surrounded by three golden halls, was a new thing on these islands, where until then the gods had been honoured in groves and simple shrines.

  2. 2. Kurizuka Kofun

    South of Nara city, the plain is flat and green, with rice paddies in every direction and low, rounded mountains standing on its rim, and the first Japanese states grew here. Kashihara, on the southern side of the basin, sits near the old capitals of Asuka and Fujiwara, where the court and the first lasting Buddhist temples took shape in the sixth and seventh centuries. Kurizuka is one of the many kofun, ancient burial mounds, scattered around the district, and it is a modest, tree-covered hump rather than a spectacular monument.

  3. 3. Nara Palace Site

    For most of the eighth century, Nara was the capital of Japan. The city, then called Heijō-kyō, was laid out on a grid modelled on the Chinese capital Chang'an, and at its northern end stood the imperial palace, Heijō-kyū, a vast compound of halls, offices and gardens. When the court moved away at the end of the century, the palace was abandoned and the land eventually returned to rice fields. Today the Nara Palace Site is a huge open space on the western side of the modern city, part of the UNESCO-listed Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara.

  4. 4. Byodo-in

    Take a ten-yen coin out of your pocket in Japan and you are holding a picture of this place. The Phoenix Hall of the Byōdō-in in Uji, with its wings stretching out on either side of a central hall and bronze phoenixes on the roof, is one of the most famous buildings in the country. It was built in 1053 by Fujiwara no Yorimichi, a member of the most powerful aristocratic family of the Heian period, who converted his father's villa into a Buddhist temple. Its reflection in the pond in front was meant to evoke the Pure Land paradise of the Amida Buddha.

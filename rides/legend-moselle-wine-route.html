Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Koblenz

    Two rivers meet at the tip of Koblenz, and a gigantic bronze-and-stone monument marks the spot. The German Corner, the Deutsches Eck, is a green point of land where the Moselle slides into the Rhine, with a copy of an equestrian statue of Emperor Wilhelm I standing on a high plinth at the end. The Romans called the place Confluentes, the confluence, and the name has worn down over two thousand years into Koblenz. Across the Rhine, the fortress of Ehrenbreitstein sits on a rocky ridge above the water, reached by cable car, and the view from the walls takes in both rivers and the whole old town.

  2. 2. Cochem

    Cochem has a castle that is younger than it looks. The Reichsburg, perched on a hill above the town, was built in the eleventh century, destroyed by French troops in 1689, and left a ruin for nearly two hundred years, until a Berlin businessman bought it and rebuilt it in neo-Gothic style in the 1870s. The result is a storybook castle, with turrets and pointed roofs, and a view over the Moselle that is among the finest in the valley.

  4. 4. Bernkastel-Kues

    Bernkastel-Kues is two towns glued together by a bridge across the Moselle. Bernkastel, on the right bank, is the one that appears on postcards, with a market square of leaning half-timbered houses, including one narrow gabled building from the early fifteenth century that looks like it was drawn by a child with a wicked sense of humour. Kues, on the other bank, is where the philosopher and cardinal Nikolaus von Kues, better known as Cusanus, was born in the early fifteenth century, and his old hospital foundation still stands, with a library of manuscripts that he collected.

  5. 5. Trier

    Trier calls itself Germany's oldest city, and nobody in a position to argue has managed to win. The Romans founded Augusta Treverorum around 16 BC, and by the fourth century it was one of the largest cities in the western empire, an imperial residence with palaces, baths and a great amphitheatre. The Porta Nigra, a massive black stone city gate from the second century, still stands on the northern edge of the old centre, grim and perfectly preserved because a hermit and later a church used it in the Middle Ages. A short walk away the Basilica of Constantine, a vast brick hall that was once a throne room, has a ceiling over thirty metres above the floor.

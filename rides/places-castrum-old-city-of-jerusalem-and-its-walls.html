Why this ride

A 367 km round trip from Umm ar Raşāş - roughly 6 h 20 in the saddle. The stops in between: Baptism Site “Bethany Beyond the Jordan”, As-Salt - The Place of Tolerance and Urban, Old City of Jerusalem and its Walls and back through As-Salt - The Place of Tolerance and Urban. It crosses from Jordan into Palestinian Territories - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Castrum

    Out on the plateau of central Jordan, east of the Dead Sea and south of Madaba, the land opens into a wide, treeless plain of wheat and barley, with low stone walls and the odd shepherd's tent. On that plain stand the ruins of Umm ar-Rasas, and the castrum is its oldest part, a rectangle of massive walls that began as a Roman military camp. It is probably of the late third or the fourth century, one of many forts that guarded the empire's edge against the desert tribes, and a garrison of auxiliary soldiers lived here. Its Roman name has been forgotten, and in the Byzantine age the town was called Kastron Mefaa. The walls still stand to a fair height, and inside is a jumble of collapsed houses, cisterns and arches, with a few churches among them.

  2. 2. Baptism Site “Bethany Beyond the Jordan”

    Down here you are well below sea level, close to four hundred metres under it, and the air shows it: thick, warm and heavy, with a mineral haze rising off the Dead Sea a few kilometres to the south. The baptism site known as Bethany Beyond the Jordan, Al-Maghtas in Arabic, lies on the east bank of the river Jordan, the place many Christians identify with the baptism of Jesus by John. Across a narrow, muddy channel of water stands Qasr al-Yahud on the western side, close to Jericho, and the two banks look at each other across one of the world's most heavily watched borders.

  3. 3. As-Salt - The Place of Tolerance and Urban

    As-Salt climbs three steep hills in the Balqa highlands north-west of Amman, a town of honey-coloured limestone houses with arched windows, terraced streets and long staircases. In the late Ottoman period, from the 1860s to the 1920s, it was the most important town in the region east of the Jordan, a trading hub where merchants from Nablus, Damascus and Beirut settled and built grand houses in a mix of local and European styles. Muslims and Christians lived side by side, sharing guesthouses and charitable traditions, and the town was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 2021 as As-Salt, the Place of Tolerance and Urban Hospitality.

  4. 4. Old City of Jerusalem and its Walls

    Few square kilometres on earth carry as much weight as these. The Old City of Jerusalem holds sites sacred to Judaism, Christianity and Islam, packed into a maze of stone lanes inside walls built by the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent in the 1530s. The Western Wall, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the Dome of the Rock and the al-Aqsa Mosque lie within a few minutes' walk of each other, and the city's four traditional quarters, Jewish, Christian, Muslim and Armenian, each have their own character, markets and rhythms. The Old City and its walls were added to the UNESCO list in 1981.

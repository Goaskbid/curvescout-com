Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Rice Terrace Clusters of Hungduan

    Rice terraces here follow the contours of the land like the lines of a map. Hungduan, in the province of Ifugao, high in the Cordillera Central of northern Luzon, is known for terraces built in a tight, spiralling pattern that some describe as a spider's web, climbing the steep sides of the Hapao river valley. They are part of the Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras, which UNESCO recognised in 1995, and they were shaped, over many centuries, by Ifugao farmers working without machines, using stone walls and bamboo channels to guide water from the forest above.

  2. 2. Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordillera

    People say the rice terraces of the Ifugao are two thousand years old, though archaeologists now think the oldest of them may be a few centuries old, and either way the thing remains astonishing. Up in the Cordillera mountains of northern Luzon, the Ifugao people shaped whole mountainsides into stairways of stone-walled paddies, fed by bamboo pipes and channels bringing water from the forest above. If the terraces were laid end to end, it is said, they would circle half the globe. They were built by hand, without machines, and they are still farmed by hand, with rice planted in the rains and harvested once a year. UNESCO listed them in 1995, and they remain one of the country's best-known cultural landscapes.

  3. 3. Rice Terrace Clusters of Mayoyao: Mayoyao

    High in the mountains of northern Luzon, the hillsides are carved into thousands of rice terraces, rising in curved steps from the river to the ridge, and the Mayoyao cluster is one of five inscribed by UNESCO in 1995 as the Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras. The cluster is in Ifugao province, about seven kilometres from the town of Lagawe, and the terraces here feel more intimate than those of the better-known Banaue, with the houses of the villages set among the paddies and the forest above.

  4. 4. Rice Terrace Clusters of Banaue: Bangaan

    The terraces of Banaue are carved into the Cordillera mountains of northern Luzon, and the cluster at Bangaan is one of the most photographed, a tight village of thatched huts at the bottom of a steep amphitheatre of stone-walled paddies. The Ifugao people built these terraces over centuries, shaping the mountainsides with walls of stone and earth, and channelling mountain springs through bamboo pipes and canals so that water flows from one paddy to the next. Popular accounts say they are two thousand years old, but archaeologists now think most of what we see grew over the past four or five centuries. Either way, it is a monument of patient labour with hand tools, and it is a living landscape, still planted and harvested by hand.

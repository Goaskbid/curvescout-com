Why this ride

curated scenic road. rolling wheat hills, buttes, rural towns. A one-way ride of 141 km, Spokane to Clarkston - roughly 2 h 34 in the saddle. On the way it passes Palouse Scenic Byway and Pullman. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Spokane

    Spokane is the biggest city between Seattle and Minneapolis, about two hundred thousand people in the dry country on the eastern side of Washington state, and it exists because of a waterfall. The Spokane River drops through a basalt gorge in the middle of downtown, a thundering set of falls that is the heart of Riverfront Park, a space created for the 1974 World's Fair. The Spokane tribe lived and fished here for thousands of years, and the name means children of the sun.

  2. 2. Palouse Scenic Byway point 2

    Steptoe Butte rises out of the Palouse like an island out of a green and gold sea, and that is exactly how the geologists describe it. The butte is a knob of quartzite, far older than anything around it, that poked up through the lava flows and wind-blown soil that buried the rest of the landscape. It stands about 1,100 metres above sea level, and from the top you look out over the rolling hills of the Palouse, a landscape of soft, curving ridges covered with wheat, peas and lentils. The word steptoe has entered the language of geology, meaning an island of older rock surrounded by younger lava.

  3. 3. Pullman

    Pullman is a college town surrounded by wheat, and it carries the confidence of both. Washington State University, founded in 1890, sits on the hill above the town, with its red-brick buildings, clock tower and sports stadium, and the whole place hums with students for most of the year and falls quiet in the summer. The town sits among the rolling Palouse hills, with fields of wheat, peas and lentils curving away on all sides, and the smell of cut grass, dust and diesel drifts in on the wind.

  4. 4. Clarkston

    Clarkston sits on the Washington bank of the Snake River, directly opposite Lewiston in Idaho, and the two towns, one on each side of a bridge, effectively form a single community in a river canyon of dry hills. The river here is wide and green, and the elevation is low, only about eight hundred feet, which makes it one of the warmest places in the Pacific Northwest. In summer the heat can be fierce, with temperatures well above thirty-five degrees Celsius, but the winters are mild.

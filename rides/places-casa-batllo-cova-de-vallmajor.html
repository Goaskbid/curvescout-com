Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Casa Batlló

    Passeig de Gràcia is the grand avenue of Barcelona, and on its sunny side stands a building that looks like it was made out of something living. Casa Batlló, remodelled by Antoni Gaudí between 1904 and 1906 for the textile industrialist Josep Batlló, is a facade of mottled glass and ceramic, with balconies shaped like masks or skulls and bone-like columns, and a roof ridge that arches like the back of a dragon, with a tower topped by a cross. Locals call it the House of Bones, and the roof is said to represent the dragon slain by Saint George, patron saint of Catalonia.

  2. 2. La Pedra de les Orenetes

    La Pedra de les Orenetes, the Swallows' Stone, is a granite boulder in the hills above La Roca del Vallès, and its surface is covered with small carved hollows and other marks left by people in prehistory. Nobody knows exactly what they meant; such engraved rocks are found across Catalonia, and theories range from ritual offerings to maps of the stars. The rock sits among cork oaks and pines in the Serralada Litoral, the coastal range that separates the Vallès plain from the sea, and the walk to reach it is short and pleasant, through scented forest with views over the plain.

  4. 4. Cova de Vallmajor

    Inland from the Costa Daurada, behind the beach towns of El Vendrell and Calafell, the land rises into the low hills of the Baix Penedès, a landscape of vineyards, almond and olive groves, pine woods and small villages. The Cova de Vallmajor, near Albinyana and Bonastre, is a rock shelter in these hills with prehistoric paintings, part of the Levantine rock art of the Mediterranean that is recognised as a World Heritage Site. The paintings are faint and the shelter is protected, so visits are usually by arrangement.

Roadbook

  1. Irkutsk start

    Irkutsk was a Siberian trading post that became a city of merchants, exiles and gold, and it has an air of faded elegance that surprises people who expect only snow and railways. It stands on the Angara river, the only outlet of Lake Baikal, about seventy kilometres downstream from the lake, and it was founded in 1661 as a fort. Fur traders, tea merchants and gold dealers made fortunes here, and the grand wooden houses with carved window frames, known as wooden lace, still line some of the old streets, leaning a little as the ground shifts.

    Irkutsk was also a place of exile, and the Decembrists, aristocratic officers who had rebelled against the tsar in 1825, were sent here, bringing with them books, music and manners. Their houses, now museums, are among the city's best-known sights, and the city was long known as the Paris of Siberia, an exaggeration but a charming one. The Trans-Siberian railway arrived in 1898, and the big station is still a landmark. Soviet housing, wide avenues and modern shopping centres fill the newer districts, with a long riverside promenade along the Angara.

    For riders, Irkutsk is the starting point for Baikal, the deepest and oldest lake in the world, holding about a fifth of the planet's unfrozen fresh water. The road to Listvyanka, a lakeside village, winds through hills and forest for around seventy kilometres, with a good surface and fair traffic, though weekends are busy. Farther south, the old Circum-Baikal route and the coast road toward Slyudyanka and Baikalsk pass between steep wooded slopes and the clear blue water, with mountains rising on the far shore. The water is cold enough to take your breath away, even in August.

    The weather is continental, with hot summers, cold winters of minus twenty or lower, and sudden storms. Wind on the lake can be strong, and fog and rain are common in late summer. The best season is June to September, with a short, golden autumn.

    Irkutsk has all the services of a big city, with fuel at many stations, repairs, supermarkets and hotels. Beyond the city, villages have small stations, but they are spaced apart, so fill the tank before leaving. Try omul, a smoked or salted fish from Baikal, sold at stalls in Listvyanka, and drink hot tea afterward. Mosquitoes can be fierce in the forests in early summer.

  2. Taltsy stop

    On the bank of the Angara, a little under halfway along the road from Irkutsk to Lake Baikal, stands a wooden tower that has travelled a long way. It was once part of the fort of Ilimsk, a seventeenth century Cossack outpost far to the north, and when the waters of a new reservoir threatened to drown the old fort in the Soviet era, the tower was dismantled, log by log, and rebuilt here. It is the centrepiece of Taltsy, an open-air museum of wooden architecture that brings together houses, barns, churches, mills and other buildings from villages across the Irkutsk region.

    Wandering through the museum is like stepping back into Siberian village life of the nineteenth century. There are peasant houses with carved window frames, a small wooden church, a school, a blacksmith's forge and water mills, along with Buryat and Evenk dwellings that show how the native peoples of the region lived. Craftspeople sometimes demonstrate traditional skills, and there are festivals in summer and at Maslenitsa, the end-of-winter carnival. The setting, among birch and pine on a slope above the wide, clear Angara, is lovely in any season.

    The road from Irkutsk to Listvyanka, sometimes called the Baikal Tract, is a pleasant ride, following the Angara through rolling forest with gentle bends and occasional views of the river. The surface is good, but the road is busy, especially on summer weekends when half of Irkutsk heads for the lake. Watch for speed traps and impatient drivers overtaking on blind bends. The best times are early morning or late afternoon, when the light on the river is soft and the traffic lighter.

    The museum has a cafe and souvenir stalls, and entry costs a modest fee. Fuel and other services are available in Irkutsk and Listvyanka, about twenty-five kilometres further on. If you have time, try some of the local food, such as pozy, the steamed meat dumplings of the Buryats, or smoked omul, the famous fish of Baikal, which is sold in markets and stalls along the road. Allow an hour or two for the museum, then continue on toward the lake.

  3. Listvyanka stop

    Listvyanka is the small lakeside village where the Angara River leaves Lake Baikal, and the first place most visitors see the great lake. Baikal is the deepest lake on Earth and holds roughly a fifth of the world's unfrozen fresh water, which is a fact people repeat constantly because it stays astonishing. The village lies about seventy kilometres from Irkutsk, along a road that follows the Angara, with Bolshaya Rechka a few kilometres up the shore.

    The road from Irkutsk is quick and mostly well surfaced, climbing out of the Angara valley through pine and larch forest, with open bends and short straights. It is also busy: on weekends and in summer, traffic can be heavy, with tour buses and local cars, and the final approach into the village is slow. The best times are early morning and weekdays, or the shoulder seasons, when the forest turns gold in September and the lake mists hang in the air. In winter the lake freezes into clear blue ice, but the road can be slippery.

    Listvyanka is a tourist village in the best and worst senses. It has a long lakeshore lined with cafes, souvenir stalls and guesthouses, and the main attraction is the market on the waterfront, where women sell smoked omul, the local whitefish, wrapped in paper and still warm. The Baikal Limnological Museum, which has an aquarium of local species, including the freshwater Baikal seal, the nerpa, is worth an hour. Nearby is the Shaman Stone at the source of the Angara, a rock that local legend says was thrown by Baikal in anger at his daughter's elopement with the river. The village's name comes from the larch, listvennitsa, which grows on the hills.

    Nature is the real reason to come. The lake water is cold, clear and deep, the forest smells of resin and wet stone, and the hills behind the village are lined with trails, including one up to a viewpoint known as Chersky Stone, named after a Polish geologist who studied the lake. Baikal's endemic species number in the thousands, and the whole lake is listed by UNESCO as a World Heritage site.

    Buy fuel in Irkutsk, since options in the village are limited and prices higher. Food is good and plentiful, and rooms fill early in summer.

  4. Bolshie Koty stop

    No road reaches Bolshie Koty, and that is the first thing to know. This small village on the western shore of Lake Baikal, about fifteen kilometres northeast of Listvyanka, can be reached only by boat or hydrofoil in summer, by walking the shoreline trail, or, in the depths of winter, across the ice. If the ride takes you here, it means parking the bike in Listvyanka and continuing on water or on foot, which is a fine way to see a stretch of shore that has kept its quiet precisely because cars cannot get there.

    The village was born in the nineteenth century as a gold-mining settlement, when prospectors worked the streams flowing down to the lake. The gold ran out, but the village stayed, and today it is home to a small number of residents, a few guesthouses and a biological station of Irkutsk State University, where scientists study the extraordinary life of Baikal. There is a small museum of the lake's endemic species, including the tiny crustaceans that keep the water so clear, and the nerpa, the freshwater seal that lives nowhere else on earth.

    The walk from Listvyanka along the Great Baikal Trail is a popular day hike, following the shore through forest and over rocky headlands, with stunning views of the lake and the mountains on the far side. It takes most of a day, with some steep, rough sections, so good shoes, water and food are essential. Boats run regularly in summer, taking under an hour, and are the easier option if time is short. The lake can be rough in wind, so check conditions before setting out.

    Services in Bolshie Koty are limited to a shop, a couple of cafes and guesthouses, and there is nowhere to buy fuel, nor any need for it. Listvyanka has all the services you need, and the nearest larger towns are back toward Irkutsk. Spend a night if you can: the evening light on the lake, with the mountains of the far shore turning pink and the village falling silent, is unforgettable. In winter, the frozen lake, clear as glass, is one of the strangest and most beautiful sights in Siberia.

  5. Baikal Shore finish

    Baikal holds about a fifth of all the unfrozen fresh water on the planet, in a rift so deep that its floor lies more than a kilometre below sea level. At this point near Listvyanka, where the Angara River leaves the lake as its only outflow, you can stand on the shore and look across a body of water so clear that stones are visible many metres down, and so cold that even in August a swim is a quick, gasping affair. In the middle of the river's source lies the Shaman Stone, a rock the Buryats held sacred and around which many legends are told.

    The lake is ancient, around twenty-five million years old, and it has evolved its own world of life: hundreds of species found nowhere else, from the nerpa seal to translucent fish and sponges. The Baikal Museum in Listvyanka explains it all, with aquariums where you can watch the seals play. The village itself is a long strip of houses, hotels and cafes along the shore, busy and touristy in summer, with markets selling smoked omul, the lake's famous fish, and stalls of cedar nuts, berries and souvenirs.

    As the finish of a ride along the Angara from Irkutsk, this is a fine end. The road follows the river through forest, then reaches the lake and runs along the shore into Listvyanka, with views across the water to the mountains of the Khamar-Daban range on the far side. The surface is good, though traffic is heavy in summer. A chairlift near the village climbs to a viewpoint named after the geologist Ivan Chersky, from which the view over the source of the Angara is magnificent, especially at sunset.

    Listvyanka has fuel, shops, cafes and restaurants, and plenty of accommodation. Try the smoked omul, hot from the smokehouse, eaten with bread and a cold drink on the shore. In winter, the lake freezes solid and the ice becomes a highway for cars, skaters and walkers, with cracks, bubbles and blue hummocks that are mesmerising. At any season, watching the light play across the water here is the best way to end a day of riding.

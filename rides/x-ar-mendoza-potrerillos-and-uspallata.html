  1. 1. Mendoza

    Mendoza is a city of trees. Walk or ride any of its wide avenues and you will pass under rows of old plane trees, fed by narrow irrigation channels called acequias, a system that the indigenous Huarpe people started long before the Spanish arrived. The reason is the desert: Mendoza gets very little rain, and every green leaf here exists because snowmelt from the Andes is carried down in channels and spread across the plain. It is a city of nearly nine hundred thousand people in the greater area, set against a wall of mountains that, on a clear winter morning, includes the white shoulder of Aconcagua far to the west.

  2. 2. Potrerillos

    Potrerillos is a small mountain resort on the Mendoza River, a short climb from the city, where the vineyards of the plain give way to the dry slopes of the Andean foothills. The village sits beside a large reservoir created by a dam, and the water, a startling blue against the pale rock, is used for sailing, kayaking and rafting. The air is clear and dry, the sun is strong, and the mountains seem close enough to touch, with the sharp, bare ridges of the Precordillera on one side and the snow-covered high Andes on the other.

  3. 3. Uspallata

    Uspallata is a high, dry valley with a lot of history. The village sits at nearly two thousand metres in the Andes of Mendoza, in a wide basin ringed by brown, bare mountains, and it is a halfway point on the old road between Argentina and Chile. In 1817, the Army of the Andes under General José de San Martín crossed the mountains in several columns, and one of them marched through the Uspallata valley on its way to liberate Chile, a story that every schoolchild here knows.

  1. 1. Icefields Parkway point 1

    Lake Louise is where the Icefields Parkway begins, and the lake has been drawing people for well over a century. Tom Wilson, a packer working for the Canadian Pacific Railway, was led to its shore in 1882 by a Stoney Nakoda guide, and the lake was soon renamed after Princess Louise Caroline Alberta, a daughter of Queen Victoria. The railway built a chalet there, which grew into the grand hotel at the water's edge, and the picture of that milky turquoise lake under the Victoria Glacier became one of the defining images of the Canadian Rockies.

  2. 2. Icefields Parkway point 2

    Bow Lake is the source of the Bow River, the same water that eventually flows through Calgary, and it sits in a high, open bowl at the foot of the Bow Glacier. The lake is long and green-blue, rimmed by scree slopes and cliffs, and the peaks of the Waputik Icefield rise above it. On a calm morning, the surface reflects the mountains with such clarity that the picture looks the same upside down.

  3. 3. Icefields Parkway point 3

    Peyto Lake is the one every photographer wants, and a short climb is all it takes. From the car park at Bow Summit, the highest point on the parkway at just over two thousand metres, a paved path leads uphill for about ten minutes to a viewing platform, and then the whole lake appears below you, a vivid turquoise shape in the forest. It looks like the head of a wolf, with a long muzzle pointing down the valley, and the colour is so intense that visitors sometimes assume the pictures have been altered.

  4. 4. Icefields Parkway point 4

    Mistaya Canyon is the kind of place you only find if someone tells you, and then you wonder how you almost drove past. A short, easy trail drops from a small car park through the forest for about ten minutes, and suddenly the river is below you, squeezed into a slot of smooth, grey limestone, spinning through potholes and polished chutes. The name is said to mean grizzly bear, and the water does have a muscular power as it pours through the narrow gorge.

  5. 5. Icefields Parkway point 5

    The Columbia Icefield is the heart of the Rockies, a huge sheet of ice that covers more than two hundred square kilometres and feeds at least half a dozen major glaciers. It is the largest icefield in the Rockies, and the Athabasca Glacier, one of its tongues, flows down almost to the roadside. You can see the ice from the car park, a grey-white wall tumbling down the mountain, its surface cracked and streaked with dirt. The glacier is retreating fast, having lost more than a kilometre since the nineteenth century, and signs by the road mark where its toe used to be.

  6. 6. Icefields Parkway point 6

    Sunwapta Falls is where a big, glacier-fed river throws itself down a rocky drop and then vanishes into a gorge. The name comes from a Stoney word that is usually translated as turbulent river, which is an accurate description, because the water is milky grey with sediment and moves with real force. The upper falls plunge about eighteen metres over a ledge, and a short path leads to a viewing platform. A longer trail leads downstream to the lower falls, passing through cool, mossy forest.

  7. 7. Icefields Parkway point 7

    Athabasca Falls is not tall, only about twenty-three metres, but it makes up for its height with sheer volume. The Athabasca River, swollen with meltwater from the Columbia Icefield, funnels into a narrow gap in the rock and thunders over the lip into a deep, roiling pool. The whole canyon trembles with the force of it, and the spray hangs in the air, drifting over the viewing platforms and wetting your jacket even from a distance.

  8. 8. Jasper

    Jasper is a mountain town of about four thousand people in the heart of the Canadian Rockies, and it feels like it was built on a wild animal path. Elk wander across lawns and down the main street, deer graze in gardens and signs warn of bears. The Athabasca River runs past the town below ridges of grey limestone, and the Jasper Park Lodge, a grand old resort on Beauvert Lake, sits among spruce just beyond. In July 2024 a major wildfire swept into town and destroyed a large number of buildings, and the community has been rebuilding since, so it is worth checking current conditions and services before planning a stay.

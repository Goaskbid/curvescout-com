Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Villa Mairea

    Villa Mairea stands in a pine wood at Noormarkku, in western Finland, and it is among the masterpieces of Alvar Aalto. He designed it in 1938 and 1939 for Harry and Maire Gullichsen, a wealthy industrial family who owned the Ahlstrom company, a large Finnish timber and engineering business, and who wanted a house that would be a laboratory for modern living. Aalto gave them a house that is white, curved and warm, with materials, wood, stone, brick and cane, mixed in a free and surprising manner. The living room opens through large glass doors to a garden, a sauna and a swimming pool shaped like a kidney, and the interior flows from room to room.

  2. 2. Bronze Age Burial Site of Sammallahdenmäki

    In the countryside of Satakunta, west Finland, a low forested ridge hides thirty-odd heaps of stone laid down more than three thousand years ago. Sammallahdenmäki is a Bronze Age burial site, with granite cairns up to several metres across, built by people who lived along what was then the coast. When these cairns were raised, the sea lapped nearby. Since then the land has been rising, still lifting after the weight of the ice sheet, and the shore has retreated kilometres, leaving the graves stranded on the hill among spruce and pine. UNESCO added the site to its list in 1999.

  3. 3. Paimio Sanatorium

    In the early 1930s, Finland needed a hospital for tuberculosis, and the architect Alvar Aalto won the competition to design it. The Paimio Sanatorium, finished in 1933, is one of the landmarks of modern architecture, a white, functionalist building set among pines on a ridge, in which every detail was designed to help the patient recover. Rooms faced south to catch the sun, windows were large, floors were coloured to calm the eye, and basins were shaped to make noise as little as possible so that the person in the next bed would not be disturbed. Aalto thought of the building as a medical instrument.

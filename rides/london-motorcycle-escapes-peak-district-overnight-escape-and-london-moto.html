Why this ride

A one-way ride of 847 km, Dunstable to Waltham Cross - roughly 15 h 24 in the saddle. The stops in between: Edgware, in Leysdown-on-Sea, Scenic stretch near Maidstone and Peacehaven. The climb of the day is near Thame. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Fuel stop near Dunstable

    Dunstable sits where Watling Street, the old Roman road from London to the north-west, cuts through the chalk of the Chiltern Hills, and the town has been a stopping place for travellers for nearly two thousand years. Henry the First founded a priory here in the twelfth century, and its great church still stands, though much reduced. Today the town is busy and practical, with plenty of service stations and supermarkets.

  2. 2. Near Edgware

    Most people know Edgware as the end of a Tube line, but riders know it for something older. The A5 that runs through it is Watling Street, the Roman road that ran from the Kent coast to the far side of Britain, and the long dead-straight run from here towards St Albans has the unmistakable stamp of legionary surveyors. The finish lies a little south, around Kingsbury and Queensbury, where north-west London becomes an endless spread of semis, shopping parades and busy junctions.

  3. 3. Photo stop in Leysdown-on-Sea

    Leysdown-on-Sea, at the eastern end of the Isle of Sheppey, has the faded cheerfulness of a traditional English seaside resort: caravan parks, amusement arcades, chip shops and a long shingle beach looking out over the Thames estuary towards the open sea. It is not glamorous, but it has a certain charm, and the sense of being at the edge of things is strong.

  4. 4. Scenic stretch near Maidstone

    Maidstone is the county town of Kent, built on the banks of the River Medway, which carried ragstone, timber and hops to London for centuries. The town has a busy modern centre, but its riverside has old buildings, including the medieval Archbishop's Palace, a stopping place for archbishops of Canterbury travelling between London and their cathedral. It is a practical place, with good roads, shops and services, and a gateway to some of the loveliest countryside in the south-east.

  5. 5. Peacehaven

    Peacehaven was a field of cabbages until the First World War. Then an entrepreneur named Charles Neville bought the clifftop, laid out a grid of roads and started selling plots, at first under the name New Anzac-on-Sea, after the Australian and New Zealand troops camped nearby. The name changed after Gallipoli, and the result is one of England's odder towns: a sprawling bungalow suburb perched on top of chalk cliffs between Brighton and Newhaven.

  6. 6. Food stop in Overton

    The paper for Bank of England banknotes was made near Overton for centuries. The Portal family founded a mill on the River Test in the early eighteenth century, and their watermarked paper became the currency of the empire; the business, later part of De La Rue, still operates here. It is a curious claim to fame for a quiet Hampshire village of brick and flint houses, but it says much about the clear, fast chalk streams of this part of England.

  13. 13. Near Croydon

    Croydon is where the South Downs ride comes home to London, and it is honest about it: a big, busy town of glass office towers, flyovers, trams and shopping centres, sitting at the southern edge of the capital where the chalk hills of the North Downs fade into suburbs. It has a population larger than many British cities and its own strong identity, and for a rider it is the last stretch before the metropolis takes over.

  15. 15. Near Berkhamsted, on the way back

    Berkhamsted lies in a narrow chalk valley cut through the Chilterns, with the Grand Union Canal, the River Bulbourne, the railway and the old main road all squeezed into the bottom together, and beech woods rising on both sides. It is a prosperous market town of around twenty thousand people, with a long high street of old coaching inns and shops, and it makes a fine last stop before the run back into the outskirts of London.

  16. 16. Back to South Downs

    Waltham Cross takes its name from one of the most moving things a medieval king ever built. When Queen Eleanor of Castile died in 1290, her husband Edward I had her body carried from Lincolnshire to London, and at each place the funeral procession stopped overnight he put up a stone cross. Only three of the twelve survive, and the one at Waltham Cross, with its carved figures of the queen and its pinnacles, is the most complete. It stands in the middle of the high street, among bus stops and cafés, and most people walk past it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Strausberg fuel gate

    Strausberg is the last proper town before Berlin turns into Brandenburg, and it knows how to be useful. It sits on the shore of a long lake, the Straussee, about thirty kilometres east of the capital, with the S-Bahn running straight in, and about twenty-six thousand people live here. The medieval centre kept a stretch of its town wall and the brick gothic Marienkirche, and the little ferry across the lake, the Strausberg ferry, is one of the nicest short crossings in the region. The town also runs one of the smallest tram networks in Germany, a single line that has served for a century.

  2. 2. Buckow lake bend core

    The road bends around the Schermützelsee here, and the bends are the point. Buckow lies in a hollow of the Märkische Schweiz, where glaciers once carved a long channel and filled it with water, and the road follows the lake shore in a series of turns that make a welcome change from the straight lines of the Brandenburg plain. Beech woods climb the slopes, and the water flashes between the trunks.

  3. 3. Schermützelsee water stop

    The water here is the point. Schermützelsee is the largest lake in the Märkische Schweiz, a long, clear glacial channel that runs alongside Buckow, and in summer the shore is dotted with small beaches, jetties and boat hire stations. The water is clean and refreshingly cool, edged with reeds and beech woods, and on a hot day it is hard to resist.

  4. 4. Neuhardenberg palace roads

    Neuhardenberg is a village on the edge of the Oderbruch, and its palace has an unusual story of renaming. The estate was called Quilitz until 1814, when the Prussian king gave it to his state chancellor, Karl August von Hardenberg, as a reward for his service in the reform era and the wars against Napoleon, and Hardenberg renamed the place after himself. The great architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel redesigned the palace and built the church in the village, a pale, clean-lined building that is among his best small works. In East Germany the village was renamed again, as Marxwalde, and it kept that name until 1990.

  6. 6. Königs Wusterhausen fuel

    In December 1920 a group of postal engineers at Königs Wusterhausen broadcast a concert of carols and instrumental music from a hill outside town, and the sound went out across Germany. It is remembered as the country's first radio broadcast, and the hill, the Funkerberg, still carries a historic radio station and a museum of early broadcasting. Whatever the exact date, this was where German radio began, and the masts on the hill have been a landmark ever since.

  7. 7. Schlepzig forest water

    Schlepzig sits where the Spreewald stops being a tourist postcard and goes back to being forest. This is the Lower Spreewald, north of Lübben, where the Spree splits into slow channels beneath alder woods so dense that the light turns green, and the ground is more water than land. The trees grow in the wet, their roots arching above the surface, and in autumn the leaves float on the channels in drifts of orange.

  8. 8. Lübben old town

    Lübben is the stylish older sister of the Spreewald towns, a place of about fifteen thousand people on the river, with a small castle, a lively harbour and enough old streets to make a lunch stop feel like an event. The Spree comes in from the south in several arms, and the town grew up on the island between them, with water on every side.

  9. 9. Burg Spreewald water stop

    Burg is where the Spreewald water becomes a thing you can touch. The village has canals on every side, and a number of small landing stages where people launch punts and canoes, and in summer the water is warm, shallow and the colour of tea from the alder roots. A swim here is a traditional pleasure, and several spots along the channels have been set aside with steps and platforms.

  10. 10. Beeskow river roads

    Beeskow is a small town on the Spree with a castle that has found a second life. Burg Beeskow, a medieval fortress of brick and fieldstone beside the river, was once a seat of local lords and later a prison and a granary. Today, it houses an art archive that holds thousands of paintings and sculptures commissioned by the East German state, a large and strange collection that gives a picture of the art of the old regime, and it also hosts concerts and exhibitions. The town around it, with about eight and a half thousand people, still has parts of its medieval wall, a Gothic church of St Marien and a market square with old houses.

  11. 11. Back to Königs

    Königs Wusterhausen has two claims on history, one to do with a soldier king and the other with a radio mast. In the early eighteenth century, Frederick William I of Prussia, the Soldier King, used the hunting lodge here as his favourite retreat, and it was the setting for the Tobacco Collegium, an all-male evening society where he smoked pipes with his generals and played rough jokes. The squat little palace still stands beside the Dahme river and is open as a museum. His son, who grew up to be Frederick the Great, hated the place.

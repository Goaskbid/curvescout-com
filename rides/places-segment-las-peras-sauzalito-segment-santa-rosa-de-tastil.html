Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Segment Las Peras-Sauzalito

    This stretch lies in the high valleys of Salta province, in the dry north-west of Argentina, and it belongs to the great Inca road system the Incas called Qhapaq Ñan. UNESCO listed the network in 2014, a web of tracks and stone-built stairways, bridges and storehouses reaching from Ecuador to central Chile, built to link a mountain empire with no wheeled carts. Messengers on foot, the chasqui, ran in relays along these routes, and llama caravans carried loads on the long climbs. Some stretches are still walkable, and sections survive near modern roads.

  2. 2. Subsection Abra de ChaupiyacoLas Capillas

    The Incas built a road across the Andes, and in the north of Argentina it still runs under the hooves of mules and the boots of walkers. The Qhapaq Nan, the Great Inca Road, was a network of some 30,000 kilometres linking the empire from Colombia to Chile and Argentina, and parts of it were inscribed on the World Heritage list in 2014 across six countries. The segment of Chaupiyaco to Las Capillas, in the province of Salta, runs through the high valley of the Quebrada del Toro, one of the old routes between the Altiplano and the lowlands, and the section near Santa Rosa de Tastil follows the course used long before the Inca, by herders with llamas.

  3. 3. Segment Santa Rosa de Tastil

    The Quebrada del Toro is a long, narrow valley cut through the Andes of Salta, in north-west Argentina, and the old road that follows it was a trade route for centuries. Santa Rosa de Tastil sits on a terrace above the river, at around 3,200 metres, and it was a town: a settlement of stone houses, perhaps several hundred of them, built in the fourteenth century and inhabited until shortly before the Spanish conquest. People here farmed, kept llamas and traded across the mountains, and when the Incas arrived they connected the place to their road system, the Qhapaq Nan, which was inscribed by UNESCO in 2014.

Why this ride

From Barinas it runs 257 km to Mérida; allow about 3 h 30 of riding. The stops in between: Santo Domingo and Pico El Aguila Pass. The highest point is about 4,100 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Roadbook

  1. Barinas start

    Barinas lies in the llanos, the great tropical grasslands of western Venezuela, at the foot of the Andes. It is a hot, flat, spreading city of three hundred thousand or more, a place of cattle ranches, rice and tobacco fields, and wide avenues lined with trees. The air is humid and heavy, and the sky is huge. This is cowboy country, and the music on the radio is joropo, played on harp, cuatro and maracas. It is also the state capital and the home region of Hugo Chávez, who was born in the village of Sabaneta nearby.

    For the rider, Barinas is the lowlands anchor of one of the most dramatic crossings in South America. From here, the Trans-Andean Highway rises west toward Mérida, climbing out of the plain along the river valleys, first through the foothill town of Barinitas, then into cloud forest and finally above the tree line. The highest point, near Pico El Águila, tops four thousand metres, and in that single day you go from tropical heat to a cold, windswept páramo of frailejón plants, the silvery, furry-leaved shrubs that grow only in high Andean moorland. The first part of the climb is wide and steady, with long curves and lorry traffic, but the upper section can be narrow, steep, and wet, with thick fog and sudden cold.

    You should start early, because afternoon rain in the mountains is common, and the descent into Mérida is better in daylight. Carry warm and waterproof clothes, as the temperature drop is large. Check the current road conditions before you set out. Landslides and damaged pavement are possible after heavy rains, and services are thin on the high stretch.

    Fuel has been subject to shortages and queues in recent years, so plan your tank carefully, fill up whenever you can, and ask locally about availability. Barinas is large enough to have many stations, shops and restaurants, and it is the best place to prepare.

    Eat something substantial before you go. The local beef is excellent, and in the llanos, they grill it over open fires. Then turn your front wheel to the mountains.

  2. Santo Domingo scenic

    Santo Domingo is a mountain village in the Venezuelan state of Mérida, strung along the river of the same name as it tumbles down from the high Andes toward the plains. The altitude here is already considerable, and the air smells of wet earth, wood smoke, and pine. For a rider coming up from the lowlands, this is where the road changes character, from tropical heat to cool cloud forest, with mist rolling over the ridges and the temperature falling with every hairpin.

    The Trans-Andean Highway, known locally as the Trasandina, was built to link the plains of Barinas with the high cities of Mérida, and it crosses the Andes over one of the highest roads in the country. Santo Domingo marks the point at which the climb begins in earnest. The valley narrows, the slopes steepen, and the road follows the river through terraced fields, small farms, and cloud forest thick with ferns and orchids. Trout from the cold streams are a local speciality, and a plate of fish with potatoes is a good reward after a long ascent.

    The riding is demanding but wonderful. The road climbs through a long series of bends, some tight and some flowing, with steep drops on the outside and often poor visibility in mist. The surface can be uneven, with potholes, rockslides, and patches of wet leaves, and heavy trucks use the road. Daytime riding is strongly advisable, and fog can arrive in minutes. Higher up, temperatures drop sharply, and a waterproof layer and warm gloves are essential even in a country that is mostly tropical.

    Practical matters need honesty. Fuel supply in Venezuela has been unreliable in recent years, and the safest approach is to carry a reserve and fill up whenever possible. The nearest large town on the map is Barinitas, about thirty-three kilometres back down the valley, and it is best to check local conditions and security before travelling. Bring cash, spare parts, and patience. The best season is the dry months from December to March, when the skies are clearer, while the rainy season can bring landslides and low cloud. The welcome from the people of the mountains, though, tends to be warm.

  3. Pico El Aguila Pass scenic · 4118 m

    At 4,118 metres, Pico El Águila is the highest point on any road in Venezuela, and arriving there feels like reaching the roof of a different world. The vegetation has changed completely: below the pass lie cloud forest and farmland, but up here is páramo, a high, treeless Andean landscape of tussock grass, lakes, and the strange silver-grey frailejones, tall plants with furry leaves that look like they belong on another planet. A stone monument with a condor stands near the top, marking the spot.

    The air is thin and cold, and a rider who has come up from the plains can feel it in the head and in the engine. A motorcycle loses power at this altitude, and the rider may feel breathless or lightheaded. It is sensible to stop and rest, drink water, and not hurry. The wind can be fierce, and clouds can sweep over the pass in moments, so visibility can fall to a few metres. In clear weather, though, the view over the mountains and valleys is vast, and the sky is a deep, clear blue.

    The road across the pass is the Trans-Andean Highway, climbing from the Barinas side through a series of hairpins and then dropping toward the Mérida side, through the high valley of Apartaderos and the villages beyond. It is well graded but narrow in places, with steep drops and few barriers. Frost is possible at night even in the dry season, and rain, hail, and sudden fog are common. Take it slowly and keep your speed down on the descent, because brakes overheat and corners come at you quickly.

    Small cafes and stalls in the villages on either side sell hot drinks and snacks, which are welcome after the cold of the summit. The nearest big towns are some distance away, and it is wise to check road and security conditions locally before travelling. Dry-season mornings, between December and March, offer the best chance of a clear view, and the sight of condors, if you are lucky, is something to remember.

  4. Merida finish

    Mérida spreads along a high Andean shelf at about sixteen hundred metres, with the Sierra Nevada rising behind it to the highest peaks in Venezuela. Pico Bolívar, at nearly five thousand metres, carries a small glacier that is shrinking year by year, and on a clear morning you can see it from the city streets. The place has the unhurried, cool-aired feel of a university town, helped along by the Universidad de los Andes, one of the oldest in the country, whose students fill the cafés and plazas.

    The city was founded in 1558 by Spaniards who came up from the lowlands, and the old centre keeps its colonial grid, with a cathedral, churches, and shaded plazas where families sit in the evening. The cuisine of the Andes is hearty: pisca andina, a milk and egg soup, pan de jamón, trout from mountain streams, and strong coffee from the hills. One ice cream parlour in the city has made a name for the number of its flavours, running to hundreds and including ones like trout and garlic, and it has made the world record books more than once. Whether or not you love the idea, it is an amusing stop.

    As the end of the Trans-Andean ride, Mérida is a good place to land, with proper hotels, restaurants, mechanics and shops. The final descent into town from the pass area is long and winding, through cloud forest and farm terraces, with tight bends, lorries, and fog that can drop visibility to a few metres. After days of cold air at altitude, the warm valley feels like a reward. The city also has a famous cable car system, the Teleférico de Mérida, once the highest and longest in the world, which climbed toward Pico Espejo above four thousand metres.

    The surrounding mountains are excellent for further riding, with small roads climbing to villages like Jají and Los Nevados, and the high páramo. Weather is the main variable, with a dry season roughly from December to March, and a wetter, cloudier period in the middle of the year.

    Fuel supplies in Venezuela can be unpredictable, so ask locally and fill up when you see a pump. The city is large enough to have several stations, but queues are possible.

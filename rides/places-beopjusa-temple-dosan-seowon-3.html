Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Beopjusa Temple

    Look up from the car park at Beopjusa and you see an enormous bronze Buddha, thirty-three metres tall, standing in the open air against a backdrop of granite peaks. He is a modern figure, finished at the turn of the millennium, but the temple he guards is very old: tradition says the monk Uisin founded it in 553, bringing Indian scriptures back to Korea in a donkey's pack. Its main attraction, Palsangjeon, is a five-storey wooden pagoda with eight panels depicting scenes from the Buddha's life, rebuilt in the 1960s on an older design, and one of the few such wooden pagodas in Korea.

  2. 2. Historic Villages of Korea: Hahoe and Yang

    The Nakdong river makes a wide loop around Hahoe, almost turning it into an island, and inside that loop a village of tiled-roof mansions and thatched cottages has stood for some six centuries. Hahoe is the ancestral home of the Pungsan Ryu clan, a lineage of scholar-officials during the Joseon dynasty, and its layout follows Korean principles of geomancy, with houses arranged to face the river and the cliffs of Buyongdae across the water. It was added to the World Heritage list in 2010, together with Yangdong village near Gyeongju, as the finest surviving examples of clan villages of the Joseon period.

  3. 3. Bongjeongsa Temple

    Tucked into the wooded slopes of Cheondeungsan, a modest mountain north-west of Andong in North Gyeongsang province, Bongjeongsa is a temple that wears its age quietly. Its main claim to fame is a small, plain hall called Geungnakjeon, believed to be the oldest surviving wooden building in Korea, dating back to the Goryeo dynasty. Repairs in the twentieth century turned up records suggesting the hall had been rebuilt in the thirteenth century, which makes it older than almost anything else made of timber in the country. Bongjeongsa is one of seven mountain monasteries inscribed by UNESCO in 2018 as Sansa, Buddhist Mountain Monasteries in Korea.

  4. 4. Dosan-seowon

    If you have handled a Korean thousand-won note, you have already seen Dosan Seowon. The building on the back of the note is this Confucian academy, and the scholar on the front is Yi Hwang, known by his pen name Toegye, one of the most influential Neo-Confucian thinkers of Korea. He set up a small study hall here in the sixteenth century, on a quiet slope above the Nakdong river, and after his death in 1570 his disciples expanded it into a full academy, a seowon, where students came to study the classics and honour his memory. It is one of the nine Korean Confucian academies listed by UNESCO in 2019.

Out of Gryllefjord, one way: 95 km, roughly 1 h 37 in the saddle. It goes by way of Bergsbotn, Tungeneset and Husoy. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Gryllefjord

    Gryllefjord is a fishing village at the north-western end of Senja, Norway's second largest island, and it is about as far as you can go before the road gives out and the Atlantic takes over. It has fewer than five hundred inhabitants, a harbour, a small ferry quay and a handful of buildings, and the ferry runs from here across to Andenes on Andøya, a crossing of a couple of hours that is worth taking in summer. The mountains behind the village are jagged and steep, and the sea is a deep, cold blue. Finnsnes, on the mainland side of the island, is the nearest town of any size, nearly forty kilometres away.

  2. 2. Bergsbotn

    Bergsbotn lies at the head of Bergsfjorden, on the north-west coast of Senja, Norway's second-largest island, and it is the place where the island's reputation as Norway in miniature starts to make sense. Steep peaks drop straight into the sea, the fjord runs cold and dark between them, and the lights of a few small houses stand at its edge. A viewing platform reaches out over the water, built as part of the scenic route programme, and from it you can look across the fjord to a wall of jagged mountains whose profile reminds some people of a saw and others of the horns of an ox. Local names say the same thing in Norwegian.

  3. 3. Tungeneset

    Tungeneset is a viewing platform on the west side of Senja, set on a rocky point above the sea, and it offers what is perhaps the most photographed outlook on the island. A path of steel and timber boardwalks leads out over the rock to a viewing point, designed to be unobtrusive but strong enough to stand the weather. In front of you, the mountains rise in sharp, bare peaks straight from the water, and depending on your angle and the cloud, you can pick out the pointed silhouettes that appear in every Senja guidebook. Below, the sea works at the foot of the rock, and in the right light it goes from slate to silver to green.

  4. 4. Husoy

    Husøy is a fishing village on a small island off the coast of Senja, tied to the larger island by road, and the lanes of its wooden houses are painted in the traditional reds, yellows and whites of the north. It is a working harbour first of all: boats at the quay, nets and crates on the wharf, and the air heavy with the sharp smell of salt, diesel and fish. Racks of drying cod stand in places, the old method of preserving the catch that built the economy of the Norwegian north. Stockfish, as it is called, was shipped to southern Europe for centuries, and the trade shaped places like this.

  5. 5. Botnhamn

    Botnhamn is a tiny fishing settlement on the north side of Senja, facing the open sea, and it is an end of the road in the best sense. The little harbour is sheltered by rocky headlands, with a handful of houses and boat sheds, and behind it rise steep, jagged mountains that drop straight into the ocean. Finnsnes, over thirty kilometres away, is the closest town of any size, so supplies need to be sorted earlier. The area has been settled for thousands of years, with traces of Sami and Norse inhabitants, and fishing has been the main livelihood for most of that time.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Yusuf Hamdani

    The Yusuf Hamdani complex lies in the Merv oasis, just under seven kilometres from Bayramaly, among low walled courtyards and a handful of new-looking domes. Unlike most of Merv, which is ruin, this is a living shrine: pilgrims arrive from across Turkmenistan and beyond, women in colourful headscarves, men in lambskin hats, many tying cloth strips to trees and leaving small offerings.

  2. 2. Kelte Minara koshk

    Once there were few cities in the world as big as Merv. At its height, in the twelfth century, under the Seljuk sultans, the oasis city on the Murghab river, in the sands of what is now Turkmenistan, may have been the largest on the planet, with a population counted in the hundreds of thousands, libraries that Arab geographers praised and a position on the Silk Road between Persia and Central Asia. In 1221, the Mongols arrived, and the chroniclers say that the slaughter was beyond counting. The city never recovered its size, but the ruins, which cover a vast area of low walls, mounds and shattered brick, were listed as a World Heritage site in 1999.

  3. 3. Yaz and Gobekli Tepes

    Out on the edge of the Murghab oasis in south-eastern Turkmenistan, where irrigated cotton fields meet the sand of the Karakum, low mounds rise from the flat land. Tepe means hill, and these hills are the eroded remains of ancient settlements: Yaz Depe, which gave its name to an Iron Age culture of the early first millennium BC, and Gobekli Depe nearby. To an untrained eye they are just humps of clay, but they belong to one of the longest continuous stories of settlement in Central Asia.

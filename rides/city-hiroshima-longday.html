Why this ride

From Hiroshima it runs 254 km to Hiroshima; allow about 4 h 23 of riding. It goes by way of Yataki-jô Site, Okidomari, Yahazu-jô Site and Iwami Ginzan Kaidô Yunotsu-Okidomaridô.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hiroshima

    Hiroshima is a city that has made peace its work. On 6 August 1945 the first atomic bomb used in war was dropped here, and most of the city was destroyed. The skeletal dome of the former Industrial Promotion Hall, now the Genbaku Dome, stands by the river as it did then, preserved in ruin, and the Peace Memorial Park beside it holds a museum, a cenotaph and the Flame of Peace. It is a serious place and deserves unhurried time, and visitors are quiet there.

  2. 2. Yataki-jô Site

    The name Yataki-jo sounds like a puzzle until you realise it just means Yataki castle, and the castle is a ridge in the forested hills behind the old silver town of Omori, in Shimane on the Sea of Japan side. There is no keep, no white walls. What survives is earthworks, terraces cut into the hillside, and the kind of lookout position that makes sense once you stand there and see how many routes it watches. The reason anyone fought over this ground is under it. Iwami Ginzan was among the largest silver mines in the world in the sixteenth century, and the lords of the region, the Ouchi, the Amago and the Mori among them, spent decades taking it from one another.

  3. 3. Okidomari

    Okidomari is a small fishing harbour on the Shimane coast, wedged into a cove with steep green hills at its back, and today it looks like nothing much at all. Boats are pulled up, nets are drying, an old man is doing something patient to an engine. In the sixteenth century the same cove was one of the ports through which Iwami silver left for the world. Ore from the mines at Omori was carried down to the coast on foot along a mountain track, the Iwami Ginzan Kaido, and shipped from places like this to the markets of Japan and abroad. Silver from this stretch of coast helped shape trade across East Asia, and it is a startling thought when you are looking at a tiny slipway.

  4. 4. Yahazu-jô Site

    Yahazu-jo is another mountain castle site in the hills behind Omori, among the ridges that guarded the Iwami silver mines, and it adds up to the same story as its neighbour Yataki-jo, but with its own angle. Where one site is about watching a valley, this one is about the chain: a series of small fortresses linked by ridgeline paths, so that smoke or a signal fire could pass a warning along the hills. Silver paid for armies in the sixteenth century, and whoever held the mountains held the mines. The castles were mostly built of earth and timber, and what remains are ditches, flat platforms and the shape of old gateways, now overgrown with cedar, oak and bamboo grass.

  5. 5. Iwami Ginzan Kaidô Yunotsu-Okidomaridô

    The old road from the Iwami Ginzan mine to the sea at Yunotsu and Okidomari was the route silver took to the world. For centuries, ore and then refined silver were carried on the backs of porters and pack horses along a mountain track that dropped from the mine to the Sea of Japan, a distance of several hours on foot. At the coast, ships collected the cargo and carried it to the markets and ports of the country. Yunotsu was a harbour town with a deep inlet; Okidomari, the name of which means something like moorage offshore, was where the boats waited in the bay.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Kottenforst Süd | Professorenweg 1

    The southern end of the Kottenforst lies where the plateau west of Bonn begins to tilt toward the Eifel, and the ground, which is flat and wooded to the north, starts to roll in low hills around Wachtberg and Meckenheim. Anyone who has walked the Kottenforst on a Sunday knows the Professorenweg, a long forest track whose name is thought to come from the university professors of Bonn who took their daily constitutional along it, thinking aloud with a colleague.

  2. 2. Remagen

    On 7 March 1945, soldiers of the US 9th Armored Division reached Remagen and found the Ludendorff railway bridge across the Rhine still standing, despite German attempts to blow it. They rushed across, and for the first time the Allies had a foothold on the far bank of the great river. Ten days later the damaged bridge collapsed, but by then pontoons had been built and the war in the west had entered its last chapter. What remains today are the black stone towers on both banks, and those on the Remagen side house a small peace museum telling the story with honesty and without glorification.

  3. 3. Watchtowers WP 1/11 to 1/20 including the

    Here is where the Upper German-Raetian Limes, the longest stretch of Roman frontier in Europe, begins. The Romans started the line at the Rhine near Rheinbrohl and ran it up over the hills of the Westerwald, then on through the Taunus and beyond for more than five hundred kilometres to the Danube. The section with watchtowers numbered WP 1/11 to 1/20 follows the first climb away from the river through the forests above Rheinbrohl, Hammerstein and Niederbreitbach. Most of the towers survive only as low mounds of stone in the woods, but the course of the frontier ditch and rampart can often be traced on foot, and there are information boards and a few reconstructions along the way.

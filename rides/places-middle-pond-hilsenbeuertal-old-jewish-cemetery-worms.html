Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Middle pond Hilsenbeuertal

    The middle pond in the Hilsenbeuertal is where the valley shows its character most clearly. It sits between the upper and lower waters in a chain, each held by its own dam, and the Cistercian monks arranged them so that the stream passed from one to the next, feeding fish at each stage and powering a mill at the bottom. It is cool engineering for the twelfth century, and it has lasted. Part of what UNESCO lists at Maulbronn is this whole hydraulic landscape, the dams, ditches and ponds spread through the forest around the monastery.

  2. 2. Baden-Baden

    Baden-Baden is so nice they named it twice, as the old joke goes, though the doubling actually comes from distinguishing it from other places called Baden. Romans soaked in its hot springs, and the remains of their baths lie under the old town, but the town's golden age came in the nineteenth century, when European royalty, aristocrats, writers and gamblers flocked here to take the waters, stroll the avenues and lose fortunes at the casino. Dostoevsky, Turgenev and Brahms all spent time here. Baden-Baden is one of the eleven Great Spa Towns of Europe, listed together by UNESCO in 2021.

  3. 3. Old Jewish Cemetery Worms

    The Heiliger Sand in Worms is the oldest surviving Jewish cemetery in Europe, with gravestones going back to the eleventh century. Weathered sandstone slabs lean at angles among the grass and trees, carved with Hebrew inscriptions, some barely legible after nearly a thousand years. Pebbles and small notes lie on many of them, left by visitors as a sign of remembrance, especially on the grave of Rabbi Meir of Rothenburg, a great scholar of the thirteenth century who died in captivity. Together with the synagogue and ritual bath of Worms and the Jewish sites of Speyer and Mainz, the cemetery forms the ShUM Sites, listed by UNESCO in 2021, recognising the Rhineland communities that shaped Ashkenazi Jewish culture in the Middle Ages.

  1. 1. Puno

    Puno stands on the shore of Lake Titicaca at about 3,800 metres, and it is the capital of the Peruvian side of the lake and of Andean folk culture. It is a city of about 116,000 people, a bustling, cold, windswept place with a cathedral in baroque style, a busy port and streets that climb steeply from the water. It began in the seventeenth century as a silver-mining town, and the colonial wealth is visible in the stonework of the churches. Today it is best known for its festivals, notably the Candelaria in February, a spectacular fiesta of dance and music that draws thousands of dancers in elaborate costumes.

  2. 2. Chucuito

    Chucuito is a quiet lakeside town about fifteen kilometres south of Puno, on the shore of Lake Titicaca, and it was once the capital of the Lupaca, a powerful Aymara kingdom that ruled this part of the Altiplano before the Incas. The town has a colonial centre with a neat plaza, stone houses and two fine churches, and the low hills behind it are patched with fields of potatoes and quinoa. The lake is only a short walk away, a vast sheet of dark blue water stretching toward distant mountains.

  3. 3. Juli

    Juli is a small town of about seven thousand people on the southwest shore of Lake Titicaca, and it carries the nickname Rome of the Andes because of its four colonial churches, some of the grandest in the Altiplano. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the Jesuits made Juli a centre of missionary work among the Aymara, and they set up a school and a press that is said to have produced some of the first books printed in South America, including works in Aymara and Quechua. The churches, with their stone facades, bell towers and paintings, are still standing, and they give the town a surprisingly serious, historic feel.

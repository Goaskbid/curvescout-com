  1. 1. Regensburg - Legionslager IV

    A legionary in Castra Regina had a life of drill, labour and waiting. He served for twenty-five years, was paid in coin, was fed on bread, porridge and a little meat and wine, and he could not marry legally while in service. The soldiers of the Third Italic Legion spent much of their time building: roads, bridges, brick kilns and the camp itself. Their stamps appear on tiles and bricks all around the area, which is how historians know where they went. When there was no war, there was always another ditch to dig.

  2. 2. Weltenburg‐Am Galget - Kleinkastell

    The Danube does something unexpected just upstream of Kelheim. Instead of wandering across flat Bavarian farmland, it cuts straight through a band of white Jurassic limestone, squeezed between cliffs that rise steeply from the water. This is the Weltenburg Narrows, and on the hill above the abbey at Weltenburg, at the spot called Am Galget, the Romans put a small fort to watch the river and the frontier.

  3. 3. Watchtowers WP 15/11 to 15/26 and fortlets

    The Altmühl valley in Bavaria is one of Germany's best kept riding secrets, a winding river valley cut deep into Jurassic limestone, lined with juniper heaths, rock towers and castles. Near where the valley meets the Danube, the Roman frontier, the Raetian Limes, ran across the land, and the stretch of watchtowers and small forts numbered WP 15/11 to 15/26 lies in the wooded hills here, between Riedenburg, Dietfurt and the Danube town of Vohburg.

  4. 4. Fort site and civil settlement of Pfünz

    Above the village of Pfünz, on a spur of white Jura limestone looking down into the Altmühl valley, the Romans built a fort they called Vetoniana. Today a reconstructed gate and a stretch of wall stand on the hilltop, and from up there you understand the choice at once: the valley floor curls below you, the river meanders slowly, and anyone moving along it would have been seen hours before they arrived.

Why this ride

418 km from Åndalsnes to Åndalsnes, about 8 h 20 of riding. The stops in between: Valldal strawberry stop, Eagle Road and Geiranger fjord. The climb of the day is Trollstigen. It tops out at about 1,150 m. Trollstigen is subject to seasonal closure - check it is open before you set off. The passes are the reason to go.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Trollstigen hairpins

    Trollstigen, the Troll's Ladder, climbs out of the Isterdalen valley in eleven hairpin bends, with a gradient of about one in ten, and the road is as steep and tight as it looks in photographs. It was opened in 1936, after years of work, by King Haakon VII, and it still has the narrow width and stone-walled edges of its era, with passing places on the bends. The Stigfossen waterfall plunges beside the road, and a bridge crosses it at one of the hairpins, so that you ride through the spray.

  2. 2. Valldal strawberry stop

    Valldal is a village at the head of the Norddalsfjord, and each July it becomes the strawberry capital of Norway. The south-facing slopes above the water, sheltered by mountains, catch long summer light, and the berries that ripen on them are small, deep red and very sweet, with a flavour more intense than the larger kinds. Growers sell them from roadside stands, in cardboard boxes or as soft ice cream, jam and cake, and the season is short, starting in early July and ending in a few weeks. A bowl of fresh strawberries with cream, eaten on a bench with the fjord below, is a very good reason to stop.

  3. 3. Eagle Road viewpoint

    Ornesvingen, the Eagle Bend, is a hairpin on the road above the Geirangerfjord, and the viewpoint beside it gives the classic picture of the fjord: a long, narrow slot of green water, hemmed by cliffs and waterfalls, with the village of Geiranger at the far end and the white wakes of ships crossing. The road that leads to it, the Eagle Road, climbs from the fjord in eleven hairpins, and takes its name from the birds that sometimes soar here. The name suits the bend, which seems to hang in the air.

  4. 4. Geiranger fjord

    Geirangerfjord is one of those places where the photographs do not exaggerate. A narrow arm of the Storfjord, about fifteen kilometres long, it is squeezed between mountain walls that rise to well over a thousand metres, with waterfalls pouring down the cliffs. UNESCO listed it in 2005 along with the Nærøyfjord, and the tour ships that fill it in summer are a sign of how famous it has become.

  5. 5. Back to Trollstigen

    The Trollstigen is best understood from below. Coming up from Åndalsnes, the road leaves the broad floor of the Isterdalen and rises through birch woods into a narrow valley, where a wall of rock closes in, and then begins to climb in earnest. Eleven hairpins, each with a name or a number painted on the stone, carry it up the slope, and the Stigfossen waterfall, which falls over three hundred metres, crashes beside the road and under a stone bridge, spraying anyone who stops. It is one of the great Alpine-style roads of Scandinavia, and it was a wild feat of engineering for its time.

  6. 6. Åndalsnes

    Åndalsnes is a small town at the head of the Romsdalsfjord, hemmed in by some of the most vertical mountains in Norway. The Romsdalshorn, a sharp pyramid of rock, rises above the valley to the south, and the Trollveggen, Europe's tallest vertical rock face at around a thousand metres, stands a short way up the valley. The town itself is modest, with a few thousand people, a railway terminus, a harbour and a handful of cafes, and its main business is welcoming people who come for the mountains.

  7. 7. Trollstigen viewpoint

    The Trollstigen Plateau is a bare, glacier-scoured saddle at 858 metres, ringed by peaks with names like Bispen, the Bishop, and Kongen, the King, and the viewing platform here, a sculptural structure of steel and concrete that hangs over the drop, is the best place to understand what the road does. Below, the Trollstigen, the Troll's Ladder, zigzags down the side of the valley in eleven hairpins, with the long white thread of the Stigfossen waterfall falling beside it, and beyond lies the broad green Isterdalen and the far peaks of Romsdalen.

  8. 8. Valldal

    Valldal is a small town at the head of the Norddalsfjord, in the green and orderly farmland that lies at the foot of the great Sunnmøre mountains, and it is the first proper settlement you reach after the long descent from the Trollstigen. Valldal is famous in Norway for its strawberries, which ripen in July in the long summer light and are sold in roadside stands, and the local people take a great deal of pride in them.

  9. 9. Geiranger

    The Geirangerfjord is a narrow cut, around fifteen kilometres long, between cliffs that rise over a thousand metres from the water, and it is among the best-known sights in Norway. The village at its head, Geiranger, is tiny, with only a few hundred residents, but in summer, cruise ships anchor in the fjord one after another and the pier fills with people. UNESCO listed the fjord in 2005, along with Naeroyfjord, and a short walk along the shore brings you to the base of the Seven Sisters waterfall, which pours straight into the fjord from a high ledge.

  10. 10. Ørnesvingen Eagle Road

    There is a bend in the road above the Geirangerfjord where the whole country seems to drop away in front of you. Ørnesvingen, the Eagle Bend, is a hairpin on the road from Eidsdal, and the viewpoint at its edge, at about six hundred and twenty metres, looks straight down onto the fjord, a long, narrow slot of green water between cliffs with waterfalls pouring from the rim. Ships look like toys at the village of Geiranger below, and the air smells of cold stone and wet moss.

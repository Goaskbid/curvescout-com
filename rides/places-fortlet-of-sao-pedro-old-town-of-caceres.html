  1. 1. Fortlet of São Pedro

    The Alentejo plain rolls away from Elvas in every direction, a country of cork oak, olive and wheat, and the little fort of São Pedro stands on a low hill outside the town as one of a ring of outworks around it. Elvas is a town ringed by star-shaped bastions built in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when Portugal and Spain fought repeatedly over this frontier, and just across the border lies Badajoz. The fortified town and its outer forts, including the fortlets of São Pedro, São Mamede and São Domingos, were listed by UNESCO in 2012 as the largest dry-ditch fortification system in the world.

  2. 2. Alcantarilla Bridge

    Mérida was founded by the Romans in 25 BC as Augusta Emerita, a city for veterans of the Cantabrian wars, and it grew into the capital of the province of Lusitania. Few places in Spain carry their Roman past so openly. The small Alcantarilla bridge on the edge of town is one piece of the Archaeological Ensemble of Mérida, listed by UNESCO in 1993, and its name, from the Arabic for little bridge, tells you how many hands this landscape has passed through. It is a quiet, unassuming structure, and that is a good reason to start here before you tackle the big sights.

  3. 3. Old Town of Cáceres

    Cáceres has an old town so complete that film crews treat it as a ready-made set, and walking its streets at dusk, when the stone glows amber and the crowds have gone, you can see why. Inside the walls, built largely by the Almohads in the twelfth century, are dozens of fortified mansions, towers, churches and palaces, many built by noble families who made their fortunes in the conquest of the Americas. The towers once had battlements, but Queen Isabella ordered most of them cut down to curb the nobles' power. UNESCO listed the old town in 1986.

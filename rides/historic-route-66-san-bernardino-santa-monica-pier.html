Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. San Bernardino

    Hamburgers changed the world here, or at least the way Americans eat. The first McDonald's restaurant opened in San Bernardino in 1940, a drive-in run by two brothers, and the idea of fast, standardised, cheap food that began on this corner went on to spread around the globe. The original site is gone, but a small museum on Route 66 tells the story, and it is a curious pilgrimage for any rider following the old highway. The city itself is large, a place of more than two hundred thousand people stretched across a plain at the foot of the San Bernardino Mountains.

  2. 2. Los Angeles

    Route 66 does not end in a blaze of glory. After more than three thousand kilometres from Chicago, the old highway frays into the boulevards of Los Angeles, and its last stretch is a patchwork of Foothill Boulevard, Colorado Boulevard and Santa Monica Boulevard, ending officially at the pier in Santa Monica, with the Pacific beyond. The point on the map here is the old centre, where the city began in 1781 as a Spanish pueblo, and a few blocks of adobe buildings and a Mexican market street, Olvera Street, are still standing, near the grand Spanish-style Union Station, which opened in 1939.

  3. 3. Santa Monica Pier

    The longest road in American mythology runs out of land here. Route 66 was commissioned in 1926 to link Chicago with Los Angeles, and as it went through eight states on its two-thousand-mile journey it picked up the nickname the Mother Road. The official end of the highway is a matter of fine print, with the route having been rerouted several times, but the pier at Santa Monica carries a sign calling it the End of the Trail, and that is where riders photograph their bikes, so that is where this ride finishes.

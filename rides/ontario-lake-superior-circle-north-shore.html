Why this ride

From Sault Ste. Marie it runs 1075 km to Thunder Bay; allow about 14 h 43 of riding. It goes by way of Wawa and Neys Provincial Park. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Sault Ste Marie

    Sault Ste. Marie sits on the St. Marys River, the short, fast channel that links Lake Superior to Lake Huron, and the city exists because the water here drops about seven metres over a set of rapids. The Ojibwe called the place Bawating, the place of the rapids, and it was a fishing and gathering ground long before French fur traders arrived in the seventeenth century. The name Sault, pronounced Soo, is the old French word for rapids. A twin city of the same name faces it across the river in Michigan, joined by the International Bridge, and the two are bound together by locks, trade and history.

  2. 2. Wawa

    Wawa is famous for a goose. A huge steel statue of a Canada goose stands at the junction on the highway, put up in the 1960s, and the name itself comes from the Ojibwe word for the wild goose, which migrates over the lake in flocks each spring and autumn. The town, home to around twenty-six hundred people, grew up as a gold rush camp in the nineteenth century and later lived off the iron ore mines to the north, which have since closed.

  3. 3. Neys Provincial Park

    Ney's Provincial Park holds one of the quieter chapters of Canadian history. During the Second World War, a camp here held German prisoners of war, and visitors can still learn something about it at the site. Today the park is a pleasant place, with a long sand beach on Lake Superior, dunes, a rock headland with a lookout, and a campground in the trees, all within a few kilometres of the town of Marathon.

  4. 4. Thunder Bay

    Thunder Bay is the end of the line for the Ontario shore of Lake Superior, a port city of just under a hundred thousand people tucked against a huge bay with the long, flat shape of Sleeping Giant rising across the water. That peninsula, a cluster of cliffs and ridges, gives the city's skyline a nearly mythic profile, and the Ojibwe story says it is Nanabijou, the spirit of the deep sea, turned to stone. The name of the city comes from the bay, and the weather justifies it: storms off the lake can be sudden and loud.

Why this ride

Five passes over 4,000 m. Open roughly June to September. 232 km from Manāli to Leh, about 4 h 13 of riding. The highest point is about 5,350 m. Baralacha La, Lachulung La and Tanglang La are open in summer only - check it is open before you set off. The line favours views over speed.

Scenic road chapters

Leh–Manali Highway

Expect company on a summer weekend. Early is better than fast. For 18 km this is Leh–Manali Highway, a road with a name of its own and a reason for it.

Rider note

Check the pass is open before you commit to it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Near Manāli

    Apple orchards, deodar cedars and the roar of the Beas river surround Manāli, the hill town at the head of the Kullu Valley in Himachal Pradesh, and this spot lies just outside it, at the point where the road to Ladakh starts its long climb into the high Himalaya. Manāli sits at about two thousand metres, and from here the snow peaks of the Pir Panjal range rise straight up behind the town, with the Rohtang Pass hidden above.

  2. 2. Leh–Manali Highway point 2

    Sissu is the first village on the far side of the Atal Tunnel, and the landscape changes at once. In Manali the hills are green and wooded, but here in the upper Chandra valley of Lahaul, the monsoon barely reaches, and the mountains are bare, brown and grey, with glaciers hanging on the heights. The village sits at about three thousand metres beside the river, with a waterfall that tumbles down the cliff, and poplar and willow trees along the stream. Manali is sixteen kilometres or so behind, and Kyelang, the district town, lies further north.

  3. 3. Leh–Manali Highway point 3

    Baralacha La is a pass at about 4,850 metres, a bare, wind-blasted saddle where the road crosses between Himachal Pradesh and Ladakh, and the landscape feels like the edge of the world. Near the top lies Suraj Tal, a high, round lake that reflects the sky, and the mountains around are snow-capped even in summer. Three rivers, the Chandra, the Bhaga and the Yunam, rise in this area and flow in different directions. Kyelang lies a long way back, some forty kilometres by the crow, and there is nothing else near.

  4. 4. Leh–Manali Highway point 4

    Sarchu is a place with no village, only tents. At about 4,300 metres, on the high plateau between the Himachal and Ladakh borders, a scatter of seasonal camps sits beside a stream, serving as an overnight halt for riders and drivers on the long road to Leh. The nearest settlements are far away, and the landscape is a vast bowl of brown hills, with snow peaks on the horizon and a sky that seems enormous. The mountains are bare, and the air is thin and cold.

  5. 5. Leh–Manali Highway point 5

    Pang is a cluster of tents at about 4,500 metres, perched at the foot of a series of hairpins on the Leh-Manali Highway, and it is the last stop before the road climbs onto the high plateau. The settlement consists of a few dhabas and tent camps serving the passing traffic in summer, and it has a rough, frontier feel. A short way beyond lies the Moray Plains, a vast flat expanse at about 4,700 metres, where the road runs straight for kilometres across a gravel desert surrounded by distant mountains.

  6. 6. Leh–Manali Highway point 6

    Somewhere past the loops that drop off Tanglang La, the mountains step back and the road runs out onto a wide, pale plateau. The air is thin enough that a bike loses a visible slice of its power, and a rider who climbed out of Leh a few hours ago can feel a dull pressure behind the eyes if the day started too fast. Go slowly and drink more water than seems sensible.

  7. 7. Leh

    Leh sits in the high desert of Ladakh at about 3,500 metres, in the broad valley of the Indus, with the Karakoram on one side and the Zanskar range on the other, and it is the end of the Manali-Leh highway, one of the great road journeys in the world. The last stage of that road is the best: you cross the Tanglang La, among the highest passes in the world, then drop to the Indus plain, through villages with whitewashed chortens and prayer flags, and the town appears below a hill crowned by the old Leh Palace, a nine-storey building of mud and wood modelled on the Potala in Lhasa. Behind it, the Namgyal Tsemo monastery and a ruined fort hang on the ridge.

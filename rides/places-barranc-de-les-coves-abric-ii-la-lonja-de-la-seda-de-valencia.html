Why this ride

A 387 km round trip from Ibi - roughly 6 h 40 in the saddle. On the way it passes Barranc de les Covatelles, Pinós, Abric de Lambert and La Lonja de la Seda de Valencia. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Barranc de les Coves- Abric II

    Ibi is the first thing to understand here, because the town sits so close that the rock shelter feels like its back garden. Ibi is a place that makes toys, and has done for generations; the whole Hoya de Castalla and the neighbouring town of Onil grew a tinplate-and-plastic industry that once filled children's bedrooms across Spain. Before the factories, Ibi was known for something colder: snow and ice gathered from the high ground of the Sierra de Mariola and stored in pits for the summer. A town that lives on cold in a region that bakes is a good story to carry up the hill.

  2. 2. Barranc de les Covatelles

    Guadalest is a tiny village on a rock, entered through a tunnel cut in the stone, with a castle, a bell tower perched on a pinnacle and a view over a turquoise reservoir below. It is one of the most visited places in the province of Alicante, and it sits at the heart of a valley surrounded by mountains: the Aitana, the highest in the province at over fifteen hundred metres, the Serrella and the Xortà. The Barranc de les Covatelles is one of the ravines in this mountainous country, near Benimantell, Sella and Castell de Castells, with rock shelters that hold prehistoric paintings, one component of the World Heritage inscription for the rock art of the Mediterranean Basin.

  3. 3. Pinós

    Inland from the busy coast of the Marina Alta, behind Benissa and Calp, the land folds into a series of steep, terraced valleys where almond and olive trees cling to dry-stone walls and white farmhouses sit among the pines. Pinós is a rural area in the hills near Benissa, Senija and Jalón, and the rides link it to the rock-art shelters of the barrancs, the ravines that cut through the limestone of this region, part of the UNESCO listing of Mediterranean prehistoric art. The exact contents of the nearby shelters are for specialists, but the landscape itself has a quiet, timeless beauty, especially in the soft light of late afternoon.

  4. 4. Abric de Lambert

    Cullera is not where most people would look for prehistory. It is a busy beach town at the mouth of the Júcar, with high-rise apartments along the sand, a lighthouse on the point and rice fields stretching inland toward the Albufera lagoon. But the town has a mountain at its back, a limestone outcrop crowned by a castle and a sanctuary, and in that rock there are shelters, including the one known as the Abric de Lambert.

  5. 5. La Lonja de la Seda de Valencia

    In the heart of Valencia's old town, a Gothic building with crenellated walls and gargoyles looks more like a castle than a market, and that was rather the point. The Lonja de la Seda, the silk exchange, was built in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries when Valencia was one of the richest cities in the Mediterranean, and it was meant to show the power of its merchants. Its great hall, the Sala de Contratación, is one of the masterpieces of late Gothic architecture, with slender columns that twist like palm trunks up to a ribbed vault. It was listed by UNESCO in 1996.

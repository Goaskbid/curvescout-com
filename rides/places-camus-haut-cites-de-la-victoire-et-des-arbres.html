Why this ride

68 km round trip from Camus Haut, about 1 h 34 of riding. It runs out through Monument aux morts et grilles de la cité d, Cités de la Victoire et des Arbres, Cités du Nouveau Monde et des Fleurs and Château Mercier et maisons d’ingénieur and comes back by another road.

The stops

  1. 1. Camus Haut

    Start here.

    Camus Haut lies on the eastern edge of the mining basin, near Annay, Pont-à-Vendin and Harnes, where the canalised Deûle carries barges north towards Lille. The label refers to one of the many mining sites and cités of the area, and while the exact spot is hard to identify from a distance, the landscape tells you what kind of place it is: brick housing estates, old pit heads, spoil heaps going green, and the wide, flat horizon of the Gohelle.

    Read the complete Camus Haut write-up.

  2. 2. Monument aux morts et grilles de la cité d

    17 km in.

    In the mining towns of northern France, the companies built not only pits and houses but whole communities, with churches, schools, dispensaries and, after the First World War, monuments to the dead. This war memorial and the iron gates of a mining cité in Mazingarbe are small parts of that story, included in the Nord-Pas de Calais Mining Basin World Heritage site. They stand among rows of brick houses, gardens and allotments in a town shaped by coal.

    Read the complete Monument aux morts et grilles de la cité d write-up.

  3. 3. Cités de la Victoire et des Arbres

    35 km in.

    Houdain is a mining town in the Pas-de-Calais, a couple of kilometres from Bruay and a short hop from Béthune, and its workers' estates carry names that read like a municipal promise: Victory and Trees. They belong to the western end of the northern French coalfield, which stretches in a long band across the Nord and Pas-de-Calais and which was the engine of the region for more than two centuries. Here the company built housing on a scale that still surprises. Whole districts of identical brick houses, each with its own garden plot, were laid out in rows and crescents, so that miners and their families would live close to the pit and under the eye of their employer.

    Read the complete Cités de la Victoire et des Arbres write-up.

  4. 4. Cités du Nouveau Monde et des Fleurs

    37 km in.

    The coal companies were not short of optimism when they named their housing. In Bruay-la-Buissière, two neighbouring cités were called the New World and the Flowers, and their brick houses with front gardens, laid out along tidy streets, were meant to sell a better life to the families who came from across France, Poland, Italy and North Africa to work the pits. They belong to the Nord-Pas de Calais Mining Basin World Heritage Site, and they remain ordinary homes, so look respectfully.

    Read the complete Cités du Nouveau Monde et des Fleurs write-up.

  5. 5. Château Mercier et maisons d’ingénieur

    52 km in.

    Mining companies built for rank as well as for need. The ordinary miners got brick cottages in terraces, but the engineers and directors got something grander, detached houses with larger gardens and, for the top man, a house that was called a château and looked like one. Château Mercier at Mazingarbe belongs to that tradition, and the ride titles pair it with the engineers' houses. A walk through the old cité shows you the whole hierarchy of a company town in a few hundred metres, from the pit and the cottages to the engineers' row and the director's lawn. UNESCO's 2012 listing of the mining basin covers this social geography as much as the machinery.

    Read the complete Château Mercier et maisons d’ingénieur write-up.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Tossa de Mar

    Tossa is the town that made me understand why people fall for the Costa Brava. A ring of medieval walls and round towers, the Vila Vella, sits on a rocky headland above a curve of beach, and the old town behind it still has the tangled lanes of a fishing village. The walls date to the Middle Ages and were built to keep out pirates, which tells you something about the sea they were guarding against. A lighthouse crowns the point, and the whole thing glows orange in the evening light.

  2. 2. Sant Feliu de Guíxols

    There is a gate in the middle of Sant Feliu de Guíxols that is about a thousand years old. The Porta Ferrada belongs to a Benedictine monastery whose origins go back to the early Middle Ages, and the Romanesque and later buildings grew around it. Today the monastery complex holds the town's museum and an auditorium that hosts a summer festival, so the old stones are still in use. It is a pleasing fact that a place of about twenty-two thousand people has such a calm, historic centre.

  3. 3. Palamós

    Palamós is a working port that happens to sit on a beautiful bay. Unlike some of its neighbours on the Costa Brava, it has kept a fishing fleet and a daily rhythm that has little to do with tourists. In the afternoon the boats come in, and the fish auction in the harbour sells the catch. The town is especially famous for its red prawns, the gamba de Palamós, which Catalan cooks regard as among the finest in the Mediterranean, and you will see them on menus all along this coast, usually with a price that will make you think twice.

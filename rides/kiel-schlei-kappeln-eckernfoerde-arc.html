Why this ride

Fjord air, small bridges and north-coast rhythm. The best part sits around Eckernförde bay, Schwansen backroads, Kappeln Schlei bridge, while Schleswig food stop keeps the day practical instead of turning it into a fuel or food hunt. Check the OSM map, Google preview and GPX checkpoint order before departure; they use the same waypoint sequence. Ride it with legal pull-offs, patience through villages and enough daylight to stop without rushing.

Roadbook

  1. Kiel west exit start · 5 m

    Kiel is a city with a fjord at its heart, and the west side is where you most feel the practical, working character of the place. The Kiel Fjord cuts deep inland, giving the city a sheltered harbour, and the Kiel Canal begins at its western edge, so the neighbourhoods here are tied up with ships, locks and shipyards. This was the home of the German Imperial Navy in the nineteenth century, and the sailors' revolt in 1918 that helped end the First World War started here. The city was bombed almost flat in the Second World War, which is why so much of the centre is plain postwar architecture rather than old brick.

    Today Kiel is a university city of about two hundred and thirty thousand people with a lively waterfront, and every June the sailing event called Kieler Woche fills the harbour with boats and the streets with music and food stalls. If you leave to the west you pass through Kronshagen and Mönkeberg, suburbs that are mostly residential, and the first part of the ride is urban, with traffic lights, cyclists and commuter flows. Allow a bit of time for the first few kilometres and do not expect much until you clear the ring road.

    Once outside the city the landscape opens into farmland and woods, with roads that pick up gentle curves and small climbs, and the Baltic sea air makes the whole place feel fresh. The surface is generally fine. The wind can come in sharply off the water, so layers are useful. A short walk along the fjord before you set off is a pleasure on a clear morning, with ferries and sailboats crossing the water and the smell of salt and diesel rising off the quay. The Kiel Canal crossings, by bridge or small ferry, add a bit of variety to the ride.

    Fuel is everywhere in the city, with stations on every main road, so fill up and eat a proper breakfast before you leave. Fish rolls are the local fast food, and a Fischbrötchen with a coffee on the waterfront is a good way to start. Plan to leave early on weekends in summer, when the city fills with visitors and the roads toward the coast can be slow. The reward comes after the first half hour: lakes, hedgerows, small villages with brick churches, and a big sky that belongs entirely to the north.

  2. Eckernförde bay swim · 8 m

    Eckernförde Bay is a long, narrow arm of the Baltic that cuts into the coast of Schleswig-Holstein between the Danish Wohld and the Schwansen peninsula, and the little port of the same name sits at its head. The water is shallow, the beaches are wide and sandy, and the whole place has the relaxed air of a north German seaside town, with a promenade, a harbour full of boats and a smell of smoked fish. The altitude here is a modest eight metres, and the horizon is all sea and sky.

    The town is known for its smoked sprats, the Kieler Sprotten's nearest rival, which are sold hot from small smokehouses near the harbour. The bay was also a naval base for many years, and the German navy still has a presence nearby, a reminder that this quiet coastline has long had strategic importance. Behind the town, the land is a mix of low hills, farmland and woods, with small villages and the traces of Viking-age and medieval history never very far away. Windeby, a few kilometres inland, is famous for the Iron Age bog body found nearby in the 1950s.

    The riding along the bay and across Schwansen is gentle, not a challenge. Roads run between fields of rapeseed and wheat, often lined with trees, with long straights and occasional bends, and the surface is usually good. In summer the coast road can be busy with holidaymakers, and the wind off the Baltic can be strong, so be ready for gusts on exposed stretches. Early mornings and evenings are the best times, when the light over the water is soft and the beach is almost empty.

    Swimming is the draw here. The Baltic is not warm, and the water takes time to heat up, but on a calm summer afternoon it is clear and calm, with a shallow slope and sandy bottom, and a dip after a long ride is a good reward. Eckernförde, with over twenty thousand people, has petrol stations, cafés and restaurants in plenty, and a fish roll by the harbour is a classic lunch.

    The ride combines the bay with the Schlei, the long inlet to the north, where small villages and coffee stops invite a slow pace.

  3. Schwansen backroads scenic · 35 m

    Schwansen is a blunt thumb of land between the Schlei and the Bay of Eckernförde, and it is one of the most underrated corners of Schleswig-Holstein. The countryside is gently rolling farmland, divided into fields by Knicks, those hedge-topped earth banks that give the Baltic coast its distinctive patchwork, and dotted with old estates, thatched barns and brick villages. The sea is always near, on one side or the other, and glimpses of water through gaps in the hedge give the roads a pleasing sense of mystery.

    The area has a distinctly Danish feel, which is no accident. This was border country for centuries, and for much of the Middle Ages it belonged to the Danish crown, with Danish place names and Danish habits that lingered long after the political border moved. The languages of the area were Low German, Danish and Frisian, and you still see double-language signs in places farther north. The villages of Schwansen have names like Rieseby and Waabs, and the big landowners built manor houses with long drives and avenue trees, some of which are still in private hands.

    The backroads are the best way to see it. They run in gentle undulations, over small bridges and through patches of beech wood, with low stone walls, lines of poplar and old oaks, and every few kilometres a church tower. The surface is generally good, though some roads are narrow and patched, and farm vehicles take up the whole width at harvest. On a clear day the road sometimes opens to a view of the Baltic with sails on the water. Wind is a factor, and the weather changes quickly, so be ready for a sudden shower.

    Services are modest. Damp, on the coast, is a holiday resort that grew up in the 1970s and has shops, cafés and a long beach, and villages like Waabs and Rieseby have a shop or a bakery. For fuel and a bigger choice, Kappeln, on the Schlei, about nine kilometres away, is the nearest town with fuel stations and supermarkets, and Eckernförde to the south has more. Plan the tank accordingly. Spring, when the rape fields are bright yellow, and early autumn, with stubble gold and the sky enormous, are the best times for this quiet, reflective ride.

  4. Kappeln Schlei bridge sight · 8 m

    Kappeln lies on the Schlei, a long narrow inlet of the Baltic that reaches forty-odd kilometres inland, like a fjord that forgot to be steep. The bridge itself is the town's emblem: a lifting bridge from the 1920s that opens to let ships through, so be prepared to wait a few minutes while the roadway rises and the sailing boats glide past.

    Kappeln is the capital of Angeln, the rolling, hedge-patterned peninsula between the Schlei and the Flensburg Fjord that gave England its name. The Angles, a Germanic tribe who migrated to Britain in the fifth century, are said to have come from this region, and you will see the name on signs and shops. The country is a landscape of low hills, farms and thatched cottages, with winding lanes under old trees and hardly any traffic. The surface is generally good, and the bends are gentle, making it a very pleasant place to ride slowly.

    On the water, the Heringszaun is the unique sight. This is the last herring fence in Europe, a long line of stakes and nets in the shape of a funnel that traps herring as they swim in during spring, and it has been used for centuries. Fresh and smoked herring are sold from stalls and small restaurants nearby, and a roll with smoked fish and onion is the thing to eat while watching the boats. A Dutch-style windmill stands on the harbour, and there are cafes along the waterfront.

    Gelting, ten kilometres away, is the gateway to the Geltinger Birk, a nature reserve of heath, dunes and grazing cattle on the Baltic coast, where the land is flat and the wind is steady. Go in the morning, when the air is bright and the reeds shine. The old harbour and the maritime atmosphere make Kappeln a good stop for a long lunch, and for an excursion boat down the Schlei.

  5. Schleswig food stop food · 12 m

    Schleswig is a town you eat in with history around your ears. The quickest way to understand it is to walk to the harbour on the Schlei, where fish smokehouses and small restaurants sit beside a basin of yachts and the old cathedral spire rises over the roofs behind. Smoked herring, Schlei eel and fried plaice are all on offer, with potatoes and a pot of tea or coffee, and a table by the water on a warm day is hard to beat. Prices are fair, portions generous, and nobody hurries you.

    A few kilometres south lies the Danewerk, the great line of earthen ramparts that the Danes built across the narrow neck of the Jutland peninsula, beginning as early as the fifth century and extended over the next six hundred years. It was a defensive wall against the Germans and Franks, and its remains, grassy banks several metres high, can still be walked. At one end stood the Viking trading town of Haithabu, which lay on the Schlei and was among the largest settlements in northern Europe about a thousand years ago. Both are listed as a World Heritage site, and the museum at Haithabu has reconstructed longhouses and a remarkable collection of finds.

    In the town itself, on an island in the Schlei, stands Gottorf Castle, a huge, pale baroque pile that was the seat of the dukes of Schleswig-Holstein-Gottorf. It now houses two state museums, one of art and one of archaeology, and the archaeology museum has one of the most extraordinary collections in Germany, including a large wooden ship from the fourth century found in a Danish bog, and the bodies of Iron Age people preserved in peat. It is a strange and sobering place to spend an hour.

    The ride in and out is simple, with quiet farmland roads in every direction and a long, open approach along the Schlei from the east. Schleswig has about twenty-four thousand people, so fuel stations, supermarkets and bakeries are all easy, and parking near the harbour is straightforward, if the cobbles around the old town are rough. Weekends in summer fill the harbour with visitors, so arrive before noon. After a meal, you can go on along the Schlei toward Kappeln or turn west toward the North Sea coast.

  6. Rendsburg canal check sight · 10 m

    Rendsburg is a town made by a ditch, and what a ditch. The Kiel Canal, known in German as the Nord-Ostsee-Kanal, was opened in 1895 as the Kaiser-Wilhelm-Kanal, cutting across the base of the Danish peninsula so that ships need not sail all the way around Skagen. It is still the busiest artificial seaway in the world by number of ships, and standing on the bank at Rendsburg you can watch container vessels, tankers and ferries glide past at what looks like walking pace, a few metres from the road. A quiet fact makes it more striking: the surrounding land is so flat and low, only a few metres above sea level, that the ships seem to be sailing through meadows.

    The landmark is the high railway bridge of 1913, a great steel loop that lifts the line high enough to clear the shipping, and beneath it hangs a transporter ferry, a platform on cables that carries cars and bikes across the canal. It is one of very few still working in the world, and riding onto it is a genuine curiosity. The old road tunnel under the canal, from 1961, is the other crossing, and several ferries operate free of charge along the canal. Beside the water is the old town on the Eider river, a tidy mix of garrison buildings and brick houses dating to the time when the Danish crown fortified the place.

    The riding in this region is gentle, flat and often windy, a world of long straight roads, hedge-lined fields called knicks, reed beds and quiet villages. It is not a place for hairpins, but it has a generous, slow rhythm, and the canal-side roads on both banks are very pleasant on a bike, with the ships alongside. Wind from the North Sea can be strong, and cross-winds on the bridges can surprise you.

    Rendsburg has nearly thirty thousand people, so you will find fuel, bakeries, fish sandwiches and cafes, and the neighbouring town of Büdelsdorf has the old iron works turned into an art venue. Try a Fischbrötchen by the water on a sunny afternoon, and watch for the big ships, which announce themselves with a deep horn.

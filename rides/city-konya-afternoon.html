Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Konya

    Konya lies on the dry central plateau of Anatolia, about a thousand metres above the sea, and it has been an important city for as long as anyone has kept records. The Seljuk Turks made it their capital in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, and it became a centre of learning and Sufi mysticism, the home of Jalal ad-Din Rumi, the poet and teacher who is known in Turkey as Mevlana. After his death in 1273 his followers founded the Mevlevi order, whose members perform the whirling ceremony, the sema, in which dancers in tall felt hats and white skirts turn slowly with one palm raised and one lowered. His tomb, under a fluted dome covered in turquoise tiles, is the most visited shrine in the country, and the pilgrims who gather there at all hours give the city a quiet devotional tone.

  2. 2. Neolithic Site of Çatalhöyük

    Nine thousand years ago, people here lived in a town with no streets. Çatalhöyük is a pair of low mounds on the Konya plain, and what makes it famous is how its houses were packed together wall to wall, entered by ladders through the roof, with the dead buried under the floors and bulls' horns plastered into the walls. It is one of the earliest large settlements anywhere and has been a World Heritage site since 2012.

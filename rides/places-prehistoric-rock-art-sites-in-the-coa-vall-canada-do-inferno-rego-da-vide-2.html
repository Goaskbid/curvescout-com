Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Prehistoric Rock Art Sites in the Côa Vall

    On the Spanish side of the border with Portugal, the river Águeda cuts through granite and schist on its way to the Douro, and on the rock faces along its banks, at Siega Verde, Palaeolithic people engraved horses, aurochs, deer and goats more than ten thousand years ago. In 2010 Siega Verde was added to the Prehistoric Rock Art Sites in the Côa Valley, the Portuguese World Heritage Site just across the border, recognising that these open-air engravings form one of the largest collections of Ice Age art in the world.

  2. 2. Faia - Vale Afonsinho

    Thirty thousand years ago, give or take, people in this valley scratched horses, aurochs and ibex into the schist rocks along the River Côa, and their drawings would almost certainly have been drowned if a dam had been built here in the 1990s. Instead, after a fierce campaign, the dam was abandoned and the Côa Valley rock art became one of the most important open-air Palaeolithic art sites in the world, listed by UNESCO in 1998 together with Siega Verde across the border in Spain. Faia, in the side valley near Vale Afonsinho, is one of its core sites, with engravings and some rare painted figures on the rock faces above the river.

  3. 3. Canada do Inferno / Rego da vide

    The Côa valley holds one of the greatest open-air galleries of Paleolithic art on earth, and it was almost lost. In the early 1990s, as work began on a dam to flood the valley, archaeologists found engravings of horses, aurochs and ibex on the schist cliffs, some of them more than twenty thousand years old. After a public campaign, the dam was abandoned in 1996, and in 1998 the site was listed by UNESCO. It remains one of the rare cases in Europe where a planned dam gave way to cave art.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Mosquée de Nambira ou Namboura missiri kor

    In the north of Côte d'Ivoire, where the forest gives way to savanna, the villages are built of mud and every mosque looks like a sculpture grown out of the ground. They belong to the Sudanese style, a tradition from the Sahel that spread south along trade routes carried by Dyula merchants, and UNESCO listed several of them in 2021. The mosques of Kaouara, Kouto, Nambira, Tengrela and others are the survivors of that tradition in this country.

  2. 2. Mosquée de Kouto

    Kouto is a small town in the far north of Côte d'Ivoire, close to the borders with Mali and Burkina Faso, and its mosque is one of the best-known of the Sudanese-style buildings of the region. Mud-brick walls rise in battered buttresses, each topped by a small pinnacle, and timber beams project from the surface like the quills of a hedgehog. Seen across a field in the morning light, it looks as though it grew there.

  3. 3. Sudanese style mosques in northern Côte d’

    Across the savanna of northern Côte d'Ivoire stands a small family of mud mosques that look as if they were grown rather than built. Eight of them were inscribed together by UNESCO in 2021 as the Sudanese style mosques of the north, among them the mosques of Kong, Kawara, Sorobango, Samatiguila, Nambira and Tengréla. They belong to an architectural tradition that came south with Mande traders and clerics along the old caravan routes from the Niger bend, and that took root in the cotton and kola country between the Sahel and the forest.

Why this ride

A one-way ride of 515 km, Odawara to Atami - roughly 9 h 20 in the saddle. On the way it passes Hakone Turnpike, Lake Ashi view, Fuji Five Lakes window and Atami and coast. The climb of the day is Izu Skyline high road. It tops out at about 1,050 m. Back roads rather than main roads, where the map allows.

Scenic road chapters

Hakone Turnpike and Skyline

For 36 km this is Hakone Turnpike and Skyline, a road with a name of its own and a reason for it — expect company on a summer weekend. Early is better than fast.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Odawara fuel gate

    Odawara is a castle town at the foot of Hakone, and for most riders it is the place where the mountains begin. The castle, which belonged to the Hojo clan until Toyotomi Hideyoshi besieged it in 1590, has a reconstructed keep, built in 1960, with a museum inside. The old town around it is quiet, with a modest shopping street and some traditional shops. It sits on Sagami Bay, with the coast road leading east and west and the Hakone mountains rising behind.

  2. 2. Hakone Turnpike rhythm

    Rhythm is the right word for the Hakone Turnpike, because the road does not so much challenge you as set a tempo. Near Hakone, with Yugawara down the hill and Odawara on the coast, it climbs through broad bends that follow the shape of the old volcano, and after a few kilometres you find a steady pace in which gear changes and lean angles settle into a pattern. It is a road for enjoying rather than attacking, with smooth tarmac, clear sightlines and a limit that police take seriously.

  3. 3. Lake Ashi view

    Lake Ashi fills the caldera of the old Hakone volcano, at 720 metres, and it is one of the most visited places in Japan, partly because of the view. On a clear morning, particularly in winter, the cone of Mount Fuji rises above the water in perfect symmetry, and the red torii gate of Hakone Shrine stands in the lake in the foreground.

  4. 4. Fuji Five Lakes food window

    Fujikawaguchiko and Fujiyoshida stand a few kilometres either side of this stop, and between them the northern face of Mount Fuji dominates everything. The Fuji Five Lakes, strung along the foot of the volcano, are the usual base for people who want a view of the mountain, and the whole district has grown up around tourism, with inns, restaurants and shops catering to hikers, photographers and bus tours. It is a busy place in season, but the food is worth the crowds.

  6. 6. Atami fuel and coast gate

    Atami is where Tokyo comes to forget about Tokyo. It is a hot-spring resort stacked up a steep hillside above Sagami Bay, with concrete hotels, narrow lanes, a small harbour and a long curve of beach, and it has the happy, slightly faded air of a seaside town that was fashionable in the 1960s. The city has fewer than forty-five thousand people, but it feels bigger, because everything is vertical and every street seems to have a view of the water.

  7. 7. Izu Skyline high road

    At the high end of the Izu Skyline, with Atami five kilometres away and the Tanna basin tucked in the valley to the west, the road runs along a grassy ridge where the wind is a constant companion. Gusts can be strong enough to push a bike sideways, particularly at the exposed corners, so keep a firm grip and reduce speed when the grass starts to bend. On the ridge the air is cool and clean, and even in July the temperature can be ten degrees below that on the coast.

  8. 8. Shuzenji onsen food

    Shuzenji sits in a narrow valley in the middle of the Izu peninsula, on the Katsura river, and it is the oldest hot spring resort on Izu. The onsen town grew around a temple, Shuzen-ji, founded according to tradition by the monk Kobo Daishi in the early ninth century, and it has an old-fashioned, quiet charm that the busier resorts on the coast have lost. Hot water comes from springs beneath the town, and an open-air bath in the middle of the river, called Tokko no Yu, is the symbol of the place.

  9. 9. Ito ocean stop

    Itō sits on the eastern side of the Izu Peninsula, a city of about seventy thousand strung along a coast of black lava and quiet coves. It is a place of hot springs, fishing boats and gentle roads, and it has an unexpected piece of history. In the early 1600s the English sailor William Adams, who became a samurai advisor named Miura Anjin, supervised the building of Japan's first Western-style ships here, and a small monument by the river recalls it. It is a small but real moment: the beginning of Japan's contact with European shipbuilding.

  10. 10. back to Atami fuel and

    Coming back to Atami at the end of a long day on the Izu roads has its own pleasures. The town appears first as lights climbing the hillside, hotels glowing up the slope like a stack of lit windows, and the harbour below dark and still. The road drops down a series of tight curves, and the temperature changes as you descend, usually a degree or two warmer at sea level, with a smell of salt and sulphur in the air that is the signature of a hot-spring town.

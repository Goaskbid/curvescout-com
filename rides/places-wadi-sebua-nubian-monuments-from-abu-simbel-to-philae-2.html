Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Wadi Sebua

    Wadi es-Sebua means Valley of the Lions, and the name is earned. The temple here, built by Ramesses II, is approached down an avenue of sphinxes, with a colossal statue of the king standing at the front, and then it burrows into the rock behind. The sphinxes have lions' bodies and the faces of the pharaoh, and in the late afternoon their shadows lie long along the sand. Part of the building is freestanding stone, part is cut from the cliff, which makes it feel half temple and half cave.

  2. 2. Amada

    Amada is the smallest of the Nubian temples saved from Lake Nasser, and in some ways the finest. It was built in the fifteenth century BC under Thutmose III and Amenhotep II, and its painted reliefs are among the best preserved in Egypt, with colours still fresh inside the sanctuary. When the High Dam threatened to drown Lower Nubia, the whole temple was lifted onto rails in the mid-1960s and moved a couple of kilometres inland to higher ground, a feat of engineering that is easy to miss when you stand in front of the modest sandstone walls. The famous Abu Simbel temples lie farther south, and Wadi es-Sebua farther north, with Amada in between.

  3. 3. Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae

    Everything at Abu Simbel is a rescue story. Ramesses II had the two rock temples carved into the cliff above the Nile in the thirteenth century BC, with four colossal seated figures of himself guarding the larger one. Then, in the 1960s, the Aswan High Dam was about to drown the whole valley, and an international UNESCO campaign cut both temples into huge blocks and rebuilt them on higher ground, inside an artificial hill. It is one of the great engineering efforts of the twentieth century, and it is the reason a rider can stand here at all, with Lake Nasser glittering off to one side.

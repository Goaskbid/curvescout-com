Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Selent lake shore

    Selent is a village of about thirteen hundred people, and its lake, the Selenter See, is the second largest in Schleswig-Holstein, a long glacial basin of roughly twenty-two square kilometres. Nothing here is grand. The shores are low and wooded, reed beds line the edges, and there are good places for birdwatching, with grebes, swans and cormorants common. It is a quiet bit of Holstein Switzerland where the main events are the arrival of the wind, the colour of the water and the occasional sailing dinghy.

  2. 2. Lütjenburg snack stop

    A snack stop in a town like Lütjenburg is an education in how small-town northern Germany eats between meals. Every bakery has a counter of rolls, filled with ham, cheese, egg, smoked fish or a slab of Leberwurst, and most of them also sell cake by the slice and coffee in paper cups. You pay at the till, take your bag and sit on a bench in the square, and nobody minds. A fish roll from a stall, with pickled herring, onions and a dill pickle, is the more adventurous choice, and a sweet pastry from the baker the safer one.

  3. 3. Hohwacht water stop

    Not every water stop has to involve getting wet. Hohwacht works for riders because it offers a place to refill a bottle, sit near the sea and let the engine cool, and because the little harbour has a calm, working character that is a relief after the busier resorts to the east. The marina holds a mix of small fishing boats and sailing yachts, and a few smokehouses and kiosks do a quiet trade. In the early morning, before the day-trippers arrive, you can hear the halyards tapping against the masts and the cry of gulls.

  4. 4. Preetz return line

    Preetz sits on the Schwentine river between two lakes, and its old convent is the reason to slow down. The Benedictine nunnery of Preetz, founded in the early thirteenth century, survived the Reformation by turning into a Protestant convent for unmarried daughters of the Holstein nobility, a role it still plays today, and its Gothic brick church and cloister stand on a peaceful spit of land beside the Kellersee. The nuns, or rather the ladies, are not in view, but the buildings are lovely, and a short walk around the grounds is a quiet pleasure.

  6. 6. Boknis Eck viewpoint

    Boknis Eck is a headland on the coast of the Eckernförde Bay, on the Baltic side of Schleswig-Holstein, and the first thing you notice is how low everything is. The land at this point stands only a few metres above the sea. It is a coastline of small cliffs, beaches strewn with flint and boulders left by the glaciers, and long views across the water toward Denmark. The sea off Boknis Eck is also known to marine scientists, who have run a time series station here since the 1950s, measuring temperature, salinity and nutrients in the bay.

  7. 7. Eckernförde lunch

    Eckernförde sits on a bay that curves into the Baltic like a cupped hand, and its old harbour is where a day in the northern lowlands comes to rest. The town is known above all for smoked fish, and in particular for the Kieler Sprotten, small, golden-brown sprats that are named after Kiel but are traditionally made here, in smokehouses along the water. They are eaten whole, by hand, with a bit of bread, and they taste of oak smoke and salt. If you eat one thing in Eckernförde, make it that. A fish roll with fried herring or plaice is the safer alternative, and plenty of harbour kiosks sell them.

  8. 8. Kappeln bridge

    Kappeln guards the middle of the Schlei, and its bridge is the town's pulse. The Schlei is a long, narrow inlet of the Baltic that winds forty kilometres inland to Schleswig, and the bridge at Kappeln is where the road crosses it. It is a double-leaf bascule bridge, which opens at set times to let sailing boats pass, and when it lifts, traffic stops and everyone gets out to watch. Waiting for it is part of the local rhythm, and you will not be hurried.

  1. 1. Gdansk

    Gdansk has been a trading port for a thousand years, and the front of its Long Market, the Dluga Targ, shows what the money bought. Tall, narrow merchant houses stand shoulder to shoulder, their facades painted and gilded and carved with figures, around the Neptune Fountain and the Artus Court. In the Second World War, the city was ninety percent destroyed, and what you see today is a painstaking reconstruction from old paintings and photographs, which makes it more remarkable. The Gothic crane on the quay, the Zuraw, was once the largest in Europe, and it hoisted masts and cargo from ships in the Middle Ages.

  2. 2. Castle of the Teutonic Order in Malbork

    Seen across the river Nogat on a clear day, Malbork looks almost too large to be real. It is the biggest brick castle in the world by area, built by the Teutonic Knights from the late thirteenth century and made their capital in 1309, when the Grand Master moved here from Venice. They called it Marienburg, in honour of the Virgin, and it served as the fortress, monastery and administrative headquarters of a military state that stretched along the Baltic. The brick walls rise in layer on layer, with a high castle, a middle castle and a lower castle, protected by moats, towers and gates, and it was more of a small city than a single building.

  1. 1. Fez

    Fez is the oldest of Morocco's imperial cities and it has been busy for well over a thousand years. The medina, Fès el-Bali, founded in the late eighth and early ninth centuries and listed by UNESCO, is a maze of thousands of alleys, many too narrow for anything but a donkey, and it is often described as the largest car-free urban area in the world. Inside are workshops, mosques, madrasas, fountains and markets, all packed so tightly that the sky is often a thin strip above your head.

  2. 2. Chefchaouen

    Chefchaouen sits at the foot of two peaks in the Rif Mountains, in northern Morocco, and it is famous for being blue. Nobody is quite sure why the old town was painted in shades of sky and indigo. Some say it started with Jewish refugees in the 1930s, others that the colour keeps mosquitoes away or cools the houses, and local explanations vary. What is certain is that the effect is extraordinary, with stairs, doorways and walls washed in blue and fresh paint going on every spring. The town was founded in 1471 as a small fortress by Moulay Ali Ben Rachid, to guard against Portuguese raids from the coast, and it later received Muslim and Jewish refugees from Andalusia, whose influence shows in the architecture.

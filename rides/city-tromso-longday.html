  1. 1. Tromso

    Tromsø is unexpectedly lively for a town so far north. It stands on an island, linked to the mainland by a bridge and a tunnel, at nearly seventy degrees north, with snow-covered peaks all around and about fifty thousand people who have built a university, a brewery and a fine collection of bars into the Arctic. It has been called the Paris of the North, partly in jest and partly because it has long had a worldly side, thanks to the polar expeditions that set out from its harbour.

    Kåfjord is a side arm of the Altafjord in Finnmark, and the little settlement on it lies just a few kilometres from Alta. It is a quiet place of birch, low farms and water, with the big mountains of the plateau standing behind. In the middle of the nineteenth century a copper mine operated here, financed by British investors, and the English managers brought a style of living that left a few odd traces in this far northern corner. The mine's story is a reminder that the Arctic coast was never as empty as the map suggests.

  3. 3. Rock Art of Alta

    On the shore of the Altafjord, a few kilometres from the town, a long hill of smooth grey rock slopes down to the water, and its surface is covered with pictures. There are thousands of them, carved by hunters between roughly seven thousand and two thousand years ago: reindeer, elk, bears, boats, fish, and human figures that seem to dance or drum. The carvings sit at Hjemmeluft, beside the Alta Museum, and the whole group is a World Heritage site. The oldest are life-size reindeer, and some of the later scenes show hunting and ritual in strikingly lively detail.

  4. 4. Hjemmeluft

    Six thousand years ago, someone stood on a polished rock slab beside the head of the Alta fjord and began to peck a reindeer into the stone. Then they did it again, and so did their descendants for the next four thousand years. The result, at Hjemmeluft and the other carving fields around Alta, is the biggest collection of prehistoric rock art in northern Europe, thousands of figures of elk, reindeer, bears, fish, boats and people, and some in curious shamanic poses, drumming or dancing. It was listed by UNESCO in 1985. The carvings were made by hunter-gatherers whose world was the shore, the ice and the seasonal movement of animals, and many show hunting scenes, with the line of a reindeer fence or a figure drawing a bow.

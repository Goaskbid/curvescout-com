Why this ride

From Groß Munzel it runs 423 km to Laatzen; allow about 9 h 21 of riding. The stops in between: Rinteln, Extertal hill roads and Lemgo old town. The climbs: Barntrup ridge and Oker valley bends. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hannover west escape

    The Deister is a long, wooded ridge that rises abruptly from the flat country west of Hannover, and this starting point lies at its northern foot, in the farmland between Groß Munzel and Großgoltern. Barsinghausen, a town of fourteen thousand about seven kilometres away, is the gateway to the hills. Coal was mined in the Deister until 1957, and traces of that industry survive in the old shafts and railway embankments that you find in the forest.

  2. 2. Rinteln fuel gate

    Rinteln lies on the Weser, in the hills of Lower Saxony where the river cuts through a gap in the wooded ridges, and it has the look of a town that was prosperous in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and was then left in peace. Half-timbered houses with carved beams line the old market square, and the town hall and the Nikolai church stand among them. The architecture belongs to what is known as the Weser Renaissance, a decorative style with gables, bay windows and carved ornament that spread along the river in that period.

  3. 3. Extertal hill roads

    South of Rinteln the land rises into the Lippe hills, and the Exter valley runs up through them in a quiet, winding way. The area has a name among riders for its relaxed and flowing roads, and it is easy to see why: the lanes follow the folds of the land, climbing gently through beech and oak woods, dropping to small streams and passing through villages of half-timbered houses with red tiled roofs. It is a landscape on a human scale, with no big peaks or dramatic views, just mile after mile of green.

  4. 4. Lemgo old town

    Lemgo is one of those German towns where the old centre survived intact, a mass of half-timbered and stone gabled houses in the Weser Renaissance style, grouped around a market place and a clutch of churches. The town of about forty-two thousand people lies in the Lippe region in eastern North Rhine-Westphalia, on the edge of the Teutoburg Forest, and was once a member of the Hanseatic League, which explains the wealth of building from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

  5. 5. Barntrup ridge

    Barntrup is a small town in the Lippe uplands, and the ridge above it gives one of the pleasanter views in this part of Germany. The land here is a series of long, wooded ridges separated by wide, farmed valleys, and the road along the top gives views over the patchwork of fields, with villages scattered between. It is a landscape of modest scale, but with a calm beauty that grows on you the longer you ride.

  6. 6. Hameln coffee return

    Everyone knows the story, and Hameln has not tried to hide it. According to the legend, in 1284 the town was overrun by rats, and a stranger in colourful clothes offered to rid it of them in return for payment. He played his pipe, the rats followed him into the Weser and drowned, and the town refused to pay. So he played again, and this time the town's children followed him out of the gate and were never seen again. The Brothers Grimm wrote it down, Robert Browning wrote a poem, and the Pied Piper became one of the best-known figures in European folklore. The historical record does record some tragedy in 1284, though what actually happened is still a mystery.

  7. 7. Back to Hannover

    Coming back into Hannover from the west, the city rises out of flat farmland a little too modestly, with the green mass of the Deister ridge behind you and the tower of a power station somewhere ahead. Hannover does not shout. It is a city of half a million people that was flattened by bombing in the Second World War and rebuilt with more sense than glamour, and the best of it is tucked into corners you have to know about. The Maschsee, a big artificial lake dug by hand in the 1930s as a work programme, is the place the locals walk, run and sail on summer evenings.

  8. 8. Hannover south-east edge

    Laatzen is where Hannover's southern edge turns into the exhibition halls, a town of forty thousand, with the great fair grounds a few minutes away. The Expo of the year 2000 was held on this side of the city, and the vast halls and the odd, slightly futuristic pavilions that survive give the district a strange, half-empty feel on a quiet morning. It is a spot to meet and set off, not a place to linger.

  9. 9. Salzgitter fuel gate

    Salzgitter is not a town for postcards, and that is exactly why it makes a good fuel gate. It was built around steel. In 1937 the Nazi government founded a huge ironworks here to exploit local iron ore, and the city that grew around it was a collection of villages merged into one, with a sprawling industrial landscape. Today the steelworks still operates, and the skyline is a mix of chimneys, rail yards and housing estates, softened by green belts and lakes.

  10. 10. Goslar old road

    Goslar is one of those towns that makes you stop the engine and simply look. It sits on the northern edge of the Harz mountains in Lower Saxony, and it was for centuries an imperial city, with a palace where German emperors held court in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. The Kaiserpfalz, a huge Romanesque hall restored in the nineteenth century, still stands on the hill, with murals showing scenes of imperial history. Around it the old town is a maze of cobbled lanes, crooked half-timbered houses and slate-roofed gables, so well preserved that UNESCO listed it in 1992.

  11. 11. Oker valley bends

    The Oker is the river that cuts the Harz in two, and the road that follows it from Goslar toward Altenau is among the best in northern Germany. The valley is narrow and steep, with dark spruce forest on the slopes and bare granite cliffs breaking through, and the road keeps close company with the water, bending with every meander. The corners come one after another, tight enough to keep you busy and open enough to enjoy, and the surface is generally good.

  12. 12. Clausthal-Zellerfeld high point

    Clausthal-Zellerfeld sits on a plateau at around six hundred metres in the Upper Harz, and the town has a surprise at its centre: the Marktkirche, a huge church built entirely of wood, said to be the largest wooden church in Germany. It was built in the seventeenth century by a mining community that was rich enough to afford it and short of stone, and it is a fine sight, with its dark timber walls and a roof of slate. Inside it is painted and carved with a quiet grandeur.

  13. 13. Seesen food stop

    Seesen sits at the western edge of the Harz, where the hills begin to rise out of the plain, and it is a handy town for a stop with a little history tucked inside. In the early nineteenth century it was the home of the Jacobson School, founded by a Jewish banker and reformer named Israel Jacobson, one of the first schools to bring Jewish and Christian pupils together and to push for the reform of Jewish worship, and a temple he built beside it became a model for the first Reform synagogues. The temple was destroyed in 1938, and a memorial now marks the site.

  14. 14. Back to Hannover

    Hannover is not the first name that comes to mind when riders talk about great destinations, and it would probably say that is fine. It is a big, calm, practical city of half a million people on the northern edge of the German hills, where the flat North German Plain begins to roll up toward the Deister and the Weser Uplands.

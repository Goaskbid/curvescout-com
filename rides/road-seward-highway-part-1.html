Why this ride

From Anchorage it runs 251 km to the finish; allow about 3 h 35 of riding. On the way it passes Beluga Point and Portage. The highest point is about 800 m. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Anchorage

    Anchorage lies where Cook Inlet meets the Chugach mountains, a city of about three hundred thousand people that holds nearly half of Alaska's population and yet ends abruptly at the edge of the forest. Moose wander through the suburbs, and on a clear day, the snowy peaks of the Alaska Range, including Denali far to the north, float on the horizon. The city grew from a railway construction camp in 1915, and was badly damaged by the Good Friday earthquake in 1964, among the most powerful ever recorded.

  2. 2. Beluga Point

    Turnagain Arm has the second-highest tides in North America, and Beluga Point is the best seat for watching them work. The Seward Highway runs along the cliffs here, squeezed between the Chugach Mountains and a long, silvery fjord that empties and refills twice a day with a tidal range of about nine metres. At low water you look down onto grey glacial mudflats; a few hours later the sea comes back, sometimes as a tidal bore, a single low wave that runs up the inlet at walking speed. The best views of the bore come an hour or two after low tide in Anchorage, and the locals keep a tide table the way others keep a weather forecast.

  3. 3. Portage

    Portage is hardly a place any more, and that is part of what makes it interesting. It lies at the head of Turnagain Arm, about forty miles south of Anchorage, where the Seward Highway runs along the flat, silty edge of the inlet with the Chugach Mountains rising steeply behind. The town was a railroad stop and farming settlement until the Good Friday earthquake of 1964, which struck with a magnitude of 9.2, dropped the land by several metres and let salt water flood the area. The grey drowned forest, bleached trunks of spruce standing in tidal flats, is still visible from the road, and the ghost town has long since been abandoned.

  4. 4. Moose Pass

    Moose Pass is a small cluster of cabins, a general store and a roadhouse beside a turquoise lake, and the speed limit through it is low for good reason. The village lies on the Kenai Peninsula in south-central Alaska, about forty kilometres north of Seward on the Seward Highway, and perhaps two hundred people live here. Trail Lake, in front of the village, is fed by glacial meltwater, and the water has the milky blue-green colour of ground rock, which makes the whole valley glow. Mountains still holding patches of snow rise on both sides, and spruce forest covers the lower slopes.

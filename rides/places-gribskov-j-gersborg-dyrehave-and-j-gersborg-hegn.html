Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Gribskov

    Gribskov is the second largest forest in Denmark, a broad expanse of beech and spruce on the north Zealand plain. Walking or riding here, the first impression is of light. Beech trees in spring have a green so fresh and bright that it is almost unreal, and in autumn they turn bronze and gold, and the forest floor is carpeted with leaves. The ground is gently rolling, with small lakes and bogs hidden between the trees, and red deer and roe deer live in the woods.

  2. 2. Kronborg Castle

    Hamlet never lived at Kronborg, but Shakespeare put him there, and the castle has been Elsinore in the imagination of the world ever since. It stands on a point of land at Helsingør where the Øresund narrows to about four kilometres, with Sweden clearly visible across the water, and for centuries it controlled one of the most important shipping lanes in Europe. King Frederik II built the castle in its Renaissance form in the 1570s and 1580s, and every ship passing through the strait had to stop and pay the Sound Dues, a toll that made the Danish crown rich for over four hundred years until it was abolished in 1857. Kronborg was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 2000.

  3. 3. Jægersborg Dyrehave and Jægersborg Hegn

    Danish kings loved to hunt, and in the late seventeenth century they laid out forests north of Copenhagen in a geometric pattern of straight paths radiating from central points, designed for par force hunting, in which riders and hounds chased a stag until it was exhausted. Jægersborg Dyrehave, the deer park, and Jægersborg Hegn are included in UNESCO's inscription of the par force hunting landscape in North Zealand, together with forests further north. The star-shaped network of rides is still visible, with numbered stone posts at the intersections that helped the hunters find their way.

  1. 1. Archaeological Ruins of Liangzhu City

    Liangzhu is a city that existed before writing. About five thousand years ago, on a marshy plain south of the Yangtze, a Neolithic people built a walled capital with a palace platform, canals, workshops and cemeteries, and they carved jade into ritual objects of astonishing skill. The city covered several square kilometres, and its walls, built of rammed earth on a base of stone, were up to sixty metres wide at the base. UNESCO listed the archaeological ruins in 2019 as evidence of an early regional state.

  2. 2. West Lake Cultural Landscape of Hangzhou

    There is a Chinese saying that in heaven there is paradise, and on earth there are Suzhou and Hangzhou. The West Lake, on the western edge of Hangzhou, has inspired poets, painters and garden designers for more than a thousand years, and its blend of water, hills, causeways, pagodas and temples became a model for landscape gardens across China, Japan and Korea. UNESCO inscribed it as a cultural landscape in 2011. The Su causeway, built by the poet-governor Su Dongpo in the eleventh century, and the Bai causeway, named after the Tang poet Bai Juyi, cross the lake, lined with willows and peach trees.

  3. 3. Jiaxing-Hangzhou Section of Jiangnan Canal

    The Grand Canal of China is the longest artificial waterway in the world, a chain of canals and rivers linking Beijing with Hangzhou that was begun in sections well over two thousand years ago and unified under the Sui dynasty in the seventh century. Its southernmost stretch, the Jiangnan Canal, crosses the flat, watery plain between Jiaxing and Hangzhou, and this section is part of the UNESCO World Heritage site. Grain, silk and salt moved north along it for centuries, and the towns that line its banks grew rich on that traffic.

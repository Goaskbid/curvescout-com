Why this ride

81 km from Giza to Giza, about 1 h 24 of riding. It goes by way of Al-Imam ash-Shaf'i Necropolis, Site of Memphis and back through Al-Imam ash-Shaf'i Necropolis. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Pyramid fields from Giza to Dahshur

    The pyramids of Giza are the most famous ancient monuments on earth, and they are also, absurdly, right at the end of a city street. The plateau lies on the western edge of Giza, a city of some two and a half million, and the urban sprawl runs right up to the fence. The Great Pyramid, built around 2560 BC for the pharaoh Khufu, was the tallest human structure in the world for nearly four thousand years, and it still takes the breath away, even when the first view comes between a fried-chicken sign and a minibus.

  2. 2. Al-Imam ash-Shaf'i Necropolis

    Cairo has a second city that most visitors never think about, a long ribbon of tombs on the southern flank of the old town known as the Qarafa, or City of the Dead. Families live in some of the funerary courtyards, there are shops and bakeries between the mausoleums, and in the middle of it all stands the tomb of Imam al-Shafi'i, the scholar who founded one of the four great schools of Sunni law and died in Egypt in 820. The big domed mausoleum you see today was raised in 1211 by the Ayyubid sultan al-Kamil, and the dome carries a bronze boat-shaped ornament on top that you can spot from surprising distances.

  3. 3. Site of Memphis

    Memphis was the first capital of a unified Egypt, or so tradition says, and for much of the Old Kingdom it was the centre of the world's most ordered state. Today there is little of it to see, because the city was built of mud brick and stripped for stone, and the Nile shifted away. What remains is a quiet area of palm groves and fields near the village of Mit Rahina, south of Cairo, where an open-air museum shows a few fragments of the capital that once stretched for kilometres along the river.

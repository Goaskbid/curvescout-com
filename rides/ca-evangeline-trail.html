  1. 1. Yarmouth

    Yarmouth is where Nova Scotia ends and the sea begins. It sits at the south-western tip of the province, closer to Maine than to Halifax, and for generations its harbour has been the door through which visitors from New England arrived, first on steamers and later on car ferries across the Gulf of Maine. The ferry still comes and goes on a seasonal schedule, so check before you plan around it, and you may see the big ship sitting at the wharf like a block of flats.

  2. 2. Digby

    Digby has two claims to fame, and both end up on a plate. The first is scallops: the fleet here lands some of the finest in the Atlantic, and the town leans proudly into the title Scallop Capital of the World. The second is the Digby chicken, which is not poultry at all but smoked herring, once packed and shipped across the continent. Walk along the wharf and you will smell the sea, diesel and fried fish in roughly equal measure.

  3. 3. Windsor

    Windsor lives beside a river that disappears twice a day. The Avon, where it meets the Minas Basin of the Bay of Fundy, drains almost completely at low tide and then fills again so fast that the mudflats vanish under a brown flood. The tide here ranks among the highest anywhere, and you can stand on the old causeway and watch the water climb the bank as if someone were running a bath. A tidal bore also pushes up some of the rivers in this area, and a quiet summer evening is a good time to look for the effect.

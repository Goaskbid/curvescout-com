Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Tunis

    Tunis does not hurry to impress you. It sits between a salt lagoon and the Gulf of Tunis, with a wide boulevard, Avenue Habib Bourguiba, running through the French-built part of the city and the old medina rising behind it. The medina's maze of covered souks climbs to the Zitouna Mosque, which dates from the eighth century and anchors the whole quarter. Cafes spill onto the pavements, men play cards and drink coffee, and the smell of grilled meat, jasmine and spices rolls out of the alleys. About seven hundred thousand people live in the city proper, with several million more in the larger area.

  2. 2. Land of the Roman houses of Amilcar

    The ground around Carthage is one huge archaeological layer cake. Phoenician traders arrived about the ninth century BC, a Punic city grew up and dominated the western Mediterranean, and in 146 BC the Romans finished it off after a long series of wars. A century later they decided to build a new city on the same hill, and it grew into one of the richest in the Roman world, the capital of the province of Africa. The Amilcar district, a residential area on the coast just north of the main ruins, is where some of the wealthy Romans built their villas, with views of the sea and gardens running down to the shore.

  3. 3. Basilica of Saint Cyprien

    Cyprian was bishop of Carthage in the middle of the third century, a former orator who converted late in life and became one of the great voices of the early African church. He argued about how to treat Christians who had given way under persecution, he stayed with his flock during a plague, and in 258 the Roman authorities beheaded him for refusing to sacrifice to the gods. The Christians of Carthage never forgot him. Over the centuries churches were built in his honour around the city, and the basilica of Saint Cyprien is associated with that memory, though what remains on the ground is modest, mostly traces of walls and columns that you need a guide or a good map to interpret.

  4. 4. Village of Sidi Bou Saïd

    Sidi Bou Saïd is a village painted in two colours, white and a deep Mediterranean blue, and rising above the Gulf of Tunis on a cliff that falls to the sea. The whitewashed houses, blue doors and window grilles, studded iron nails and bougainvillea spilling over walls are known the world over, though it was a conscious choice: in the early twentieth century a French baron and musicologist, Rodolphe d'Erlanger, who built a palace here, helped to promote a local style and then the authorities protected it by rule. The village is named after a Sufi saint, Abu Said al-Baji, who lived and taught here in the thirteenth century and whose tomb draws pilgrims.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Soganli site

    Soğanlı lies in a side valley of Cappadocia that most tour buses skip, south-east of Ürgüp and Yeşilhisar, on the slopes of the volcanic uplands. The name means with onions, which tells you something about the humble character of the place. The valley sides are made of soft volcanic tuff, which the local Christians carved out in the Byzantine centuries into churches, chapels, monasteries, and houses. Many of the rock churches date from the ninth to the thirteenth century and keep fragments of frescoes: Karabaş, the Yılanlı or Snake church, the Barbara church and others, whose names reflect old stories more than documented history.

  2. 2. Yesilöz site

    Cappadocia was made by volcanoes and carved by people. Millions of years ago, the volcanoes of central Anatolia, Erciyes and Hasan, covered the plateau in thick layers of ash that hardened into a soft rock called tuff, and wind and water have since eroded it into the cones, pinnacles and ridges that are known as fairy chimneys. People found the rock easy to carve, and for thousands of years they have dug homes, churches, storerooms and whole underground cities into it. The area is a World Heritage site, and it is hard to find a corner without a cave or a dovecote cut into a cliff.

  3. 3. Göreme National Park and the Rock Sites of

    Millions of years ago, two volcanoes, Erciyes and Hasan, covered central Turkey with ash, and the ash hardened into a soft, pale rock called tuff. Then rain and frost and wind went to work for a very long time. They carved the tuff into a landscape of cones, spires, mushrooms and canyons, the fairy chimneys of Cappadocia, in soft shades of cream, pink and apricot. About a thousand metres above sea level, around Goreme, the whole valley seems to have been sculpted by a mad pastry chef.

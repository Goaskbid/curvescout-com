Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Borobudur Temple

    Borobudur rises out of the Kedu Plain in central Java like a mountain that someone has carved into a book. Built in the ninth century under the Sailendra dynasty, it is the largest Buddhist monument in the world, a stepped pyramid of volcanic stone that stands about thirty metres high, with nine platforms, six square and three round, crowned by a central stupa. Walking up the galleries, you pass nearly three thousand relief panels telling the story of the Buddha's lives and the path to enlightenment, and as you reach the upper terraces, seventy-two perforated stupas hold seated Buddha figures looking out across the fields toward the volcanoes.

  2. 2. The Cosmological Axis of Yogyakarta and it

    Yogyakarta was laid out along a line. In the eighteenth century, Sultan Hamengkubuwono I founded the city on an axis running from the smoking cone of Mount Merapi in the north to the Indian Ocean in the south, with the Tugu monument, the Kraton palace and the Panggung Krapyak stage pavilion placed along it, embodying Javanese ideas about the cycle of life and the relation between people, ruler and the cosmos. The Cosmological Axis of Yogyakarta and its Historic Landmarks was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 2023, and the sultanate still functions: the sultan is also the governor of the Special Region of Yogyakarta.

  3. 3. Sangiran Early Man Site

    Half of what we know about Homo erectus in Asia seems to have come out of the ground near this village in central Java. Sangiran is a dome of eroded earth, a few kilometres across, in which stream cuts have exposed layers of volcanic ash and river sediment that built up over about two million years, and in those layers the bones of early humans and extinct animals have turned up. The first finds were made in the 1930s, notably by the German palaeontologist Ralph von Koenigswald, and the site has since produced the remains of some fifty individuals, about half of all known fossils of its kind in the world. UNESCO listed it in 1996.

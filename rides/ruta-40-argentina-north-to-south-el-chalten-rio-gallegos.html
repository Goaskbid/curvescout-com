Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. El Chalten

    El Chaltén is a young town in a very old landscape. It was founded in 1985, partly to establish an Argentine presence in a region disputed with Chile, and today it is the trekking capital of Argentina, a small cluster of lodges, cafes and gear shops beneath the granite spires of the Fitz Roy massif. Fitz Roy, 3,405 metres, is one of the most photographed mountains in South America, a jagged tower that the Tehuelche people are said to have called Chaltén, the smoking mountain, because clouds so often cling to its summit.

  2. 2. El Calafate

    El Calafate is a small town on the southern shore of Lago Argentino, and it is almost entirely about one thing: the ice. Eighty kilometres west, in Los Glaciares National Park, the Perito Moreno Glacier pushes out of the Andes into the lake in a wall of blue ice about five kilometres wide, rising to some seventy metres above the water. Unlike most glaciers it is relatively stable, and every few years it advances far enough to dam an arm of the lake, until the pressure of water breaks through in a roaring collapse. Watching blocks of ice calve from the face and crash into the lake, with a sound like thunder delayed by a second, is one of the great sights of Patagonia.

  3. 3. Rio Gallegos

    Río Gallegos is the capital of Santa Cruz, a city of eighty-five thousand on the Atlantic coast, near the southern tip of mainland Argentina, and it is the kind of place that you reach rather than choose. It sits on the estuary of the river of the same name, where the tide, among the largest in the world, drains the shallow water off the mudflats twice a day and leaves flocks of birds feeding on the exposed shore. Flamingos, ducks and shorebirds gather there, and the wide sky over the water is full of gulls. The wind, which blows for much of the year from the west, is a constant presence.

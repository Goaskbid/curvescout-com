Why this ride

A one-way ride of 1833 km, Tashkent to Khiwa - roughly 24 h 59 in the saddle. The stops in between: Samarkand and Bukhara. The highest point is about 1,050 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Tashkent

    Tashkent is the capital of Uzbekistan and the big, green, slightly surprising gateway to the Silk Road. It is a city of two million people with wide avenues, shady plane trees, fountains and a good deal of Soviet architecture, because a catastrophic earthquake in 1966 flattened much of the old town and the whole republic helped to rebuild it. What survived is mostly in the old quarter, around the Chorsu bazaar, with its blue-domed hall, spice stalls and mountains of bread, nuts and dried fruit.

  2. 2. Samarkand

    Samarkand is one of the oldest cities in Central Asia, and a name that has meant romance to travellers for two and a half thousand years. It sits in the green valley of the Zeravshan River, with the Pamir-Alay mountains rising to the south, on the great trade route that carried silk, paper, spices and ideas between China and the Mediterranean. Alexander the Great took the city, then called Marakanda, in 329 BC, and the mound of its ancient site, Afrasiab, still lies on the northern edge of the modern town. The paper trade is said to have reached the Islamic world here in the eighth century, when Chinese prisoners taught the craft after a battle on the Talas River.

  3. 3. Bukhara

    Bukhara is a city made of brick and memory, a trading hub on the Silk Road with a history of more than two thousand years, and its old centre is one of the best preserved in Central Asia. The Zeravshan River once watered the oasis around it, and the town's wealth came from carpets, silk, gold embroidery and its position between east and west. Genghis Khan's army burned much of it in 1220, but the city recovered and became a great centre of Islamic learning, with scores of madrasas and mosques.

  4. 4. Khiva

    Khiva rises out of the desert like a stage set that someone forgot to take down. The walled inner town, Itchan Kala, is built of mud brick and glazed tile on the edge of the Kyzylkum and Karakum deserts, in the fertile oasis of Khorezm along the lower Amu Darya. Its sand-colored walls, about ten meters high, enclose a maze of lanes, madrasas, mosques and minarets that was inscribed by UNESCO in 1990, among the first sites in Uzbekistan to be listed. Khiva was for centuries a khanate famous for its slave market and for the raiding caravans that crossed the steppe, and it kept its independence until the Russians took it in 1873.

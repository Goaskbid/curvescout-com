Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Kayseri

    Mount Erciyes dominates Kayseri in a way that the city seems to accept without comment. The extinct volcano rises to about 3,900 metres above the plain, snow-capped for much of the year, and its cone is visible from almost every street. Kayseri sits at its foot, a big, practical, trading city of nearly 600,000 with a black basalt citadel in the middle that was rebuilt by the Seljuks in the thirteenth century. The Seljuks left their mark here in the form of tomb towers, mosques and a medical school, the Gevher Nesibe complex, from 1206, which was one of the first of its kind.

  2. 2. Derinkuyu

    Beneath the ordinary Anatolian town of Derinkuyu lies a city. It was dug into the soft volcanic rock, tuff, that underlies the Cappadocia plateau, and it descends through many levels, with stables, kitchens, wine presses, storerooms, chapels and living quarters, all linked by narrow tunnels and stairways. Only the upper levels are open to visitors, but even these give a powerful sense of how large and organised the place was, and the air grows cooler and heavier as you go down.

  3. 3. Uchisar

    Everyone in Cappadocia ends up looking at Uçhisar, because it is the tallest thing for miles. A great knuckle of volcanic rock rises out of the village, honeycombed with windows and doorways, and for centuries it served as a citadel, the name itself meaning something like the fortress at the end. Climb the stairs inside and you step out onto the top with the whole plateau spread beneath you: valleys of pale tuff, pinnacles, vineyards, orchards, and far off on a clear day the snowy cone of Erciyes, a dormant volcano near Kayseri.

  4. 4. Goreme

    Göreme is the village at the centre of the postcard. Around it, in the valleys and ridges of the Cappadocia plateau, stand the fairy chimneys, tall cones of pale tuff capped with harder rock, and every other cliff face is pierced with windows and doors. The place has the odd, dreamlike quality of a landscape shaped by ash, water and patient hands, and people have lived inside it for as long as anyone can say.

  5. 5. Avanos

    Avanos is a pottery town on the Kızılırmak, the Red River, which is the longest river in Turkey and which cuts across the Cappadocia plateau on its way to the Black Sea. The river takes its name from the colour of the clay it carries, and the clay is why the town exists. For thousands of years potters here have dug the red earth from the banks, shaped it on kick wheels and fired it in kilns, a craft that is said to go back to Hittite times and still shapes the main street today.

  6. 6. Urgup

    Ürgüp has the air of a town that is comfortable with itself. It is one of the larger places on the Cappadocia plateau, with some sixteen thousand people, and it spreads across the foot of a rocky hill pocked with old cave dwellings, with stone houses in the old quarter and a lively centre of shops and cafés. Unlike Göreme, it has not been wholly given over to visitors, and in the evening the main square fills with families and teenagers rather than tour groups.

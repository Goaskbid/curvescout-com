  1. 1. Sines

    First impressions of Sines are industrial, and it is best to admit that at once. There is a big deep-water port and a petrochemical complex on the coast, and the flare stacks and tanks are visible from some distance. Yet the old town beyond the plant, set on a low headland over a bay, is a pleasant, quiet place of white houses and a castle, and it has a great claim to fame: Vasco da Gama, the first European to sail to India by sea, was born here in the fifteenth century.

  2. 2. Vila Nova de Milfontes

    Vila Nova de Milfontes sits where the Mira river reaches the Atlantic, on a low headland with white houses, a small fortress and a very good beach on either side. The fort at the river mouth, built to defend the town against pirates, has been turned into a guesthouse, and the old streets behind it are lined with cafes and small restaurants. In summer it is a favourite with Portuguese families, and in the quieter months it settles back into a gentle small-town rhythm.

  3. 3. Aljezur

    Aljezur is two villages in one. The old part climbs a steep hill under the ruins of a Moorish castle, while the newer part spreads along the valley floor beside a small river. The castle, a short walk up cobbled lanes, is a modest ruin, but the view from its walls over the valley, the whitewashed roofs and the fields running to the coast is excellent. The town is the capital of the west Algarve coast and sits in the middle of the Southwest Alentejo and Vicentine Coast Natural Park.

  4. 4. Sagres

    Sagres is where Portugal runs out of land. The village sits on a bare, wind-scoured promontory at the south-western tip of Europe, and a few kilometres away the lighthouse at Cabo de São Vicente stands on cliffs about seventy-five metres above the Atlantic. The ancient world believed this was the end of the earth, and the Romans called the cape the Sacred Promontory. The sea here is deep blue and often rough, and the light has a clarity that comes only with a long fetch of open ocean.

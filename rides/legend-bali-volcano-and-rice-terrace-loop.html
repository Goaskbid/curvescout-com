Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Ubud

    Ubud is the place where Bali's rice paddies meet its art. Set in the hills at the island's centre, the town grew from a royal village into a centre for painters, dancers and carvers in the 1920s and 1930s, when European artists such as Walter Spies, a German, and Rudolf Bonnet, a Dutchman, settled here and encouraged local painters to explore new styles. The old royal palace, the Puri Saren, still hosts dance performances in the evenings, with gamelan music and elaborate costumes.

  2. 2. Tegalalang

    Tegalalang is the rice terrace that launched a thousand Instagram posts. Just north of Ubud, on the road towards Kintamani, the land falls away into a steep valley carved into terraces of rice, curving with the contours of the hill, each step bright green or gold or flooded with water depending on the season, with coconut palms rising between them. It is genuinely beautiful, especially in the early morning when the mist rises from the valley, and it is genuinely crowded by mid-morning, when the tour buses arrive and the swings and photo platforms fill up.

  3. 3. Bali Volcano and Rice Terrace Loop point 3

    Up on the rim of the Batur caldera, the air is cool, the light is sharp, and below you lies a landscape that looks like nowhere else on Bali. The caldera is a huge bowl, about ten kilometres across, formed by a massive eruption tens of thousands of years ago, and inside it rises Mount Batur, a younger volcano with black lava flows spreading down its flanks, and beside it Lake Batur, a crescent of deep blue water. Mount Batur is still active, with eruptions in the twentieth century, and the lava fields from those eruptions are still bare and black.

  4. 4. Bali Volcano and Rice Terrace Loop point 4

    Mount Agung is the holiest mountain on Bali, and from these slopes, south-east of the Batur caldera, it fills the horizon: a great cone rising to more than three thousand metres, its summit often wrapped in cloud. The Balinese orient their lives around it, with kaja, the direction of the mountain, considered pure and sacred, and the sea considered the opposite. Temples, houses and villages are laid out in relation to it, and the island's most important temple, Pura Besakih, sits on its flanks.

  5. 5. Near Duda

    The Sidemen valley is what many people imagine Bali was like before Ubud filled up. A river runs through a deep valley in the east of the island, and on either side rice terraces climb the slopes in neat steps, with coconut palms and banana trees between them and Mount Agung looming above at the head of the valley. Villages such as Duda, Selat and Sidemen sit along the road, small and quiet, with family compounds, temples and warungs serving simple meals. It is a peaceful, green place, where the sound of water in the irrigation channels is constant.

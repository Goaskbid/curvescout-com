Roadbook

  1. Cherkessk start

    Cherkessk is the capital of Karachay-Cherkessia, a republic in the North Caucasus, and it sits on the Kuban river where the steppe of the north begins to rise toward the mountains. It is a city of over a hundred thousand people with wide Soviet avenues, concrete housing blocks and a main square, and it has little of the old-world charm that the surrounding region offers. Most travellers see it as the last proper town before the mountains, and that is how to use it, as a place for supplies.

    The mountains are the reason to come. To the south, the Greater Caucasus rises in a long wall of snow peaks, with Mount Elbrus, the highest in Europe at 5,642 metres, in the neighbouring republic and visible from some spots on a clear day. Roads climb from here into deep valleys, toward Dombay, a ski and hiking centre among jagged peaks and glaciers, and Arkhyz, a quieter area of forest and lakes with a high-altitude observatory nearby. The Teberda river cuts through spruce-clad gorges, and the valleys are green and cool in summer.

    The people of the region are Karachays, Circassians, Russians, Abazins and Nogais, and the local food reflects a mix, with khychiny, flatbreads filled with cheese and potato, and shashlik, grilled skewered meat. Ayran, a salty yoghurt drink, is offered in cafes, and the mountain honey is excellent. Hospitality is a real tradition in the Caucasus, and a polite visitor may be invited for tea.

    The road up the valley is a good one for a rider. Smooth tarmac, wide bends and the sound of the river alongside give way to steeper sections with hairpins as the valley narrows. Livestock, cows and sheep, often wander onto the road, and local drivers overtake boldly, so keep your speed under control. Rockfalls and mudflows are possible after heavy rain and in spring, and snow can close high roads outside summer. The best season is June to September.

    Cherkessk has fuel stations and shops, with more in nearby Psyzh and Druzhba, but the road into the mountains offers fewer options, so fill the tank before you go. Border zones are close in this region, and it is wise to carry your passport and check local rules for permits.

  2. Teberda stop

    Teberda is a mountain resort in the western Caucasus where the air smells of spruce and cold stone, and the first thing that strikes you is how green and tidy a high mountain village can be. It lies in the valley of the Teberda river, in Karachay-Cherkessia, ringed by peaks of two and three thousand metres. The people are mainly Karachays, a Turkic mountain people with a long tradition of herding and hospitality, and the valley has been a health resort since the nineteenth century, when doctors began sending patients with lung complaints to breathe the clean air.

    The Teberda nature reserve, one of the oldest in the country, protects a huge area of forest, alpine meadow, glaciers and rocky ridges. Caucasian bison were reintroduced here after almost vanishing in the twentieth century, and chamois, ibex and brown bears live in the high valleys. The flora is rich, with rhododendron, pine and fir, and wildflowers cover the slopes in early summer. A small museum in the village displays specimens and photographs, and it is a good introduction before heading into the hills.

    Most visitors walk, and there are well-marked trails to waterfalls, to glacial lakes and up to viewpoints under the glaciers of Alibek, whose cold green stream rushes through a forested gorge. Even a short walk from the road brings you to places where the only noise is water and wind. Higher up, the glaciers hang from the northern faces, and the summer light on them is strong and bright.

    For riders, the road south from Teberda climbs toward Dombay through a long valley of spruce, with the river alongside, and the scenery grows more dramatic with every kilometre. The surface is generally decent but can have sections of potholes and rockfall debris, and in the spring and early summer snowmelt can wash across the road. Cattle and horses graze freely in the villages, and drivers on the narrow bends tend to hug the middle.

    Fuel stations are in the valley and in Karachayevsk, the larger town, so it makes sense to top up there before climbing. Teberda has small shops, cafés and guesthouses, and the local food includes khychiny, flatbreads filled with cheese and potato, and ayran, the sour drink made from yoghurt. Summer is cool and fresh, the best weather runs from June to September, and snow closes the high roads in winter.

  3. Dombay stop

    Dombay is a high mountain basin at about 1,600 metres, where the Teberda valley opens into a green bowl surrounded by some of the most dramatic peaks in the western Caucasus. The name is said to mean bison in the Karachay language, and local legend ties it to herds that once grazed the meadows. The peaks around it are serious: Dombay-Ulgen, a pyramid of rock and ice, tops 4,000 metres, and glaciers hang above forests of spruce and pine.

    In winter, Dombay is a ski resort, with a cable car that carries passengers high up the slope to terraces at over three thousand metres, where skiers and snowboarders descend runs above the treeline. In summer the same lifts take hikers and sightseers up to views over a sea of summits. A vast panorama extends from the top station, with the ridges of the main Caucasus range fading into the haze. The village itself is a straggle of wooden chalets, hotels and cafés, with a lively and relaxed atmosphere.

    The valley is full of walking routes. A short walk leads to a waterfall, another to a glacier lake with emerald water. The nearby Alibek and Gonachkhir valleys offer longer days among rocks and snow. The weather is changeable, and clouds can wrap the peaks in minutes, so carry a waterproof layer even in July.

    For the rider, the road from Teberda to Dombay is a steady climb along the river with views opening at every bend. The surface is mostly asphalt, with patches of potholes, and in the valley bottom the road narrows between trees. In the old days, the historic Military Sukhumi Road ran from here over the Klukhor Pass to the Black Sea, a rough mountain route built in the nineteenth century, though the modern border situation keeps the high crossing out of reach for travellers.

    There are small fuel stops at the foot of the valley, but not in the village itself, so fill up before climbing. You can eat at the cafés in Dombay, where grilled meat, dumplings and flatbreads fill the menus, and the tea is served with local honey. In early summer, the meadows bloom with wildflowers, and in September the larches and birches go yellow. By November, snow lies thickly and the ski season begins.

  4. Gonachkhir Gorge finish

    High in the Teberda valley, where the road from Karachayevsk up to Dombay crosses a narrow stretch of gorge, the Gonachkhir River comes in from the west, carrying meltwater from the glaciers along the main Caucasus ridge. Here the old Military Sukhumi Road, built in the nineteenth century to link the North Caucasus with the Black Sea coast, branches off up the Gonachkhir valley toward the Klukhor Pass and the border with Abkhazia. The pass, at roughly 2,780 metres, saw fierce fighting in 1942, when German mountain troops reached the passes and were driven back.

    The landscape is classic Caucasus. Steep slopes of spruce, fir and pine rise on both sides, with granite crags and snow-streaked peaks above, and the river roars over boulders far below the road. The Teberda Nature Reserve protects much of the surrounding land, home to bears, chamois, lynx and the West Caucasian tur, a wild mountain goat. In spring, the meadows are covered in wildflowers; in autumn, the beech and birch turn gold against the dark conifers, and the first snow dusts the peaks.

    As the finish of a ride up from Teberda toward Dombay, this is a dramatic end. The road climbs steadily through the valley, with tight bends, steep gradients, and some narrow sections where rockfalls and landslides are common after heavy rain. The surface is generally paved, but can be rough and broken in places, and you will share it with buses, taxis and minibuses ferrying tourists up and down. Beyond the junction, the road toward the Klukhor Pass becomes rougher and passes into a border zone, so check rules and permits before heading further.

    At the junction itself you will find little beyond a roadside stall. Teberda, a small resort town a short ride down the valley, has fuel, shops and simple cafes, and Karachayevsk, further down, has more. Dombay, a few kilometres up the valley, is a ski resort with hotels, restaurants and cable cars that run in summer too. Stop for a while at the junction, listen to the river, look up at the peaks, and feel the scale of the mountains around you; this is one of the great valleys of the Caucasus.

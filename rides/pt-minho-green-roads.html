  1. 1. Braga

    Braga is Portugal's most religious city, and also one of its oldest. It was founded by the Romans as Bracara Augusta in the first century BC, became a Christian bishopric in late antiquity, and has been a seat of the Church ever since. Its cathedral, begun in the eleventh century, is the oldest in the country and mixes Romanesque, Gothic and Baroque, and the city's skyline is a thicket of church towers. During Holy Week, its processions, with hooded penitents and torchlit marches, are among the most intense in Portugal.

  2. 2. Ponte de Lima

    Ponte de Lima is the sort of town that has been charming people for nine hundred years, and the old bridge across the Lima is the reason it exists. The Romans built a crossing here on the road between Braga and Astorga, and the long granite bridge you see today stands on those old foundations, with medieval arches added. The town's own legend says that Roman soldiers refused to cross the river because they thought it was the River of Forgetfulness, until their commander went across and called out each soldier by name. A charter was granted in the early twelfth century, which is why locals say theirs is the oldest town in Portugal.

  3. 3. Monção

    Monção sits on the Minho river, which here forms the border with Spain, and you can see the Galician town of Salvatierra de Miño from the riverbank, close enough to wave at. For centuries this frontier was a front line, and the old fortress walls of Monção, with their bastions, tell that story. The town's most famous tale dates from the fourteenth century, when it was besieged and food ran short. The besieged are said to have thrown bread at the enemy, who believed the town still had plenty of supplies and gave up. The heroine of the story is remembered as Deu-la-Deu Martins, and her name is attached to the town's heritage to this day.

  1. 1. Umea

    Umeå is the kind of city you do not expect at sixty-four degrees north: young, bookish and full of bicycles. About eighty thousand people live here on the Ume river, a number swelled by a big university and a hospital, and the average age is low enough that cafés and bars stay busy long after you think a northern town should have gone to bed. In summer the sun barely drops and the evenings feel endless; in midwinter it is the reverse, and the whole place leans on candles, saunas and coffee.

  2. 2. High Coast / Kvarken Archipelago

    The Kvarken is a narrow strait in the Gulf of Bothnia, between Finland and Sweden, and a place where you can watch the land rise. At the end of the last ice age, a sheet of ice more than a kilometre thick pressed down on Scandinavia, and when it melted the crust began to rebound, a process that is still going on. Here the uplift is about eight or nine millimetres a year, one of the fastest rates in the world, so the coastline changes within a human lifetime. New islands emerge, bays turn into lakes and then into meadows, and boat channels need to be redrawn. The Finnish Kvarken Archipelago was listed by UNESCO in 2006 and later joined with Sweden's High Coast, which shares the same story.

  3. 3. The Kvarken Archipelago - Zone A

    Zone A of the Kvarken is the outer world of islands, where the road gives way to boats. The Kvarken Archipelago is a maze of more than five thousand islands and islets, many of them barely a metre above the water, scattered across the shallow Gulf of Bothnia, and only a few are inhabited. The ones with year-round residents are reached by bridge or ferry, and live by fishing, small farming and, increasingly, by visitors who come for the quiet. The landscape here is slow and subtle, with horizons so low that sky and water seem to be the main features, and trees bent by the wind.

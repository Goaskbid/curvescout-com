Why this ride

A one-way ride of 244 km, Port Angeles to the finish - roughly 3 h 28 in the saddle. On the way it passes Lake Crescent and Forks. The highest point is about 1,200 m. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hurricane Ridge

    Hurricane Ridge is the high balcony of the Olympic Mountains, about sixteen miles of climbing from the sea at Port Angeles, reaching roughly 1,600 metres at the visitor centre. The road leaves the town, passes the national park entrance at Heart o' the Hills, and then winds up through Douglas fir and silver fir, with the forest thinning and the views opening out until the trees give way to alpine meadow. At the top, the whole range spreads out in front of you, jagged and white, with Mount Olympus sitting among the glaciers.

  2. 2. Port Angeles

    Port Angeles sits low on the northern edge of the Olympic Peninsula, facing the Strait of Juan de Fuca and, across the water, Vancouver Island. It is a working harbour town of some nineteen thousand people, with a long curved sandspit called Ediz Hook sheltering the port, a ferry that sails daily to Victoria in British Columbia, and a downtown of brick storefronts, bookshops and seafood spots. It is also the main gateway to Olympic National Park, which means it has what the rest of the peninsula lacks: choice.

  3. 3. Lake Crescent

    Lake Crescent is the kind of lake that makes you slow down without being asked. It fills a deep glacial trough in the north of Olympic National Park, roughly six hundred feet at its deepest, and the water is so clear and blue that on a calm day it looks lit from below. Steep forested ridges drop straight into it, with Storm King rising above the south shore. Port Angeles lies about twenty-seven kilometres east, and that is where you find fuel and a proper meal.

  4. 4. Forks

    Forks is a logging town on the western side of the Olympic Peninsula, a place of about three and a half thousand people, set on a plateau between the mountains and the Pacific. It sits in one of the wettest places in the lower forty-eight states, with rainfall that often reaches three metres a year, and the forest around it is a dripping, mossy world of Sitka spruce, western hemlock and Douglas fir. The town has lived off timber for most of its history, and a small museum near the visitor centre tells the story of the loggers, the fires and the mills.

  5. 5. Ruby Beach

    Ruby Beach is where the Olympic Peninsula puts its raw side on show. A short, steep trail leads down from the highway through spruce and fern to a wide shore of dark pebbles, strewn with enormous bleached driftwood logs, with tall sea stacks standing offshore and a creek spilling out across the stones. The name comes from the pinkish tint in the sand, said to be caused by tiny garnet crystals, and when the sun hits wet pebbles the colour is genuinely warm. In the distance, Destruction Island and its old lighthouse, built in the 1890s, sit on the horizon like a model.

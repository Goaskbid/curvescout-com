Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Yogyakarta

    Yogyakarta is the cultural heart of Java, and it still has a living sultan. The Kraton, the sultan's palace, stands at the centre of the city, surrounded by courtyards, pavilions and a walled quarter, and the ruling sultan serves as the governor of the special region, a unique arrangement in Indonesia. The city is also a university town, full of students, art, batik workshops and a famous street, Malioboro, that fills with food stalls and horse-drawn carts in the evening.

  2. 2. Prambanan Temple Compounds

    Prambanan is the largest Hindu temple complex in Indonesia, a cluster of soaring stone towers on the plain between Yogyakarta and Solo, with the volcano Merapi brooding to the north. The central shrines were built in the ninth century and dedicated to the Trimurti: Shiva in the tallest tower, about forty-seven metres high, flanked by Brahma and Vishnu, with smaller temples for their mounts facing them. The walls are covered with reliefs of the Ramayana, carved so that you read the story as you walk clockwise around the galleries.

  4. 4. Mendut Temple

    Mendut is the small, elegant temple that most visitors pass on their way to Borobudur, and that is a mistake. Built in the early ninth century under the Sailendra dynasty, it stands about three kilometres east of the great monument, on a straight line that also passes through the tiny Pawon temple, and scholars believe the three were connected in a ritual sequence. Each year, at the Buddhist festival of Waisak, monks walk in procession from Mendut through Pawon to Borobudur, carrying candles and chanting.

  5. 5. Pawon Temple

    Pawon is the small one, and I have a soft spot for it. While Borobudur is huge and Mendut grand, Pawon is a compact stone temple about two kilometres from Borobudur, set in a quiet garden among village houses. It looks like a miniature tower, with a carved doorway, a tiered roof and relief carvings of trees and mythical beings on the outer walls, and inside, the chamber is almost empty. Scholars still debate what it was for, with one idea being that it was a place where pilgrims purified themselves before continuing to the larger temple, and another that it was linked to a royal figure.

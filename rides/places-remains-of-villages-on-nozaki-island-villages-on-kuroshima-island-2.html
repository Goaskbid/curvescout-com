Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Remains of Villages on Nozaki Island

    Nozaki is a long, steep island in the Goto archipelago, off the western coast of Kyushu, and almost nobody lives there now. In the nineteenth century, Christians who had kept their faith secretly for over two hundred years of persecution under the Tokugawa shogunate settled on the island, farming its steep slopes alongside the community that served a Shinto shrine. When the ban on Christianity was lifted in 1873, they built a church, and the brick church of Nokubi, completed in 1908, still stands on a hill above the sea. The island and its abandoned villages are a piece of the World Heritage property for Hidden Christian sites in the Nagasaki region.

  2. 2. Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Reg

    Far west of the Japanese mainland, the Goto Islands trail out into the East China Sea in a scatter of volcanic hills, cliffs and quiet harbours, and on them stand some of the most moving small churches in Asia. The Hidden Christian Sites of the Nagasaki Region belong to the same World Heritage listing as Oura Cathedral, but this corner of it feels very different. Here the faith was kept alive in remote farming and fishing villages, to which Christian families moved in the late eighteenth century, partly because the authorities of the Goto domain wanted settlers to open up poor land.

  3. 3. Villages on Kuroshima Island

    For more than two centuries after Christianity was banned in Japan in the early seventeenth century, small communities of believers kept their faith in secret, disguising prayers as Buddhist chants and images of the Virgin Mary as Buddhist figures. Kuroshima, a small island off the coast of Sasebo in Nagasaki Prefecture, was one of the places where these hidden Christians lived and farmed, and when the ban was lifted in the 1870s they emerged openly. Their villages are part of the UNESCO listing of Hidden Christian sites in the Nagasaki region, listed in 2018.

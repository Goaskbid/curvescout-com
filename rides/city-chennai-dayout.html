Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Chennai

    Chennai was Madras until 1996, and it is a place where tradition and technology sit side by side. The city grew up around Fort St George, which the East India Company began building in 1644, and the fort still stands near the seafront, now home to the state government. A short distance south, in the old quarter of Mylapore, the Kapaleeshwarar Temple rises in a colourful tower of carved gods and animals, and the streets around it are lined with shops selling jasmine, brass lamps and sweets.

  2. 2. Mukunda Nayanar Temple

    Mahabalipuram, the Seven Pagodas of old sailors' charts, was a port city of the Pallava kings, who ruled this coast from their capital at Kanchipuram in the seventh and eighth centuries. Their sculptors worked in granite, carving temples out of the living rock and building others from cut stone, and the town is now full of the results. The Mukunda Nayanar Temple is one of the smaller and quieter of them, a modest stone shrine of the eighth century dedicated to Shiva, standing a little apart from the crowds in the north-west of the old town.

  3. 3. Pidari Ratha / Valian Kuttai Ratha

    Cut from a single boulder, the Pidari Ratha and its neighbour the Valayankuttai Ratha belong to the earliest generation of monuments at Mahabalipuram. The Pallava sculptors who made them in the seventh century worked from the top down, starting at the roof and carving inwards and downwards through the rock, a method that left no room for mistakes. The result is a small, compact temple form, square and neat, with a pyramidal roof, and some of the details were never finished, so you can still see where the chisels stopped.

  4. 4. main complex of Mahabalipuram

    The main complex at Mahabalipuram is a stone anthology of Pallava art, set between a low granite hill and the Bay of Bengal. Its centrepiece is the Shore Temple, built in the early eighth century under King Rajasimha, a pair of slim, tapering towers of weathered granite that stand almost at the water's edge. Sea winds have rounded the carvings, and in the morning light the temple looks like a sandcastle that has been left to harden for thirteen centuries. It survived the tsunami of 2004, when the sea drew back and exposed more ancient structures on the sand.

  1. 1. Badlands Loop State Scenic Byway point 1

    The prairie lies to you here. For mile after mile around Wall, South Dakota, the land rolls flat and grassy, with wheat fields, cattle and the long, straight line of Interstate 90, and nothing suggests that a few kilometres to the south the ground is about to fall away into a maze of eroded spires and canyons.

  2. 2. Badlands Loop State Scenic Byway point 2

    From the Pinnacles Overlook, at the western end of the Badlands Loop, the earth looks as if it has been clawed open. Below the viewpoint, ridges and spires of soft rock drop steeply into a wide basin, their sides scored with gullies and streaked with bands of colour. The rock layers are sediments laid down over millions of years by ancient seas, rivers and volcanic ash falls from eruptions far to the west, and the badlands are eroding at a rate of about an inch a year, which in geological terms is lightning fast.

  3. 3. Badlands Loop State Scenic Byway point 3

    Halfway along the Badlands Loop the colours change. Around the Yellow Mounds Overlook the grey and buff spires give way to low, rounded hills of bright yellow and ochre, some streaked with red and purple, looking more like a painted desert than a prairie. They are a fossil soil, formed when the dark shale of an old sea floor, the oldest rock in the park, lay exposed to the weather after the water retreated, and they glow in the late afternoon sun.

  4. 4. Badlands Loop State Scenic Byway point 4

    At Cedar Pass, the eastern end of the Badlands Loop, the road drops through a gap in the Wall in a series of tight curves, from the high prairie down to the White River valley below. It is the most dramatic piece of riding on the whole loop, with sharp spires and cliffs rising on both sides and wide views out over the lower land to the south. The Ben Reifel Visitor Center sits at the bottom of the pass, along with a lodge, a campground and an amphitheatre for evening ranger talks.

  5. 5. Kadoka

    Kadoka is a village of fewer than a thousand people on the high plains of South Dakota, a quiet place of wide streets and grain elevators, and the name is a Lakota word often translated as hole in the wall, which refers to a gap in the Badlands wall nearby. The town sits on the interstate, at the south-east end of the long arc of the Badlands, and it is a handy base for those who want to see the park without paying resort prices.

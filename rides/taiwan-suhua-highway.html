Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Su ao

    Su'ao is a fishing port on the north-east coast of Taiwan, a working harbour with sheds, trawlers and the smell of diesel and salt, and it marks the northern gate of one of the island's dramatic coastal roads. The mountains of the Central range drop almost directly into the Pacific here, leaving very little flat land, and the town is squeezed into a narrow lowland between steep green slopes and a bay full of boats. It is not a pretty place, but it is honest, and the seafood is excellent.

  2. 2. Nanfangao

    Nanfang'ao is a fishing harbour tucked into a bay on the north-east coast of Taiwan, and it is still more working port than tourist attraction. Boats of all sizes crowd the quay, nets and floats are heaped on the dock, and the air smells of salt, diesel and frying. Behind it the coast mountains rise steeply, and the town climbs the hillside in a jumble of narrow lanes and concrete houses with tiled roofs, while the harbour wall protects the bay from the Pacific swell.

  3. 3. Qingshui Cliffs

    The Qingshui Cliffs are where Taiwan's eastern mountains simply stop and the Pacific begins. The rock face rises almost a kilometre straight out of the sea, a wall of grey and white marble and gneiss streaked with vegetation, and the road runs along its flank, cut into the cliff, with a drop of hundreds of metres on one side. The water below is a deep, intense blue, and when the light hits it right, the whole scene seems almost unreal.

  4. 4. Hualien

    Hualien lies on Taiwan's wild east coast, squeezed between the Pacific and a wall of mountains that rise to over three thousand metres within a few dozen kilometres of the sea. The city is relaxed, with a lively night market, sea-view cafes and the slightly weathered air of a place that is regularly shaken by earthquakes and typhoons. It is also the gateway to Taroko Gorge, where the Liwu river has cut a deep canyon through marble.

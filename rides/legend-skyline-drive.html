Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Front Royal

    Front Royal is the northern gateway to Skyline Drive, a small Virginia town of about fifteen thousand people in the Shenandoah Valley, where the north and south forks of the Shenandoah River meet. The Blue Ridge rises to the east, a long wall of forest that goes violet at dusk, and the main street has a mix of old brick buildings, antique shops, diners and a town green with a gazebo. It is a convenient and friendly place to prepare for the mountains.

  2. 2. Near Front Royal

    Mile zero of Skyline Drive is just outside Front Royal, and from here the road climbs onto the crest of the Blue Ridge and stays there for a hundred and five miles. This northern end is where you pay at the entrance station, pick up a map, check the speed limit, which is thirty-five miles per hour for the whole length of the drive, and accept that you are here for the views, not the pace. The first few miles climb quickly through oak and hickory forest to the first overlooks, with the Shenandoah Valley spreading out below to the west and the Piedmont rolling away to the east.

  3. 3. Near Luray

    Skyland is where Skyline Drive reaches its highest point, at about 3,680 feet, and also where the story of the park really began. Long before there was a national park, a man named George Freeman Pollock ran a mountain resort here, with cabins, a dining hall and entertainments for city folk escaping the summer heat of Washington, and he became one of the most vocal campaigners for creating a national park in the Blue Ridge. Today Skyland is a lodge with rooms, a restaurant and a taproom, and its dining room windows look straight out over the Shenandoah Valley.

  4. 4. Near Stanley

    Big Meadows is the largest open space on Skyline Drive, a broad, grassy clearing on top of the Blue Ridge at around 3,500 feet, and on a summer evening it fills with deer, grazing quietly in the long grass while families watch from the edges. It is not a natural meadow in the strict sense: it has been kept open for centuries, first by Native Americans, who burned it to attract game, then by settlers who grazed cattle on it, and today the park mows and burns it to keep the forest from taking over. Wild blueberries grow here in late summer, and the meadow is full of wildflowers in spring and early summer.

  5. 5. Near Elkton

    Swift Run Gap is where US 33 crosses the Blue Ridge, and where Skyline Drive passes over it on a bridge at about 720 metres, or 2,365 feet. It is one of four entrance stations to the park, and it is a natural break point, with the road dropping west to Elkton and the Shenandoah Valley, and east to Stanardsville and the Virginia Piedmont. The gap itself is a low saddle in the ridge, forested with oak, hickory and maple, and the drive on either side climbs back up to the high ridges in long, smooth curves.

  6. 6. Near Waynesboro

    Rockfish Gap is a low saddle in the Blue Ridge above Waynesboro, Virginia, and it is one of the great meeting points of American motorcycling. Here, on the crest of the ridge, the Skyline Drive ends its 105-mile run south through Shenandoah National Park, and the Blue Ridge Parkway begins its 469-mile journey south to the Great Smoky Mountains. Interstate 64 crosses the gap too, along with the Appalachian Trail, and on a summer weekend you will see riders from every state stopping for photos at the milepost zero sign.

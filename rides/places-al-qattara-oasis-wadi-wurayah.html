Why this ride

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Al Qattara Oasis

    Qattara is one of the old oases of Al Ain, a cluster of palm gardens that once fed a handful of mudbrick houses and towers. The area has been inhabited for thousands of years, and the whole group of oases is recognised by UNESCO for the way people have managed water in a place where it hardly rains. At Qattara you can still see the old layout: shaded lanes between walls, small farm plots under the palms, and a fort that was built to protect the wells and the people who relied on them.

  2. 2. Faya Palaeolandscape

    Sharjah's interior is a world of red dunes, gravel plains and jagged limestone ridges, and in the middle of it, near the town of Mleiha, the hill of Jebel Faya holds one of the most important records of early human presence outside Africa. Excavations in rock shelters at the foot of the mountain have revealed stone tools spanning many tens of thousands of years, suggesting that early modern humans were present in Arabia far earlier than once thought, during periods when the climate was wetter and the desert greener. The Faya Palaeolandscape was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 2025, recognising its significance for understanding human dispersal out of Africa.

  3. 3. Wadi Wurayah

    Wadi Wurayah lies in the Hajar mountains on the east coast of the Emirates, in the narrow strip of Fujairah that reaches the Gulf of Oman, and it is one of the few places in the country where water stays on the surface all year. A stream runs through the gorge, feeding pools and small falls beneath tall, folded cliffs of limestone and ophiolite, rock that was once part of the ocean floor and was pushed up onto the land when plates collided. It was the first mountain protected area in the Emirates and is now a national park. Fish live in the pools, and the rarer animals include the Arabian tahr, a shy mountain goat that few visitors ever see.

Why this ride

245 km from Luoyang to Luoyang, about 4 h 13 of riding. It goes by way of Longmen Grottoes, Architectural Complex of Shaolin Temple, Observatory and back through Architectural Complex of Shaolin Temple. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. No.160 Site of Hanjia Granary

    Luoyang was the capital of thirteen dynasties, and its history runs through the ground like a seam of coal. On the edge of the modern city, the Hanjia Granary is one of the biggest ancient grain stores ever found. It was built in the Sui dynasty, in the early seventh century, and used through the Tang, and the site has hundreds of underground storage pits, each one large enough to hold hundreds of tonnes of grain. UNESCO listed it as part of the Silk Roads in 2014.

  2. 2. Longmen Grottoes

    South of Luoyang, the river Yi cuts through a narrow gap between two limestone hills, and for about a kilometre the cliffs on both sides are honeycombed with caves, niches and carvings. Over around four centuries, starting in the late fifth century under the Northern Wei dynasty and continuing through the Tang, Buddhist sculptors carved tens of thousands of statues into the rock, from tiny figures a few centimetres high to colossal Buddhas. The Longmen Grottoes were listed by UNESCO in 2000 and are among the finest examples of Chinese Buddhist art.

  3. 3. Architectural Complex of Shaolin Temple

    Ask most people where kung fu comes from and they will name this temple, even if they have no idea where it is. The Shaolin Temple stands at the foot of Mount Song in Henan province, central China, near the city of Dengfeng. It was founded at the end of the fifth century for an Indian monk, and tradition says that Bodhidharma, the legendary founder of Chan, or Zen, Buddhism, meditated in a cave on the mountain above for nine years. The monks' martial arts became famous over the centuries, and today Shaolin is as much a brand as a monastery.

  4. 4. Observatory

    The oldest surviving observatory in China sits in an unassuming spot in Dengfeng, in Henan, and it makes astronomy look more like architecture. The Gaocheng Astronomical Observatory was built in 1276 under the Yuan dynasty by the astronomer Guo Shoujing, who designed a stout stone tower about 9 metres high with a long stone scale running north from its base. The tower was a giant gnomon: the sun cast a shadow along the scale, and by measuring its length at noon through the year, the astronomers could fix the solstices and the length of the year with remarkable accuracy. The calendar that Guo helped create was in use for several centuries.

Why this ride

The stops in between: Mount Sanqingshan National Park, Gaoling Porcelain Clay Mining Site and back through Mount Sanqingshan National Park. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Jianglangshan

    Jianglangshan looks as though somebody took a giant knife to a block of red sandstone and left three slices standing. The Three Sisters, tall, narrow slabs of rock rising well over three hundred metres above the valley, are the icon of the site, and the cliffs behind them, steep and layered in warm reds and browns, are typical of the landform called Danxia. The word means rosy clouds, and the landscape is a kind of red-bed desert eroded by wind and rain into peaks, gorges and walls. UNESCO listed a group of Danxia sites in south China in 2010, and Jianglangshan, in Zhejiang, is one of the six.

  2. 2. Mount Sanqingshan National Park

    Sanqingshan is a mountain where granite has been carved into figures. Tall pillars and spires of grey rock rise out of a sea of cloud, shaped by wind and water into forms that Chinese visitors have named for centuries: the Goddess, a pillar that looks like a woman with a tall coiffure, the Python, a slender tower that leans out over the valley. The peaks reach to about 1,800 metres, the highest, Yujingfeng, just under 1,820, and the mist that gathers in the valleys turns the whole range into a landscape out of a classical painting.

  3. 3. Gaoling Porcelain Clay Mining Site

    Few words travelled the world quite like kaolin. It comes from Gaoling, which means High Ridge, a hill village in Jiangxi province where a pure white clay was dug for centuries and sent to the porcelain kilns of Jingdezhen. French missionaries in the eighteenth century took samples of this clay back to Europe, and the Chinese place name became the English and French word for the key ingredient in porcelain. So this ordinary-looking hillside is where one of the most famous materials in the world got its name.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Glasgow

    Glasgow is a Victorian city that learned to like itself again. In the nineteenth century it was the second city of the British Empire, building ships and locomotives on the Clyde, and the grand sandstone buildings of the Merchant City and the West End are the evidence. In the twentieth century, as the shipyards declined, the city went through hard times, but today it has a thriving music and arts scene, and a reputation for warmth and humour that people from elsewhere often find startling.

  2. 2. New Lanark

    Coming down the steep, wooded road from Lanark into the gorge of the Clyde, you see New Lanark all at once: a cluster of tall, plain sandstone mill buildings and tenements packed onto the riverbank at the bottom of the valley, with the water rushing past. It is one of the most remarkable industrial villages in Europe, founded in 1786 by David Dale and the inventor Richard Arkwright to harness the river for cotton spinning, and later turned into a social experiment by Dale's son-in-law, Robert Owen. It is a World Heritage site.

  4. 4. The Forth Bridge

    When the Forth Bridge opened in 1890, it was the longest cantilever bridge in the world, and it still looks like nothing else: three enormous diamond-shaped steel towers, joined by long arms, striding across the Firth of Forth to carry the railway between Edinburgh and Fife. Its engineers, Sir John Fowler and Sir Benjamin Baker, designed it after the collapse of the Tay Bridge in 1879 had shaken public faith in iron and steel, and they overbuilt deliberately, with great tubes of steel that made the structure look as strong as it was. The bridge is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 2015.

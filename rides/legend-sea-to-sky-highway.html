Why this ride

Vancouver to Whistler along Howe Sound. A one-way ride of 101 km, West End to Whistler - roughly 1 h 50 in the saddle. It goes by way of West Vancouver, Lions Bay and Squamish. The highest point is about 1,400 m. The line favours views over speed.

Scenic road chapters

Sea-to-Sky Highway

Expect company on a summer weekend. Early is better than fast. For 15 km this is Sea-to-Sky Highway, a road with a name of its own and a reason for it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. West End

    The Lions Gate Bridge, a green suspension bridge spanning the First Narrows from Stanley Park to the North Shore, was built in the 1930s by the Guinness brewing family, who had bought land on the slopes of West Vancouver and needed a way to get buyers there. It is the gateway to the Sea-to-Sky Highway, and the West End, where this ride begins, lies at its southern end, a dense, leafy neighbourhood of apartment towers, old houses and tree-lined streets between downtown Vancouver and Stanley Park.

  3. 3. Near Lions Bay

    Above Lions Bay stand the two granite peaks that gave the village its name, The Lions, visible from much of Vancouver and known to the Squamish people as the Twin Sisters. The village itself clings to steep slopes above Howe Sound, a scatter of houses among cedars and firs, with a small beach and a marina far below. It is a residential community rather than a stop with services, but the setting is spectacular.

  4. 4. Squamish

    Squamish sits at the head of Howe Sound, where the fjord ends against a wall of granite, and the first thing it shows you is the Stawamus Chief, a monolith of rock rising some seven hundred metres straight out of the forest. It is one of the largest free-standing granite faces in the world, and on any good day the white specks on its walls are climbers, which has turned this small town into a minor capital of the sport. Beside it Shannon Falls drops in a long white thread, easily seen from the highway.

  5. 5. Whistler

    Whistler began as an idea in the 1960s, when a group of Vancouver businessmen decided that a mountain north of the city might host an Olympic ski area, and it ended as one of the largest winter resorts in North America. The village itself is purpose built, a pedestrian maze of shops, bars and hotels at the foot of two mountains, Whistler and Blackcomb, joined by a gondola called Peak 2 Peak that spans a huge gap between the summits. The name comes from the hoary marmots that whistle from the rockfalls, and the surrounding land is the shared territory of the Squamish and Lil'wat peoples, whose cultural centre in the village is worth an hour.

Why this ride

A 73 km round trip from Kashihara - roughly 1 h 16 in the saddle. The stops in between: Horyu-ji Area, Tôdai-ji and back through Horyu-ji Area. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Minegazuka Kofun

    East of the great ranges, the Osaka plain narrows toward the Yamato river, and this is where the old tomb country of the Furuichi group begins. Minegazuka is one of the burial mounds scattered through the districts of Habikino, Fujiidera and Kashiwara, all of them tucked into a landscape that is now mostly suburbs, vineyards and small factories. These monuments belong to the same fifth-century family of tombs as the ones around Sakai, and together the two clusters make up a single World Heritage property. The largest mound in this neighbourhood, close by in Habikino, is the tomb traditionally given to the emperor Ojin, one of the largest mounds in the country by volume.

  2. 2. Horyu-ji Area

    The buildings at Hōryū-ji are around thirteen hundred years old, and some of them are among the oldest wooden structures anywhere in the world. Founded in the early seventh century by Prince Shōtoku, a regent remembered as a champion of Buddhism and a founding figure of the Japanese state, the temple was rebuilt after a fire later in the same century, and parts of that rebuilding still stand. In 1993 it became one of the first sites in Japan to be listed by UNESCO, as the Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area.

  3. 3. Tôdai-ji

    Nothing quite prepares you for the scale of Tōdai-ji. You walk through the great Nandaimon gate, past two fierce wooden guardian kings carved in the thirteenth century by Unkei, Kaikei and their workshop, and there it is: the Daibutsuden, the Great Buddha Hall, for centuries the largest wooden building in the world, even though the current structure, rebuilt in the early eighteenth century, is smaller than the original. Inside sits the Daibutsu, a bronze Buddha some fifteen metres tall, commissioned by Emperor Shōmu and consecrated in 752 with a ceremony that drew monks from as far away as India. The temple is part of the UNESCO listing of Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Kabale

    Kabale is a hill town at about nineteen hundred metres in the far south-west of Uganda, and the first thing you notice is how cool it is. After the heat of Kampala and the lowlands, evenings here call for a jumper. The surrounding countryside, known as Kigezi, is a tight patchwork of terraces, so steep and so carefully cultivated that early colonial visitors compared it to Switzerland, and the nickname has stuck. Potatoes, sorghum, beans and cabbages are grown on slopes that look too steep to stand on.

  2. 2. Lake Bunyonyi

    Bunyonyi means place of little birds, and the name is accurate: kingfishers, herons and cormorants work the water, and the hills around ring with birdsong in the early morning. The lake lies in the Kigezi highlands of south-western Uganda, close to the Rwandan border, at nearly two thousand metres, and it is one of the deepest lakes in Africa. Its shore is a tangle of narrow arms and bays, with some two dozen islands scattered across the surface, and green terraced hills drop right down to the water on every side.

  3. 3. Kisoro

    Kisoro sits in the far south-west corner of Uganda, in a pocket between Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo, and the first thing you notice is the row of volcanoes. Muhabura, Gahinga and Sabinyo stand on the skyline like a set of upturned bowls, their slopes green and their summits often hidden in cloud. They are part of the Virunga range, a chain of volcanoes that straddles three countries, and the town sits at the foot of them, on a high plateau where the air is cool and the land is cultivated to the last metre.

  4. 4. Bwindi

    Bwindi Impenetrable Forest earns its name. It is a dense, ancient rainforest draped over steep ridges and deep ravines in south-western Uganda, rising from about a thousand metres to over two thousand, and it is one of the oldest and richest forests in Africa, a refuge that survived the ice ages when other forests shrank. Mist hangs in the valleys until mid-morning, and the sound is a constant chorus of birds, insects and distant water. The park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in the 1990s, and it holds a little under half of all the mountain gorillas left in the world.

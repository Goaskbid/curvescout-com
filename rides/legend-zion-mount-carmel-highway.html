  1. 1. Zion-Mount Carmel Highway point 1

    Springdale lies at the mouth of Zion Canyon, and the effect of arriving is rather like walking into a cathedral. Red and cream cliffs rise on every side, thousands of feet high, with the Virgin River winding below them and cottonwoods lining its banks. The village itself is small, a single main street running along the base of the walls, with cafes, galleries, motels and outfitters, and a grand view of the Watchman, a peak that stands guard over the entrance.

  2. 2. Zion-Mount Carmel Highway point 2

    At Canyon Junction the traffic splits, and the choice reveals how Zion works. Turn left, and you enter the Zion Canyon Scenic Drive, a six-mile dead-end road that runs up the canyon floor to the Temple of Sinawava. In most of the year, private vehicles are not allowed on it, and the park runs a free shuttle instead. Turn right, and you are on the Zion-Mount Carmel Highway, a different road in every sense, built to lift travellers out of the canyon and across the plateau to the east.

  3. 3. Zion-Mount Carmel Highway point 3

    Six switchbacks stack up the side of Pine Creek canyon here, and for a few minutes the road feels more like an alpine pass than a desert highway. The Great Arch of Zion, a huge scoop in the cliff face, sits above the road, and the sheer white face of the East Temple rises across the canyon.

  4. 4. Zion-Mount Carmel Highway point 4

    Checkerboard Mesa looks like something a giant has scratched into a loaf of bread. The great dome of cream Navajo sandstone stands just beside the road east of the tunnel, patterned with a grid of vertical and horizontal cracks that form a regular chessboard of blocks. The vertical lines are the remains of the original dune layers, and the horizontal ones come from weathering, as water seeps into the rock and freezes and thaws. The result is one of the best-known formations in the park, and one of the easiest to photograph from the roadside.

  5. 5. Zion-Mount Carmel Highway point 5

    Mount Carmel Junction is not much of a place, and that is part of its charm. A handful of buildings stand at the meeting of Utah 9 and US 89, on a high sagebrush plateau at around 5,200 feet, with the pale cliffs of the Vermilion range to the south and the long, low line of the Moquith Mountains beyond. The settlement was founded by Mormon families in the 1870s, as part of the United Order, an experiment in communal living, and the name comes from the biblical mountain.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. San Luca

    The Sanctuary of the Madonna di San Luca sits on the Colle della Guardia, a green hill above Bologna, and what makes it special is the way you reach it. A covered arcade, nearly four kilometres long and with some six hundred and sixty arches, climbs from the edge of the city to the church, built between 1674 and 1793 so pilgrims could carry an icon of the Virgin down into town and back in the rain. It is the longest portico in the world, and it forms part of the arcades of Bologna that UNESCO listed in 2021. The icon, which tradition says was painted by Luke the Evangelist, is taken in procession to the cathedral each May.

  2. 2. Vena del Gesso Romagnola - M.te Penzola

    The Santerno valley climbs south from Imola into the Apennines toward Tuscany, and about fifteen kilometres up, the river cuts through a ridge of shining grey-white rock. This is the western end of the Vena del Gesso Romagnola, the gypsum vein that runs across this part of Romagna, and Monte Penzola rises above the village of Borgo Tossignano, with Fontanelice and Casalfiumanese nearby.

  3. 3. Villa de Cafaggiolo

    In the green bowl of the Mugello, north of Florence, the Medici family kept a house long before they ruled the city. Cafaggiolo was an old family property, and around the middle of the fifteenth century Cosimo the Elder had the architect Michelozzo transform it into a fortified country villa, with battlements, a tower and a moat, a cross between a castle and a farmhouse. Lorenzo the Magnificent spent time here as a boy, and the villa became a favourite retreat for hunting and escaping the plague in the city. It is one of the Medici Villas and Gardens in Tuscany listed by UNESCO, and visits depend on current restoration and opening arrangements, so check before you arrive.

  4. 4. Historic Centre of Florence

    Florence is the kind of city where the problem is not finding something to look at but deciding when to stop. The historic centre sits on a bend of the Arno, a compact grid of Roman origin with the Duomo at its middle, Brunelleschi's dome rising over the roofs, the Palazzo Vecchio at the civic heart and the Ponte Vecchio, the only bridge the retreating Germans spared in 1944. The Medici bankers who ran the place for much of the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries paid for most of what you are about to see, and they were rather good at it.

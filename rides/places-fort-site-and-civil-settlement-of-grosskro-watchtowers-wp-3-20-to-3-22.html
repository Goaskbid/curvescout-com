Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Fort site and civil settlement of Grosskro

    Großkrotzenburg sits at a turning point of the Roman frontier. Here, on the Main east of Frankfurt, the river border that ran north from Miltenberg came to an end, and the Limes turned away from the water to head north across the Wetterau toward the Taunus. A fort guarded the spot, with a civilian settlement, and parts of its walls and gate can still be traced in the old town, where the fort's outline is marked in the streets. The site belongs to the UNESCO Frontiers of the Roman Empire, and the local museum displays finds from the fort and the vicus.

  2. 2. Fortsite and civil settlement of Ober-Flor

    The Wetterau is a broad, fertile basin north of Frankfurt, a land of rich loess soils, wheat fields, orchards and old villages, and the Romans wanted it badly. Their frontier, the Upper Germanic-Raetian Limes, bulged out here to take in the whole basin, looping around it in a wide arc. At Ober-Florstadt stood a fort with a civilian settlement beside it, where soldiers' families, traders and craftsmen lived. Today little is visible, but the site is part of the World Heritage frontier, and information boards help to picture the scene.

  3. 3. Fort site and civil settlement of Saalburg

    If you want to know what a Roman frontier fort actually looked like, this is the place. The Saalburg, on the ridge of the Taunus above Bad Homburg, is the only Limes fort in Germany rebuilt in full: walls, gates, towers, granary and the headquarters building, all standing again on their original foundations. The reconstruction was ordered by Kaiser Wilhelm II around 1900, who liked his Romans grand and imperial, and while archaeologists today debate some details, walking through the main gate is still a jolt.

  4. 4. Limessection between WP 4/37 and 4/39

    The Wetterau is a broad, fertile basin north of Frankfurt, and the Limes crosses it on its way from the Taunus hills towards the Main. Near Butzbach, the frontier runs through farmland and low wooded ridges, with a line of watchtowers, and the stretch between towers 4/37 and 4/39 is a quiet piece of it. The Saalburg, the reconstructed Roman fort in the Taunus, lies a short distance to the south, and it makes the best starting point for understanding what the frontier looked like.

  5. 5. Watchtowers WP 3/20 to 3/22

    The Taunus is the low, wooded range north of Frankfurt, and the Romans chose it as the place to draw their line. The Limes crossed the ridge at a height that gave good views over both sides, and watchtowers placed close together made it possible to send signals by fire and smoke from tower to tower. The numbers, such as WP 3/20, are from the survey carried out by German archaeologists at the end of the nineteenth century, and they refer to the third section of the line. The Upper Germanic-Raetian Limes was listed by UNESCO in 2005.

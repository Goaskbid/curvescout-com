  1. 1. Joshua Tree

    The town of Joshua Tree is a scatter of low buildings, junk-art yards, cafés and rental cabins along Highway 62, with the north entrance road to the national park running south out of it into the boulders. It sits on the high desert at around 850 metres, well above the heat of Palm Springs on the other side of the mountains, and it has become a magnet for musicians, artists, climbers and people looking to get away from Los Angeles for a weekend. The vibe is part hippie, part cowboy, part art school.

  2. 2. Joshua Tree Park Boulevard point 2

    Half an hour into the park, Park Boulevard winds into the heart of the boulder country, and the landscape becomes almost absurd. Piles of rounded granite rocks, some the size of houses, rise out of the desert floor among the Joshua trees, stacked and balanced as if a giant had been playing with them.

  3. 3. Joshua Tree Park Boulevard point 3

    Keys View is the high balcony of Joshua Tree National Park. A side road climbs south from Park Boulevard for about ten kilometres to a viewpoint on the crest of the Little San Bernardino Mountains at around 1,580 metres, and from the edge you look out over the whole of the Coachella Valley. Below you is Palm Springs and its satellite towns, beyond them the Salton Sea glittering in the haze, and across the valley Mount San Jacinto and Mount San Gorgonio rise to over 3,000 metres, often capped with snow in winter.

  4. 4. Joshua Tree Park Boulevard point 4

    The Cholla Cactus Garden appears out of nowhere on Pinto Basin Road, a dense patch of thousands of teddy-bear cholla cacti covering a gentle slope. From a distance they look soft and fuzzy, glowing silver in the sunlight, but up close their spines are sharp, barbed and notoriously eager to attach themselves to anything that brushes past. A short loop trail winds through the garden, and the rule is simple: look, but do not touch, and watch where you put your feet.

  5. 5. Joshua Tree Park Boulevard point 5

    Cottonwood Spring is the southern gate of Joshua Tree, and it feels like a different park. At around 900 metres, it is lower and hotter than the northern section, with a small visitor centre, a campground and a palm oasis. A short walk from the parking area leads to a grove of California fan palms and cottonwoods around a spring that has been used by people for thousands of years. The Cahuilla people camped here, and in the late nineteenth century miners and teamsters used the water for their animals and for processing gold ore. The ruins of an old arrastra, a primitive ore-crushing mill, can still be seen nearby.

Roadbook

  1. Murmansk start

    Murmansk is the largest city north of the Arctic Circle, a port of over three hundred thousand people on the eastern shore of Kola Bay, and it owes its existence to the sea. The water stays open all year, kept free of ice by a final tongue of the Gulf Stream, which made this a vital harbour during the First World War, when the city was founded in 1916, and during the Second, when Allied convoys brought supplies here from Britain and America. The city was heavily bombed, and its character today is dominated by blocks of Soviet housing, stern monuments and ships tied up along the waterfront.

    The most striking monument is Alyosha, a huge concrete soldier standing on a hill above the bay, raised in 1974 to honour the defenders of the Arctic. The city has the atomic icebreaker Lenin, the world's first nuclear-powered surface ship, which is now a museum moored at the harbour. In midwinter the sun does not rise for about six weeks, and the city lives under the glow of streetlights, auroras sometimes flickering above them. In summer, the sun does not set for several weeks, and the long evenings are bright and cool.

    For a rider, Murmansk is the start of the Arctic coast. The road to Teriberka, on the Barents Sea, is about a hundred and twenty kilometres of tundra, rock and water, with low birch scrub, reindeer and cold light. The coast is bare and wind-beaten, with surf crashing on dark rocks, and the old fishing village has a ship graveyard that has become well known from films and photographs. The surface is mostly good, though sections can have frost damage, and weather is a serious matter, with sudden fog, snow even in June and strong wind. The season for riding is short, roughly June to early September.

    Fuel is available in the city and in the nearby suburbs, such as Roslyakovo, and you should fill the tank before leaving, since services on the coast road are scarce. Warm clothing is essential, even in summer, and spare layers are wise. Mobile coverage is patchy once you leave the main roads.

    Try the local fish, salmon, cod and the sweet king crab that was introduced from the Pacific, and warm up in a cafe with tea and pastries. The city is not pretty, but it has a strong, stubborn character that suits the north.

  2. Titovka stop

    Titovka is barely a place at all on the map, just a bridge over a fast, rocky river on the road between Murmansk and the Norwegian border, a few buildings and a roadside stop. But anyone heading for the Sredny and Rybachy peninsulas knows the name, because this is where the rough track north leaves the paved road. From here on, the riding changes completely: the tarmac gives way to gravel, rock and mud, the landscape opens into bare tundra, and the north coast of the Kola Peninsula starts to feel very close.

    The ground around Titovka is soaked in history. During the Second World War, the front line between Soviet and German forces settled along the Litsa and Titovka rivers for three years, from 1941 to 1944, and the fighting in this barren, frozen landscape was brutal. The hills are still scattered with old trenches, bunkers, rusting metal and memorials, and on the way north you will see many of them. It is not a place for carelessness: unexploded ordnance has been found here for decades, so keep to tracks and paths.

    The road from Murmansk to Titovka is paved, with long straights and sweeping bends across a rolling landscape of low hills, lakes and dwarf birch, and the views open wide in every direction. Beyond, the track north to Sredny is unpaved, rough and in places deeply rutted, with water crossings after rain. It is best tackled on a light adventure bike with good tyres and some experience off road. Weather changes fast this far north; even in July, a sunny morning can turn to cold rain and fog within an hour.

    Practical matters need real planning. The nearest town on the map, Zaozyorsk, is a closed naval town and not open to casual visitors, so fill up in Murmansk or Zapolyarny and carry extra fuel if you plan to explore the peninsulas. The peninsulas lie in a border zone, so check the current permit rules before you go. Bring warm, waterproof clothing, food and water, and treat the roadside stop at Titovka as a last outpost of comfort before the tundra takes over.

  3. Sredny Peninsula stop

    Sredny means middle, and the name is exact: this peninsula sits between the mainland of the Kola coast and the larger Rybachy Peninsula, joined to both by narrow isthmuses, a low, treeless stretch of tundra, rock and lakes pushed out into the Barents Sea. It looks empty, and it mostly is, but it has a long human history. Pomor fishermen from the White Sea worked these coasts for centuries, and between the world wars the western part belonged to Finland, as part of the narrow corridor that gave the Finns access to the Arctic Ocean.

    The Second World War left the deepest marks. In 1941 the German advance toward Murmansk was stopped on the ridge at the southern neck of Sredny, and the front froze there for three years. Soviet troops held the peninsulas throughout the war, supplied by sea under constant attack, and the ground is still littered with trenches, gun positions, barbed wire and the debris of war. Later came the Cold War: garrisons, airstrips and radar posts, most of them abandoned in the 1990s and now crumbling slowly in the wind.

    The riding is serious off-road work. There is no paved road on the peninsula, only tracks of gravel, sand, rock and mud that braid across the tundra and split into multiple ruts where vehicles have avoided the worst sections. Some fords can be deep after heavy rain or snowmelt, and the ground near lakes and streams can be boggy. A light adventure bike with knobbly tyres, riding in company, is the right approach. In summer the sun never sets, which helps, but fog can roll in from the sea at any time and turn a clear day grey and cold within minutes.

    You are a long way from help here. Zaozyorsk, the nearest town on the map, is a closed naval settlement, so bring all the fuel, food and water you will need from Murmansk or Zapolyarny. The peninsula lies inside the border zone, and permits have been required in the past, so check before you go. In return you get an almost completely wild Arctic coast, wildflowers in July, reindeer and Arctic terns, and a sense of space that few places in Europe can match.

  4. Rybachy Peninsula stop

    At the end of the track, past the ruined garrisons of Sredny and across another narrow neck of land, lies Rybachy, the Fishermen's Peninsula, the northernmost reach of mainland European Russia. Its tip, Cape Nemetsky, has a lighthouse and a view north to nothing but the open Barents Sea. The name is old: Russian Pomors and Norwegian fishermen worked these waters for cod and halibut for centuries, salting their catch in seasonal camps along the shore, and fishing villages survived here until the Soviet period swept them away.

    The landscape is dramatic in an understated way. The peninsula is a plateau of layered sandstone and slate, tilted and broken into cliffs along the coast, with tundra on top and streams cutting down to the sea. There are waterfalls tumbling over coastal cliffs, beaches of pebbles and driftwood, and small lakes full of trout. In summer the tundra blooms with cloudberries, cotton grass and tiny Arctic flowers, and the sea birds nest on the cliffs. The light is the great thing here: soft and endless in June and July, low and golden in August.

    Getting around is hard work. The tracks across Rybachy are rough, rocky and often muddy, with fords, deep ruts and sections where the trail disappears into a confusion of options. A light, capable bike, good tyres, spare parts and a riding partner are essential, and a GPS track is very helpful. The weather can be brutal even in summer, with gale-force winds, cold rain and fog off the sea, and conditions can change within an hour. Take warm layers and full waterproofs regardless of the forecast.

    There are no services on the peninsula, apart from a few seasonal tourist camps that may or may not be operating. Carry all fuel, food and water for the round trip from Murmansk or Zapolyarny, and remember that Zaozyorsk is a closed town. The area is a border zone, so check permit requirements well ahead. People who make it out here tend to talk about it for years afterwards: the cliffs, the wind, the light, and the sense of having reached the very edge of the continent.

  5. Mustatunturi finish

    The name is Finnish, Musta-Tunturi, the Black Fell, and it refers to the long, dark ridge of rock that rises across the neck of land joining the Sredny Peninsula to the mainland. In the summer of 1941 the German mountain troops advancing on Murmansk were stopped here, and for the next three years, until the Soviet offensive of October 1944, the front line ran along this ridge. Soldiers on both sides lived in dugouts blasted out of rock, in temperatures far below freezing and in months of darkness, often within shouting distance of each other.

    Today the ridge carries a memorial complex and the remains of the positions are everywhere: trenches cut into stone, collapsed bunkers, rusting wire, shell fragments and sometimes bones. The Russian phrase associated with this place is about the enemy not passing, and the defence of the peninsulas is remembered as one of the epics of the Arctic war. Visitors leave flowers and small tokens at the monuments. It is a sombre place, and the silence of the tundra around it makes the history feel close.

    The ride here, as the finish of a loop out to the peninsulas, comes on rough tracks over the tundra, with the ridge looming ahead and the sea visible on both sides. The tracks are rocky, rutted and sometimes muddy, with steep, loose sections where the trail climbs over the ridge and fords where streams cross. In wet weather the slopes can be slippery, and fog can hide the way. The views from the top, when it is clear, take in the Barents Sea, the bays and the empty hills rolling away toward Norway.

    There is nothing here in the way of services. Bring fuel, food and water from Murmansk or Zapolyarny, and do not count on Zaozyorsk, which is a closed military town. Keep to existing tracks and paths, because unexploded munitions are still found in the area. Take the time to walk among the positions and think about what it meant to hold this ridge through three Arctic winters. It is one of the most moving places in the far north, and the right note to end a ride on.

Why this ride

210 km from Sakurai to Sakurai, about 4 h 01 of riding. It goes by way of Horyu-ji Area, Nisanzai Kofun, Kôyasan - Niutsuhime-jinja and back through Nisanzai Kofun. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Ishibutai Mounded Tomb

    Ishibutai is the skeleton of a tomb. The massive granite blocks of its burial chamber, now exposed to the open sky, form one of the most astonishing sights in Asuka, a giant stone room that was once buried under an earth mound, and it is generally thought to be the tomb of Soga no Umako, the minister who built Asuka-dera and dominated the court until his death in 626. The mound has long since been stripped away, probably by looters and by the passage of centuries, leaving the stones standing in a grassy square like the bones of some enormous animal.

  2. 2. Hōryū-ji Area

    The buildings at Hōryū-ji are around thirteen hundred years old, and some of them are among the oldest wooden structures anywhere in the world. Founded in the early seventh century by Prince Shōtoku, a regent remembered as a champion of Buddhism and a founding figure of the Japanese state, the temple was rebuilt after a fire later in the same century, and parts of that rebuilding still stand. In 1993 it became one of the first sites in Japan to be listed by UNESCO, as the Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area.

  3. 3. Nisanzai Kofun

    In the middle of the sprawling city of Sakai, south of Osaka, a large keyhole-shaped mound rises above a ring of moats, covered in dense forest and surrounded by houses, roads and shops. This is the Nisanzai Kofun, a monumental tomb built in the fifth century for a member of the ruling elite of early Japan. It is one of the largest of the Mozu-Furuichi Kofun Group, a collection of burial mounds listed by UNESCO in 2019, which includes the enormous Daisen Kofun, traditionally attributed to the emperor Nintoku and one of the largest tombs in the world by area.

  4. 4. Kôyasan - Niutsuhime-jinja

    A hunter with two dogs, according to the legend, is why Koyasan exists. When the monk Kukai, later called Kobo Daishi, returned from studying in China in the early ninth century, he threw a ritual three-pronged object into the air and asked it to land on a site for a mountain monastery. He found it hanging in a pine tree on a high plateau in the Kii mountains, and there he met a hunter, the god Karibamyojin, who led him to the place with his dogs. The hunter's mother, the goddess Niutsuhime, is honoured at the shrine that guards the approach. Kukai founded his monastery in 816, and Koyasan has been the headquarters of Shingon Buddhism since.

Why this ride

A 180 km round trip from Vatican City - roughly 3 h 36 in the saddle. The stops in between: The Via Appia across Alban Hills, Appia. Regina Viarum and back through The Via Appia across Alban Hills. The line favours views over speed.

The roads

Mille Miglia Historic Route Core

The 28 km between Vatican City and The Via Appia across Alban Hills on Via Appia Nuova. It gains around 340 m and ends high, near 367 m. This is the bit the ride is built around.

The stops

  1. 1. Vatican City

    Start here.

    The smallest state in the world is about forty-four hectares of walled hill on the right bank of the Tiber, and somehow it contains the largest church in Christendom, one of the greatest art collections ever assembled and a population that would barely fill a village. The Vatican exists as an independent country since the Lateran Treaty of 1929 between the Holy See and Mussolini's Italy, but the popes have been building here for far longer. St Peter's Basilica stands over the traditional site of the apostle's grave, and its dome, designed by Michelangelo, dominates the skyline of western Rome. Bernini's colonnade, sweeping round the square like two great arms, was meant to embrace every visitor who arrived.

  2. 2. The Via Appia across Alban Hills

    28 km in.

    The Romans built the Appian Way straight as a ruler across the Campagna, but when it reached the Alban Hills, the old volcanic massif south-east of Rome, even they had to bend a little. At Ariccia, the road crossed a deep valley on a huge viaduct of stone blocks, parts of which still survive, and climbed past Albano and Genzano toward Velletri and the south.

    Read the complete The Via Appia across Alban Hills write-up.

  3. 3. Via Appia. Regina Viarum

    75 km in.

    South of the Alban Hills, the Via Appia drops onto the Pontine plain and runs dead straight for mile after mile, as if the Roman engineers had laid it with a ruler, which in a sense they did. This was the Regina Viarum, the queen of roads, begun in 312 BC by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus to link Rome with Capua, and later extended to Brindisi. Here, near Sezze Scalo and Latina, the road crossed the Pontine Marshes, a malarial swamp that defeated would-be drainers from the Romans to the popes. Travellers sometimes took a canal boat alongside the road instead, and the poet Horace complained about the mosquitoes and the frogs keeping him awake on that very journey.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Historic Monuments and Sites in Kaesong

    Kaesong was the capital of the Koryo dynasty from 918 until 1392, and the name Korea comes from the name of that kingdom. A prosperous trading city, it was a centre of Buddhism, celadon pottery and printing, and the court at its height was among the most refined in East Asia. When the Joseon dynasty took over in 1392, the capital moved to Seoul, but Kaesong remained known for its merchants, who were pioneers in ginseng trading and banking, and its cuisine, which many Koreans still regard as among the most elegant in the country.

  2. 2. Seosamreung Cluster

    The kings and queens of the Joseon dynasty, who ruled Korea for more than five hundred years until 1910, chose their graves with enormous care. Geomancers studied hills, water and wind to find sites with the right energy, and the royal tombs were laid out with burial mounds, stone guardians, ceremonial halls and long processional paths. Forty of these tombs are listed together by UNESCO as the Royal Tombs of the Joseon Dynasty, and Seosamreung, the three western tombs in Goyang just north-west of Seoul, is one of the clusters.

  3. 3. Hwaseong Fortress

    Hwaseong is a love letter from a son to his father, written in stone. In 1762, Crown Prince Sado was locked in a rice chest by his own father, King Yeongjo, and left to die, one of the darkest episodes in Korean royal history. His son, who became King Jeongjo, never forgot, and he moved his father's tomb to a site near Suwon and built a grand fortress there, completed in 1796, intended perhaps as a new capital. The engineer and scholar Jeong Yak-yong used cranes and pulleys to speed up construction, and the walls, gates, towers and bastions combine Korean, Chinese and Western ideas of fortification. UNESCO listed it in 1997.

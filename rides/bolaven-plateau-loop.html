Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Pakse

    Pakse sits where the Xe Don river meets the Mekong in the far south of Laos, a lazy, low-rise town of fewer than a hundred thousand people that works as the gateway to the Bolaven Plateau. The Mekong here is wide and brown, with sandbanks in the dry season, and a long bridge opened in 2000 with Japanese help carries the road over to the far bank. Streets are quiet, shuttered shops sell motorbike spares and coffee, and the pace is slow in a way that grows on you.

  2. 2. Tad Fane

    Two rivers run off the Bolaven Plateau side by side and throw themselves into the same gorge, and the result is Tad Fane, one of the tallest waterfalls in Laos, with a drop of well over a hundred metres. The streams, called the Huay Champi and the Huay Bang Lieng, plunge in thin white ribbons from the rim into a deep green abyss, and from a lookout across the gorge you can watch them fall for what seems like a very long time. The sound arrives a moment late, the spray drifts up on the air, and the mist lingers in the trees at the bottom. The best view is in the rainy season, from about June to October, when the flow is at its fullest, though the dry season gives clearer skies.

  3. 3. Paksong

    Paksong, which is also spelled Pakxong, is the unofficial coffee capital of Laos, a dusty market town set at the heart of the Bolaven Plateau where the air is cool, the soil is red and the whole economy smells of roasting beans. The plateau rises to over a thousand metres and catches regular rain, which suits both Arabica and Robusta, and the French planters who began growing coffee here in the early twentieth century left a legacy that Lao farmers have turned into a thriving industry. Plantations of shiny dark green bushes cover the hillsides, and in the harvest season, red berries are spread on tarpaulins to dry beside the road.

  4. 4. Tad Lo

    Tad Lo is a place where the Bolaven Plateau puts on its gentlest face. The Xe Set river tumbles over a series of low waterfalls and rapids in a wooded setting, with simple guesthouses on the banks, a few small restaurants and a village atmosphere that is popular with travellers who want to slow down. The falls are not the largest in Laos, but they are among the most pleasant, since you can swim in the pools below them in the dry season, sit on the rocks with a cold drink and listen to the water. The name tad means waterfall in Lao, and the plural is well deserved, since there are a handful in the neighbourhood.

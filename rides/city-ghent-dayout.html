Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Ghent

    Ghent likes to think of itself as the city that Bruges forgot to be. It is a large, lively, slightly rebellious town at the meeting of the Leie and the Scheldt, and for centuries it was one of the biggest cities north of the Alps, a textile power whose weavers and guilds fought dukes and counts. The memory of that stubbornness lingers. Locals are known as the noose-bearers, after a day in 1540 when citizens were made to parade in nooses to atone for rebelling against Emperor Charles V, who was born in the town.

  2. 2. Béguinage de Bruges

    The Princely Beguinage of Bruges, Ten Wijngaerde, is the sort of place that makes people lower their voices without being asked. White-washed houses surround a grassy courtyard planted with old trees, and in spring thousands of daffodils bloom under them. A beguinage was a community where lay women lived a religious life without taking full vows, and Flanders had many of them from the thirteenth century. This one was founded around 1245 under Margaret of Constantinople, Countess of Flanders, and the UNESCO listing of Flemish Beguinages covers it, along with Diest and others.

  3. 3. Historic Centre of Brugge

    Bruges was once one of the richest cities in northern Europe, a trading hub where merchants from Italy, Germany, England and Spain bought and sold wool, cloth, spices and luxury goods. Its wealth came from its access to the sea through an inlet called the Zwin, and when that silted up in the fifteenth century, trade moved to Antwerp and Bruges slowly fell asleep. For centuries it was a quiet, poor town, and that is why its medieval centre survived almost intact. In the nineteenth century, writers and painters rediscovered it, and Georges Rodenbach's novel Bruges-la-Morte made it famous as a melancholy city of canals and bells. The historic centre was listed by UNESCO in 2000.

  5. 5. Belfort en Hallen

    Bruges grew rich in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries as a cloth and trading port, and the belfry on its market square is the proudest sign of that wealth. Rising about eighty-three metres above the Markt, with an octagonal upper stage, few towers in Belgium are better known. The Hallen, the old market halls at its base, once held the town's cloth trade, and the belfry stored the town's charters and its bells, so that merchants and citizens could be called together and warned of fire or attack. It is also a part of the group of belfries of Belgium and France on the World Heritage list.

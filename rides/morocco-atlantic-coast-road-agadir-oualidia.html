Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Agadir

    Agadir is a very new city in a very old country, and the reason is the earthquake. At a few minutes before midnight on 29 February 1960, a quake destroyed most of the old town and killed thousands of people, and the city was rebuilt from scratch in a modern style, with wide avenues, low buildings and a long promenade along the bay. The one piece that survived is the hilltop kasbah, Agadir Oufella, whose ruined walls overlook the town from about two hundred metres up, bearing an inscription in Arabic that reads God, Country, King. The view from it, across the bay and the port, is the best in town.

  2. 2. Taghazout

    Taghazout started as a fishing village and still smells like one around the harbour at dawn, but since the late sixties it has had a second life as Morocco's surf town. Travellers on the hippie trail drifted up the coast from Agadir looking for empty point breaks, and the names they gave the waves, Anchor Point and Killer Point among them, stuck. Today the village climbs a hillside above the Atlantic in tiers of whitewashed and sand-coloured houses, with surf schools and rooftop cafes in between the older family homes, and a large resort development now sits in the bay to the north.

  3. 3. Essaouira

    The wind is the first thing you meet in Essaouira, and it never really leaves. The trade winds blow in from the Atlantic for most of the year, which is why the old Portuguese-named town of Mogador is a centre for windsurfing and kitesurfing, and why the beach is a long, flat plain of pale sand with surfers working the surf at the far end.

  4. 4. Oualidia

    Oualidia is where the Atlantic stops being an ocean and becomes a lagoon. A natural sandbar and a line of rock shelter a calm, shallow bay, a few kilometres long, from the swells of the open sea, and the village, with about five and a half thousand people, sits along its shore, with small fishing boats pulled up on the sand and blue-painted houses facing the water. The tide moves in and out through narrow channels, and at low water the flats are covered with wading birds. The lagoon is protected as a wetland of international importance and is a winter home for flamingos, spoonbills and many others.

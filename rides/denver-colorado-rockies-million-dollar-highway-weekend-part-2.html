Why this ride

Out of Estes Park, one way: 493 km, roughly 10 h 07 in the saddle. The stops in between: Fort Morgan, near Fort Morgan and Lakewood. The climb of the day is Near Fort Morgan, on the way back. It tops out at about 3,200 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Photo stop near Estes Park

    Trail Ridge Road begins its climb here, a few miles west of Estes Park, and by the time you reach this stretch of US 34, you have entered Rocky Mountain National Park and left the town far below. The road winds up through forests of ponderosa pine and Douglas fir, past meadows where elk graze, and around curves that open up views of the Continental Divide. Around Many Parks Curve, a parking area on a tight bend gives one of the best early views: a series of mountain meadows, or parks, spread out below, with Longs Peak, the highest summit in the park, towering over them to the south.

  2. 2. Near Fort Morgan, on the way back

    The eastern plains of Colorado do not do subtle. The sky is enormous, the horizon dead flat, and on summer afternoons thunderheads build into towers that can drop hail the size of golf balls within minutes. This stop lies on the South Platte river, near Fort Morgan, a farming and ranching town on I-76 whose grain elevators are the tallest things for miles.

  4. 4. Near Lakewood

    Lakewood is where Denver's suburbs press right up against the mountains. A large city of more than 150,000 people, it spreads west from Denver toward the foothills, and from almost anywhere in it you can see the first ridges of the Rockies rising abruptly from the plains, often with snow on the peaks behind. This finishing point lies just east of the foothills, in a district of houses, malls and office parks, with Green Mountain, a flat-topped grassy hill, standing just to the west.

  5. 5. Back to Million

    Castle Rock takes its name from a flat-topped butte of rhyolite that rises above the town, with a crown of stone that looks like a battlement, and the rock has been used as a landmark since the days of the wagon road. It stands in the middle of town, with a little star lit on it at Christmas, and in a few minutes you can see why settlers reached for the word castle. The town, with about fifty-five thousand people, sits between Denver and Colorado Springs on the Interstate 25 corridor, and has grown fast from a small ranching and quarrying centre into a busy suburb.

Why this ride

From Salamanca it runs 364 km to Salamanca; allow about 6 h 17 of riding. It goes by way of Hermitage of San Segundo, Subsidiary building \La Casita del Infante and back through Hermitage of San Segundo. The highest point is about 1,350 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Iglesia de San Juan Barbalos

    Salamanca was a ruin when it was resettled around 1100. The Muslim and Christian armies had passed back and forth across the Duero plain for generations, and the city on the Tormes was half empty until Count Raymond of Burgundy, who had married a daughter of King Alfonso VI, brought in settlers from across northern Spain and gave them land. They came as Castilians, Galicians, Basques, Mozarabs and Franks, and each group tended to cluster around its own church, so that the old town ended up with dozens of small Romanesque parish churches, each serving a few streets. San Juan de Barbalos is one of this family, a plain, sturdy building of the twelfth century in the compact style of the time.

  2. 2. Hermitage of San Segundo

    Outside the great medieval walls of Ávila, down by the river Adaja, a small Romanesque church stands among trees, quiet and often overlooked by the crowds heading for the walls and the cathedral. The Hermitage of San Segundo is dedicated to the first bishop of Ávila, according to tradition one of seven early missionaries sent to Spain, and it holds an alabaster statue of the saint at prayer, carved in the sixteenth century by the sculptor Juan de Juni. The building itself is simple and austere, with a beautiful carved doorway, and it is a lovely, peaceful place to visit. It is part of the UNESCO listing of the Old Town of Ávila with its Extra-Muros Churches.

  3. 3. Subsidiary building \La Casita del Infante

    Most people who come to San Lorenzo de El Escorial only see the monastery, that vast granite grid Philip II raised in the second half of the sixteenth century as palace, basilica, library and royal tomb all at once. A short walk below it, through terraced gardens and woodland, sits something far more human in scale. The Casita del Infante, also called the Casita de Arriba, was designed by Juan de Villanueva in the late eighteenth century for the Infante Gabriel, a son of Carlos III, as a place to retreat, play music and escape the endless etiquette of court. Its sibling, the Casita del Príncipe, lies down the hill near the town of El Escorial and was built for the future Carlos IV.

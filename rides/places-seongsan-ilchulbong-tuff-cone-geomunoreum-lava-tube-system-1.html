Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Seongsan Ilchulbong Tuff Cone

    Seongsan Ilchulbong rises from the eastern tip of Jeju like a stone crown that someone dropped into the sea. It is a tuff cone, formed in a single explosive eruption when magma hit shallow water, roughly five thousand years ago, and the crater at the top is a wide, grassy bowl about six hundred metres across, ringed by jagged rocky teeth. The name means sunrise peak, and the Koreans take the idea seriously: on New Year's morning thousands climb the steps to watch the sun come up out of the East Sea.

  2. 2. Geomunoreum Lava Tube System - 3

    Geomunoreum is a quiet, forested volcanic cone on the north-east side of Jeju, and the real attraction is underground. Thousands of years ago lava poured from this cone down towards the sea, and as the outside of the flow cooled and hardened, the molten rock inside kept draining out, leaving long tunnels. Together they form the Geomunoreum lava tube system, which is part of the UNESCO listing for Jeju's volcanic island and its lava tubes. Manjanggul, the most famous tube, runs for several kilometres, with only a section open to visitors, and it has a lava column rising several metres from the cave floor, a remarkable sight.

  3. 3. Geomunoreum Lava Tube System - 1

    Under the northeastern slopes of Jeju there is a hollow world made by fire. Geomunoreum is a small volcanic cone, one of the hundreds that dot the island's flanks, and in the long prehistory of the island it spilled lava that ran downhill in rivers. The surface of each flow cooled and hardened while the molten rock inside kept moving, and when it finally drained away it left long empty tunnels. The Geomunoreum lava tube system, a family of caves including Manjanggul, Gimnyeonggul and others, is among the finest in the world, and it is the core of the Jeju Volcanic Island and Lava Tubes World Heritage site, inscribed in 2007.

Why this ride

155 km round trip from Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Comines, about 3 h 14 of riding. It runs out through Voormalig Stadhuis met Belfort, Stadshal met Belfort, Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Dunkerque and Beffroi de l’église St-Eloi de Dunkerque and comes back by another road.

The stops

  1. 1. Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Comines

    Start here.

    Comines is one of those towns the border runs straight through. The river Lys, the Leie to the Flemish, divides French Comines from Belgian Comines-Warneton, and you can cross from one country to the other on a bridge without noticing much more than a change in the shape of the street signs. On the French side the town hall and its belfry stand over the main square, a tall brick tower in the Flemish style with a pointed roof and a carillon, part of the group of belfries of Belgium and France listed by UNESCO.

    Read the complete Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Comines write-up.

  2. 2. Voormalig Stadhuis met Belfort

    34 km in.

    Lo is one of the smallest towns in Belgium, a cluster of brick houses around a market square in the flat polders of the Westhoek, a few kilometres from the French border. Its former town hall, built in the sixteenth century in Renaissance style, has a small belfry tower, and it is part of the UNESCO-listed Belfries of Belgium and France, alongside much grander towers in Bruges, Ghent and Lille. It is a reminder that even tiny towns had civic pride and privileges worth announcing to the world.

    Read the complete Voormalig Stadhuis met Belfort write-up.

  3. 3. Stadshal met Belfort

    56 km in.

    Nieuwpoort's cloth hall and belfry look medieval, and in a sense they are: they follow the design of the thirteenth-century originals. But almost everything you see was rebuilt after the First World War, when the town was reduced to rubble. Nieuwpoort lay at the northern end of the Western Front, where the River Yser reaches the sea, and in October 1914 the Belgians made a decision that changed the course of the war. By opening the sluices of the canals and the river at high tide, they flooded the low-lying land between Nieuwpoort and Diksmuide, creating a barrier of water that stopped the German advance. The front line stayed there for four years, and the town was shelled into ruins. The belfry is part of the UNESCO listing of belfries of Belgium and France.

    Read the complete Stadshal met Belfort write-up.

  4. 4. Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Dunkerque

    89 km in.

    The tower of Dunkirk's town hall rises above the centre of a city rebuilt from the ruins of the Second World War, a brick and stone belfry that is part of the World Heritage Site of the Belfries of Belgium and France. Belfries were symbols of civic freedom in the medieval towns of Flanders and northern France, housing bells and sometimes the town's charters, and they stood as proud counterparts to church towers. Dunkirk actually has two listed belfries, the town hall tower and the separate Saint-Éloi belfry beside the church, a reminder of how much these towns valued their bells.

    Read the complete Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Dunkerque write-up.

  5. 5. Beffroi de l’église St-Eloi de Dunkerque

    90 km in.

    The belfry of Saint-Éloi stands alone, which is odd for a church tower. It was originally attached to the church, but after fires and rebuilding in earlier centuries, a street was cut between them, and the tower became a separate landmark. Today it is one of the belfries of Belgium and France listed by UNESCO, and its carillon plays tunes across the centre of Dunkirk, including a local song. You can climb to the top for views over the port, the city and the North Sea.

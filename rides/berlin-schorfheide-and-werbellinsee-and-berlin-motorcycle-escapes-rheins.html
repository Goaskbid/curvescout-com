  1. 1. Bernau fuel escape

    Bernau is the gate to the north-eastern forest, and the way to use it is to be out of Berlin before the city notices you have gone. The B2 and the A11 both start here, with the S-Bahn line running alongside, and the stations around the edge of town are where commuters and weekend riders cross paths. With almost thirty-five thousand people, Bernau has everything a rider might need: fuel, bakeries, supermarkets and a bike shop or two.

  2. 2. Chorin monastery

    Few ruins in Germany are as quietly impressive as Chorin. The monastery was founded in the late thirteenth century by Cistercian monks, who moved here from a less suitable site nearby, and they built it in the red brick that is the signature of north German Gothic. The west gable is a masterpiece, a tall wall of brick with delicate tracery, blind windows and slender pinnacles, standing against the sky. The roofless church, the cloister and the remaining monastic buildings sit beside a small lake, the Amtssee, and the setting among woods and meadows feels remote even though Berlin is only an hour away.

  3. 3. Werbellinsee shoreline

    The Werbellinsee is a long, deep, glacial lake in the Schorfheide, about ten kilometres from end to end, and its shoreline is one of the loveliest in Brandenburg. Beech forest comes down to the edge, the water is clear and dark, and the road runs along it in places so close that you can smell the water. Joachimsthal lies at its northern end, and Eberswalde, a good-sized town of about forty-two thousand, is a short ride to the south.

  4. 4. Schorfheide forest roads

    Schorfheide is the forest that has hosted more rulers than any other in Germany. Hohenzollern electors hunted here, Hermann Göring built a vast estate called Carinhall in the woods, naming it after his first wife, and the leaders of East Germany kept their own hunting lodges, all in the same pine and beech. Carinhall was blown up in 1945 and little remains, but the forest itself is intact, and it is one of the largest continuous woodlands in the region.

  5. 5. Back to Bernau

    Bernau keeps its medieval wall. Most German towns lost theirs to the nineteenth century, but here a long stretch of the brick-and-fieldstone circuit survives, with the Pulverturm, the Steintor and the grim little Hungerturm still standing, and you can walk almost the whole circle in half an hour. Legend says the wall saved the town from the Hussites in 1432, and an annual festival with costumes keeps the story alive, whatever the historians say. About thirty-five thousand people live here now, and the old centre, with its large Gothic church of St Marien, sits quietly behind a commuter-belt outer ring.

  6. 6. Fuel stop in Strausberg

    Strausberg is the last proper town before Berlin turns into Brandenburg, and it knows how to be useful. It sits on the shore of a long lake, the Straussee, about thirty kilometres east of the capital, with the S-Bahn running straight in, and about twenty-six thousand people live here. The medieval centre kept a stretch of its town wall and the brick gothic Marienkirche, and the little ferry across the lake, the Strausberg ferry, is one of the nicest short crossings in the region. The town also runs one of the smallest tram networks in Germany, a single line that has served for a century.

  8. 8. Food stop near Neuruppin

    Two of the most important figures in Prussian culture were born in Neuruppin: the architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel in 1781 and the novelist Theodor Fontane in 1819. Fontane went on to write the Wanderungen durch die Mark Brandenburg, a multi-volume account of his travels through the region, and the first volume opens with the county of Ruppin. Reading even a few pages before riding these roads gives them a depth they might otherwise lack.

  10. 10. Near Lichterfelde

    In 1881, Werner von Siemens opened the world's first electric tram line in Lichterfelde, a short track connecting the railway station with the cadet academy. Thirteen years later, Otto Lilienthal, the pioneer of human flight, had an artificial hill built in the same suburb so that he could launch his gliders from it, whatever the wind direction. The mound is still there, now a small park with a memorial. For a quiet residential district in south-western Berlin, Lichterfelde has a surprising place in the history of technology.

  13. 13. Back to Rheinsberg Lakes

    Strausberg is a lakeside town at the eastern edge of Berlin's commuter belt, and the last stop on one of the city's suburban railway lines. It is set on the Straussee, a long, narrow lake that bends through the middle of the old town, and its medieval centre keeps some stretches of town wall and a church, with a pleasant promenade on the waterfront. A little ferry in summer crosses the lake, a pleasant way to see the town from the water. Rehfelde, a smaller village nearby, and Petershagen add a few pretty corners.

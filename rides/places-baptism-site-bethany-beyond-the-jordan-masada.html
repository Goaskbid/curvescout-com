  1. 1. Baptism Site “Bethany Beyond the Jordan”

    Down here you are well below sea level, close to four hundred metres under it, and the air shows it: thick, warm and heavy, with a mineral haze rising off the Dead Sea a few kilometres to the south. The baptism site known as Bethany Beyond the Jordan, Al-Maghtas in Arabic, lies on the east bank of the river Jordan, the place many Christians identify with the baptism of Jesus by John. Across a narrow, muddy channel of water stands Qasr al-Yahud on the western side, close to Jericho, and the two banks look at each other across one of the world's most heavily watched borders.

  2. 2. Tower

    Umm ar-Rasas rises out of the plateau east of the King's Highway with little warning, a walled rectangle of Byzantine and early Islamic ruins on a flat expanse of wheat fields and sheep. In ancient times it was called Kastron Mefaa, a Roman military camp that later developed into a town, and it remained inhabited through the Byzantine period and into the early Islamic centuries before it faded away. UNESCO listed it in 2004, and the most remarkable piece of the site is the floor of the Church of Saint Stephen, a mosaic from the eighth century that shows cities of the region in tiny, detailed vignettes, including Jerusalem and Philadelphia, the ancient name of Amman.

  3. 3. Masada

    The Dead Sea lies more than four hundred metres below sea level, the lowest point on land, and Masada rises above its western shore as a vast flat-topped rock with sheer cliffs on every side. King Herod built a palace fortress here in the first century BC, with storerooms, cisterns, bathhouses and a three-tiered palace hanging from the northern cliff. After the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem in AD 70, a group of Jewish rebels held out on the rock against the Roman army. The Romans built a siege ramp up the western side, and according to the historian Josephus, when the walls were breached, the defenders chose to die rather than be captured.

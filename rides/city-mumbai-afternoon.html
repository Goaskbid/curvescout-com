Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Mumbai

    Mumbai is a city that never quite stands still, and for a rider the first lesson is that the road belongs to everyone. Taxis, buses, rickshaws, scooters, handcarts, cows and pedestrians share every lane, and the horn is a language, used for greeting, warning and complaint. More than twelve million people live in the city proper, and many more in the surrounding region, making it one of the largest urban areas on earth. It was called Bombay until 1995.

  2. 2. Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus

    Mumbai's great railway terminus is a cathedral built for engines. It was designed by the English architect Frederick William Stevens and completed in the late 1880s as Victoria Terminus, named for the queen who was also Empress of India, and its mixture of Victorian Gothic and Indian motifs is one of the most flamboyant of the age. Domes, spires, pointed arches, carved peacocks and lions, stained glass and stone gargoyles give the whole building a theatrical quality. It was renamed in honour of the Maratha king Shivaji in the 1990s, and is now on the World Heritage list. Locals still call it VT.

  4. 4. Victorian Gothic and Art Deco Ensembles of

    Mumbai built itself a seafront in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the result is one of the most unusual architectural neighbourhoods on earth. The Victorian Gothic and Art Deco Ensembles, inscribed by UNESCO in 2018, are two separate groups of buildings, facing each other across the Oval Maidan, a big open cricket ground in the south of the city. On one side are the Gothic Revival buildings of the British Raj: the Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus, which was Victoria Terminus, the High Court, the University and the Rajabai Clock Tower. On the other are the Art Deco cinemas, hotels and apartment blocks of the 1930s, along Marine Drive.

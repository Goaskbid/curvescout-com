Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. C8: Prehistoric village of Trepucó

    Menorca has more prehistoric monuments per square kilometre than almost anywhere in Europe, and Trepucó is the one nearest to the island's capital. It is a talayotic settlement, built by a Bronze and Iron Age people whose culture lasted from around 1000 BC to the Roman conquest. The most striking things are a very large talayot, a stone tower whose purpose is still debated, and a taula, a T-shaped monument made of two enormous stones, one set on its edge and the other laid across the top. Nobody knows for certain what the tauli were for, though most scholars think they belonged to a sanctuary.

  2. 2. C5: Area between the ravines of Torrevella

    Menorca is a gentler island than its neighbours, and the Talaiotic culture left behind a remarkable landscape of stone monuments that UNESCO recognised in 2023. These are the talaiots, round or square towers of dry stone, the taulas, great T-shaped stone altars, and the navetas, boat-shaped tombs, all dating from the second and first millennia BC. Nobody knows exactly what the people who built them believed, but the sheer number is astonishing for an island that you can cross by car in an hour. C5 is one of the numbered zones of that heritage landscape, in the area between the ravines of Torrevella, south of Alaior.

  3. 3. C3: Western Migjorn area

    Menorca has more prehistoric monuments per square kilometre than almost anywhere in Europe, and the south-west of the island is a good place to see why. The people who lived here in the Bronze and Iron Ages built talayots, round towers of stacked stones, and taules, T-shaped stone altars, and navetas, burial chambers shaped like upturned boats, in a culture that lasted for over a thousand years. In 2023 UNESCO added the Talayotic Culture of Minorca to its list, and the western Migjorn area, the south-west of the island, is one of the zones included.

  4. 4. C6: South-east area-Alaior

    Menorca has more prehistoric monuments per square kilometre than almost anywhere in Europe, and the land around Alaior is one of the richest parts. In the second millennium BC the island's people, known as the Talayotic culture, built big stone towers called talaiots, sanctuaries with T-shaped stone pillars called taulas, and cave burials, all from huge blocks of limestone piled without mortar. UNESCO inscribed the Talayotic Menorca sites in 2023. Near Alaior, large settlements such as Torre d'en Galmés and Torralba d'en Salord are the best known, and their stone enclosures are scattered across open fields, with dry-stone walls in all directions.

  5. 5. C2: Southwest Area

    Ciutadella is the old capital of Menorca, a handsome town of nearly thirty thousand people at the western end of the island, with a small harbour lined with fish restaurants and a cathedral built on the site of a mosque. The south-west corner of the island, below the town, holds some of the finest prehistoric remains on Menorca, including the burial chamber known as the Naveta des Tudons, a stone structure shaped like an upturned boat that is among the oldest roofed buildings in Europe. It belongs to the talayotic culture that built towers and T-shaped sanctuaries across the island over many centuries.

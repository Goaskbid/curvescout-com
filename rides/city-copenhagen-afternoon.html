  1. 1. Copenhagen

    Copenhagen is a city that runs on bicycles, and a motorcyclist arriving here should take the hint and ride softly. Cycle lanes carry thousands of commuters at rush hour, and a car door or a turning van can ruin everybody's day, so patience and constant mirror checks are the rule. In return, the city is compact, handsome and unhurried, with copper-green spires, canals and handsome old warehouses along the harbour, where the bright houses of Nyhavn, once home to Hans Christian Andersen, are crowded with tourists on summer evenings. A short stroll away, Tivoli Gardens, opened in 1843, remains one of the oldest amusement parks in the world and glows with lights after dark.

  2. 2. Store Dyrehave

    North of Copenhagen the royal family once hunted on a scale that reshaped the landscape, and Store Dyrehave, the Great Deer Park, near Hillerød, is a piece of that design. The forest is cut by straight rides, long avenues that radiate like the spokes of a wheel, so that horsemen and hounds could follow a stag at speed without losing sight of it. This kind of hunting, called par force, was fashionable at European courts in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and the Danish kings built an entire landscape for it, now recognised by UNESCO as the Par Force Hunting Landscape in North Zealand.

  3. 3. The par force hunting landscape in North Z

    Par force hunting sounds like a French affectation, and it was. The kings of Denmark in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries imported the style from the French court, in which riders on horseback and packs of hounds chased a stag until it was exhausted. To make it work, they needed a landscape designed for speed, and in North Zealand the royal foresters cut long, straight rides through the woods, many of them meeting at star-shaped crossings, so that the hunters could see the quarry and keep control of the chase. The pattern is still visible in the forests of Gribskov and Store Dyrehave, and UNESCO listed the landscape in 2015.

  4. 4. Path

    Hillerod is a town north-west of Copenhagen with a palace that looks as if it were built for a fairy tale. Frederiksborg Castle sits on three small islands in a lake, a red-brick Renaissance building with copper-green spires, towers and gables, reflected in the water. It was built for King Christian IV in the early seventeenth century and became the site where Danish kings were crowned for generations. Today it houses the Museum of National History, with portraits and rooms full of Danish history.

  5. 5. Roskilde Cathedral

    Roskilde Cathedral is where Denmark keeps its kings. Since the fifteenth century, almost every Danish monarch has been buried here, and the chapels and crypts hold around forty kings and queens, from medieval stone tombs to ornate baroque sarcophagi and modern designs. The cathedral itself, begun in the late twelfth century under Bishop Absalon, the warrior-churchman who also founded Copenhagen, was one of the first big brick Gothic churches in Scandinavia, and it set the style for church building across the region. UNESCO listed it in 1995. Inside, look for the clock with the figure of St George slaying the dragon, which strikes the hour, and the pillar where royal visitors have marked their height.

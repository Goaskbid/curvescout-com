Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Bullring

    The bullring at Almadén is not round, and that is the first thing to know about it. It is a polygon, built in the eighteenth century, and by tradition it is among the oldest bullrings in Spain. It stands in the middle of the town, and the houses along one side were built to form part of the ring itself, so that the balconies of ordinary homes overlook the arena. The effect is odd and charming, a place where the daily life of a mining town and its festivals are interwoven. During the fiestas, the town fills with people and horses and the sand is raked, and at other times it is a quiet space where you can just look around.

  2. 2. La Alamedilla

    Nobody lives close to this point, and that is its main fact. It lies in the eastern Sierra Morena, in the wild border country between Córdoba and Jaén, where holm oak and cork oak cover steep, empty hills and the nearest villages, Mestanza, Baños de la Encina and Cardeña, are all more than twenty-five kilometres away. The ride titles link it with the mercury mines of Almadén, whose Royal Forced Labour Gaol and miners' hospital are part of a UNESCO listing shared with Idrija in Slovenia, but Almadén lies far to the north-west, and the connection is a routing choice rather than a neighbourhood.

  3. 3. Abrigo de D. Pedro mota

    Aldeaquemada means the burnt village, which tells you something about how rough the Sierra Morena used to be. This is a thinly populated corner of Jaén province, on the edge of the Despeñaperros range, and the shelter named after D. Pedro Mota is one of a large group of painted rock shelters in the hills. The paintings belong to what archaeologists call Levantine and Schematic rock art, small red and black figures of people, animals and symbols made by prehistoric hunters and later farming communities, and UNESCO listed the whole Mediterranean-arc group in 1998.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Belo Horizonte

    Belo Horizonte means beautiful horizon, and the name comes from the ridge of the Serra do Curral that closes the view to the south of town. It is a large city, a bit over two million people, and unusually for Brazil it was planned: it was laid out in the 1890s as the new capital of Minas Gerais, replacing Ouro Preto, with wide avenues and a grid cut across by diagonal boulevards. The result is a place that feels more ordered than many Brazilian cities, though traffic is dense and the hills make it a rider's workout.

  2. 2. Ouro Preto

    Ouro Preto means black gold, after the dark, iron-stained nuggets that set off the rush here at the end of the seventeenth century. For a while this town, then called Vila Rica, was among the richest and most populous places in the Americas, and its money went into churches, bridges, fountains and the steep, tilted streets you will be climbing and descending. UNESCO listed it in 1980, among the very first Brazilian sites, and walking in the centre you can see why: hardly a modern building disturbs the skyline of terracotta roofs and whitewashed towers climbing the hillsides.

  3. 3. Mariana

    Mariana is where Minas Gerais begins, at least on paper. It was the first town the Portuguese founded in the gold country, in 1696, and later the first capital and first bishopric of the whole captaincy. It was named for Maria Anna of Austria, the queen of João V, which tells you who the town was meant to impress. Today it sits at around seven hundred metres in a bowl of green, forested hills, a calm university and mining town of some forty-five thousand people, and it is the natural place to begin the Estrada Real, the old colonial road along which gold and diamonds went to the coast and everything else came back up.

Why this ride

Built as a military road across the Făgăraș ridge, the Transfăgărășan spends its best kilometres above the treeline. The northern ramps are the spectacular half; the southern side runs longer and gentler past the Vidraru dam. It is only open in the warm months and the top can shut with no notice, so treat any plan as provisional.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cârțișoara

    Cârțișoara is the village at the northern foot of the Transfăgărășan, at roughly six hundred metres above the sea, where the road from the Făgăraș mountains reaches the Olt valley. Behind it rises the highest range in Romania, a wall of ridges and glacial cirques topped by Moldoveanu, around 2,544 metres, and in front lies the gentle farmland of southern Transylvania. The contrast is sudden: a few kilometres of fields and orchards, and then a wall of forest and rock that climbs out of sight.

  2. 2. Bâlea Cascadă

    Bâlea Cascadă is the waterfall that gives its name to the stop, a thin silver plume that drops down a steep glacial step in the Fagaras Mountains, just below the Transfagarasan summit road. It sits at 1,234 metres, and the hairpins above it climb to a high lake and a tunnel at around two thousand metres. Victoria, a small town of about ten thousand people, is the nearest place of any size, some fifteen kilometres away as the crow flies, and fuel and food are down there or in the villages on the northern approach.

  3. 3. Bâlea Lake tunnel

    The Bâlea Lake tunnel marks the roof of the Transfăgărășan, the highway that crosses the Făgăraș Mountains in Romania, and at 2,042 metres the air is thin and cold, even in summer. The road was ordered by Nicolae Ceaușescu after the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968, as a military route in case of an invasion from the east, and was built between 1970 and 1974 largely by army conscripts using dynamite and hand tools. It was an extraordinary and costly effort, and many workers lost their lives. Today it is known as one of the best driving and riding roads in the world.

  4. 4. Vidraru Dam

    The Vidraru Dam closes off the Argeș river in a deep, narrow gorge in the Făgăraș mountains, and it is one of the most imposing pieces of engineering in Romania. An arch dam about 166 metres high, completed in the mid-1960s, it holds back a long, dark lake, whose surface reflects the steep, forested slopes around it, and the view from the top of the wall is dizzying. A statue of Prometheus stands on the edge of the dam, with the lake behind him, and a bungee platform has made the dam a place of some notoriety.

  5. 5. Curtea de Argeș

    Curtea de Argeș was one of the first capitals of Wallachia, in the fourteenth century, and its great monument is a monastery that everyone in Romania knows. The cathedral, built in the early sixteenth century by Prince Neagoe Basarab, is a white stone building covered in carved lace, with slim towers and delicate patterns that look more like ironwork than masonry. A legend tells that the master builder, Manole, was made by the prince to wall his own wife into the structure so that it would stand, and that he fell to his death trying to escape the roof. The tale is told in ballads, and every Romanian child knows it. The church also holds the tombs of the country's kings, including Carol I and Ferdinand.

  1. 1. Old Town of Ávila with its Extra-Muros Chu

    There is a kind of satisfaction in a city that is exactly what its postcards say, and Ávila is one. The walls are intact, golden at sunset, and the cathedral is built right into them, its apse forming one of the fortress towers, which gives the whole thing the look of a church that has chosen to defend itself. The old town and its churches outside the walls, the extra muros, were recognised by UNESCO in 1985, and most of what makes it special can be seen in a morning.

  2. 2. Subsidiary building \La Casita del Infante

    Most people who come to San Lorenzo de El Escorial only see the monastery, that vast granite grid Philip II raised in the second half of the sixteenth century as palace, basilica, library and royal tomb all at once. A short walk below it, through terraced gardens and woodland, sits something far more human in scale. The Casita del Infante, also called the Casita de Arriba, was designed by Juan de Villanueva in the late eighteenth century for the Infante Gabriel, a son of Carlos III, as a place to retreat, play music and escape the endless etiquette of court. Its sibling, the Casita del Príncipe, lies down the hill near the town of El Escorial and was built for the future Carlos IV.

  3. 3. University and Historic Precinct of Alcalá

    Alcalá de Henares has a talent for producing writers. Miguel de Cervantes was born here in 1547, and a house museum in the old town commemorates him. The university, founded by Cardinal Cisneros in 1499, was one of the earliest planned university towns in Europe and a model for institutions in the Americas, and its Renaissance and Baroque buildings, with their stone façades and courtyards, are the core of the UNESCO listing that Alcalá received in 1998 for its university and historic precinct. The Paraninfo, the ceremonial hall, is where the Cervantes Prize is presented each year.

  1. 1. Watchtower WP 7/37

    The Romans liked straight lines, and nowhere more than here. On the edge of Walldürn, in the hilly country between the Odenwald and the Tauber valley, runs the course of the Upper Germanic-Raetian Limes, the frontier wall that marked the edge of the Roman Empire across what is now southern Germany. Watchtower WP 7/37 is one of hundreds of small posts that lined it, each within sight of the next, so that signals could be passed along the frontier. The Limes here famously runs almost dead straight for many kilometres, regardless of hills and valleys, a statement of Roman confidence and surveying skill.

  2. 2. Würzburg Residence with the Court Gardens

    Balthasar Neumann was a military engineer before he was an architect, and it shows in the Würzburg Residence, a palace so confidently engineered that its great staircase vault, with no central support, was said to be impossible. It was built for the prince-bishops of Würzburg in the early eighteenth century, with the main construction running from about 1720 to the mid-1740s. The ceiling above the stairs carries a fresco by Giovanni Battista Tiepolo, which is among the largest in the world and depicts the four continents. UNESCO listed the whole ensemble in 1981, court gardens included.

  3. 3. Bad Kissingen

    Bad Kissingen is a spa town in the northern corner of Bavaria, in the valley of the Franconian Saale, between the Rhön hills and the Spessart. People have taken the waters here since the Middle Ages, and in the nineteenth century the town became one of the great resorts of Europe, visited by Empress Elisabeth of Austria, known as Sisi, by tsars and kings, and by Bismarck, who drafted his famous Kissinger Diktat in 1877. In 2021 UNESCO added the town to its list of the Great Spa Towns of Europe, alongside Baden-Baden, Bath, Karlsbad and others.

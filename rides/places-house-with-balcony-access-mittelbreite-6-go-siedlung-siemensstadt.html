Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. House with Balcony access - Mittelbreite 6

    Hannes Meyer was a Swiss architect and the second director of the Bauhaus, and in Dessau he designed a row of houses that put his ideas into concrete form. The Laubenganghäuser in Törten, finished around 1930, are blocks of flats reached by external walkways, the balcony access, so that each floor has a long open gallery along the front. It was a cheap, efficient way to give small flats light and ventilation, and it was an experiment in social housing, in an age when a decent flat was a political question.

  2. 2. The Castle Church

    On 31 October 1517, according to tradition, a professor of theology named Martin Luther nailed a list of ninety five theses to the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg, challenging the sale of indulgences by the Catholic Church. Whether he actually used a hammer is debated by historians, but the theses spread rapidly across Germany and beyond, thanks to the printing press, and they sparked the Reformation, which transformed the religious, political and cultural life of Europe. The original wooden door was destroyed by fire in 1760, and in 1858 it was replaced by bronze doors bearing the text of the theses in Latin. Luther is buried inside the church, as is his friend and colleague Philipp Melanchthon.

  3. 3. Palaces and Parks of Potsdam and Berlin

    Potsdam lies just outside Berlin, in a landscape of lakes, rivers and woods along the Havel, and for over two centuries it was the summer residence of the Prussian kings and German emperors. Frederick the Great built the palace of Sanssouci here in the 1740s, a single-storey Rococo palace above terraced vineyards, where he wanted to live without worries, as its name suggests. His successors added more palaces, gardens and follies, and today the ensemble, together with sites in Berlin, forms one of Germany's largest UNESCO World Heritage sites.

  4. 4. Goßsiedlung Siemensstadt

    Berlin's Siemensstadt began as a factory town. Werner von Siemens's company moved its electrical works to the edge of Spandau from 1899, and by the 1920s a whole district of workshops and workers' housing was growing there. The Großsiedlung Siemensstadt, built between 1929 and 1931, is one of the great modernist housing estates of that decade, designed by a team that included Hans Scharoun, Walter Gropius, Hugo Häring, Otto Bartning and Fred Forbat. It is part of the UNESCO listing of Berlin Modernism Housing Estates, inscribed in 2008.

Why this ride

293 km from Roseburg to the finish, about 4 h 05 of riding. It goes by way of Toketee Falls and Diamond Lake. The highest point is about 1,700 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Roseburg

    Roseburg is the main town of the Umpqua valley in southern Oregon, with around twenty-two thousand people, an old timber economy and a position right where Interstate 5 crosses the South Umpqua River. For many years the city was proud to call itself the Timber Capital of the Nation, and mills, logging trucks and sawdust were everyday companions. The industry has shrunk, but the town still carries a rugged working feel, with a pleasant old downtown, murals, and several breweries.

  2. 2. Toketee Falls

    Toketee Falls drops about thirty-five metres in two steps over a wall of columnar basalt into a dark green pool, and it is one of the best waterfalls in Oregon. The North Umpqua River, which feeds it, runs through a narrow gorge of volcanic rock, and just above the falls it is squeezed into a channel so narrow that the whole river passes through a slot only a few metres wide. The columns of basalt, formed when lava cooled and cracked into hexagonal prisms, give the cliff the look of a giant organ pipe, and the water breaks around them in white threads.

  3. 3. Diamond Lake

    Mount Thielsen is the first thing you see, and it is hard to look at anything else. The mountain rises behind Diamond Lake in the Oregon Cascades, a needle of dark volcanic rock about 2,800 metres high, so sharp and so often struck by lightning that it is known as the lightning rod of the Cascades. It is an old volcano, eroded by glaciers until only the plug of hardened lava in the core remains. The lake sits at around 1,600 metres in a forested basin, with the broader bulk of Mount Bailey on its other side, and the water reflects both peaks on a calm morning.

  4. 4. Crater Lake

    Crater Lake does not look real the first time you see it. The water is a deep, saturated blue, almost unnaturally pure, and it fills a bowl nearly ten kilometres across, with steep walls rising some six hundred metres above the surface. It formed about 7,700 years ago, when the volcano Mount Mazama erupted so violently that it emptied its magma chamber and collapsed in on itself. Rain and snow slowly filled the caldera, and with no rivers flowing in or out, the water stayed exceptionally clear. At nearly six hundred metres, it is the deepest lake in the United States.

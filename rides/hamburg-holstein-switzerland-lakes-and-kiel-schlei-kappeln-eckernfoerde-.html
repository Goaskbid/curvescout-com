  1. 1. Bad Segeberg exit

    Bad Segeberg has a hill that should not be there. The Kalkberg rises out of the flat Holstein plain like a stray piece of the Alps, a block of limestone pushed upward by an underground salt dome, and on its flank there is an open-air arena where, every summer since 1952, the Karl May Festival has staged Wild West adventures with real horses, stuntmen and fireworks.

  2. 2. Plön lake road

    Plön sits on a ridge between two lakes in the part of eastern Holstein that the locals call the Holstein Switzerland, a name that sounds absurd until you see the rolling, wooded country and the lines of blue water lying between the hills. The Great Plön Lake is the biggest lake in Schleswig-Holstein, with islands, bays and many smaller lakes around it, and the town of Plön, with thirteen thousand people, occupies a narrow strip of land between them. The altitude here is only twenty-eight metres, but the glacial landscape gives it a lot of character.

  3. 3. Malente coffee

    The thing about Malente is that it is really two villages that grew into one, Malente proper and Gremsmühlen, with a short stretch of the Schwentine river between them. The Schwentine rises in the hills near the Bungsberg, flows through the chain of lakes here, and eventually reaches the Kiel Fjord some sixty kilometres to the north, and in Malente it takes on the character of a lazy canal, moving slowly under bridges and between villas with gardens that run down to the water. You can sit at a café by the bridge and watch small boats pass, with the quiet noise of oars and the occasional quack of a duck.

  4. 4. Eutin castle stop

    Eutin's castle is easier to enjoy from the water side. Walk through the gardens at the back and the park slopes down to the shore of the Great Eutin Lake, with an avenue of old trees, a curving gravel path, and swans sliding across the surface in a V. The landscape garden was laid out in the English style in the early nineteenth century and is still planted with fine old trees, among them oaks and beeches that are well over a century old. On summer evenings an open-air opera festival is staged on a stage at the water's edge, and the sound carries across the lake.

  6. 6. Kiel west exit

    Kiel is a city with a fjord at its heart, and the west side is where you most feel the practical, working character of the place. The Kiel Fjord cuts deep inland, giving the city a sheltered harbour, and the Kiel Canal begins at its western edge, so the neighbourhoods here are tied up with ships, locks and shipyards. This was the home of the German Imperial Navy in the nineteenth century, and the sailors' revolt in 1918 that helped end the First World War started here. The city was bombed almost flat in the Second World War, which is why so much of the centre is plain postwar architecture rather than old brick.

  7. 7. Eckernförde bay

    Eckernförde Bay is a long, narrow arm of the Baltic that cuts into the coast of Schleswig-Holstein between the Danish Wohld and the Schwansen peninsula, and the little port of the same name sits at its head. The water is shallow, the beaches are wide and sandy, and the whole place has the relaxed air of a north German seaside town, with a promenade, a harbour full of boats and a smell of smoked fish. The altitude here is a modest eight metres, and the horizon is all sea and sky.

  8. 8. Schwansen backroads

    Schwansen is a blunt thumb of land between the Schlei and the Bay of Eckernförde, and it is one of the most underrated corners of Schleswig-Holstein. The countryside is gently rolling farmland, divided into fields by Knicks, those hedge-topped earth banks that give the Baltic coast its distinctive patchwork, and dotted with old estates, thatched barns and brick villages. The sea is always near, on one side or the other, and glimpses of water through gaps in the hedge give the roads a pleasing sense of mystery.

  9. 9. Kappeln Schlei bridge

    Kappeln lies on the Schlei, a long narrow inlet of the Baltic that reaches forty-odd kilometres inland, like a fjord that forgot to be steep. The bridge itself is the town's emblem: a lifting bridge from the 1920s that opens to let ships through, so be prepared to wait a few minutes while the roadway rises and the sailing boats glide past.

  10. 10. Schleswig food stop

    Schleswig is a town you eat in with history around your ears. The quickest way to understand it is to walk to the harbour on the Schlei, where fish smokehouses and small restaurants sit beside a basin of yachts and the old cathedral spire rises over the roofs behind. Smoked herring, Schlei eel and fried plaice are all on offer, with potatoes and a pot of tea or coffee, and a table by the water on a warm day is hard to beat. Prices are fair, portions generous, and nobody hurries you.

  11. 11. Rendsburg canal check

    Rendsburg is a town made by a ditch, and what a ditch. The Kiel Canal, known in German as the Nord-Ostsee-Kanal, was opened in 1895 as the Kaiser-Wilhelm-Kanal, cutting across the base of the Danish peninsula so that ships need not sail all the way around Skagen. It is still the busiest artificial seaway in the world by number of ships, and standing on the bank at Rendsburg you can watch container vessels, tankers and ferries glide past at what looks like walking pace, a few metres from the road. A quiet fact makes it more striking: the surrounding land is so flat and low, only a few metres above sea level, that the ships seem to be sailing through meadows.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cefalù Cathedral

    Cefalù is where Sicily squeezes a medieval town between a rock and a beach. The Rocca, a limestone crag, rises a steep couple of hundred metres straight behind the old centre, and the cathedral sits at its foot, a heavy, golden-grey Norman building with two square towers that you can see from the coastal highway long before you reach the town. According to tradition King Roger II promised to build it after surviving a storm at sea, and work began in 1131. Whatever the truth of the vow, the church is a statement of power by a Norman king who ruled Sicily and wanted it to look Mediterranean, not merely northern.

  2. 2. Admiral’s Bridge

    The Ponte dell'Ammiraglio is an old bridge that has lost its river. It was built in 1131 by George of Antioch, the admiral of the Norman king Roger II of Sicily, a Greek Christian from Syria who rose to be the most powerful man in the kingdom, and it spans the Oreto, whose course has since been diverted, leaving the bridge standing over a dry bed among modern roads. Even so, its pointed arches, built of pale stone, are a fine example of the Arab-Norman style that flourished in Sicily, and it forms part of the UNESCO listing of Arab-Norman Palermo and the cathedrals of Cefalù and Monreale, inscribed in 2015.

  3. 3. Archaeological Area of Agrigento

    The Valley of the Temples is not really a valley at all, but a long ridge running parallel to the Sicilian south coast, with the sea shining a few kilometres to the south and modern Agrigento climbing the hill behind. The Greeks called their city Akragas. It was founded around 580 BC by settlers from Gela and became one of the great cities of the Greek west, rich enough that the philosopher Empedocles, who lived here, is said to have remarked that the citizens built as if they would live forever and ate as if they would die tomorrow.

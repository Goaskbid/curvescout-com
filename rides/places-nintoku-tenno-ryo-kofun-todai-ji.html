Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Nintoku-tenno-ryo Kofun

    Nintoku's tomb is the biggest grave in Japan, and by area it ranks among the largest tombs ever built anywhere. It is a keyhole-shaped mound nearly five hundred metres long, ringed by three moats, rising out of the middle of the city of Sakai, and the Imperial Household Agency attributes it to the emperor Nintoku, who is said to have ruled in the fifth century. Archaeologists are less sure about the name, which is why scholars often call it Daisen Kofun, after the neighbourhood. Whatever the truth, the labour that went into it must have been prodigious, with estimates of years of work by thousands of people.

  2. 2. Buddhist Monuments in the Horyu-ji Area

    If you want to stand in front of the oldest wooden buildings in the world, this is the place. Hōryū-ji, a Buddhist temple in the town of Ikaruga south-west of Nara, was founded by Prince Shōtoku in the early seventh century, and although it burned and was rebuilt not long after, its central buildings, including the main hall and the five-storey pagoda, date from around the end of the seventh century. They were among the first sites in Japan inscribed by UNESCO, in 1993.

  3. 3. Tôdai-ji

    Nothing quite prepares you for the scale of Tōdai-ji. You walk through the great Nandaimon gate, past two fierce wooden guardian kings carved in the thirteenth century by Unkei, Kaikei and their workshop, and there it is: the Daibutsuden, the Great Buddha Hall, for centuries the largest wooden building in the world, even though the current structure, rebuilt in the early eighteenth century, is smaller than the original. Inside sits the Daibutsu, a bronze Buddha some fifteen metres tall, commissioned by Emperor Shōmu and consecrated in 752 with a ceremony that drew monks from as far away as India. The temple is part of the UNESCO listing of Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara.

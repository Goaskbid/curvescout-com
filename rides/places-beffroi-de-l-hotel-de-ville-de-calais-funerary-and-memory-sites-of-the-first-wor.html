Why this ride

From Calais it runs 390 km to Calais; allow about 6 h 43 of riding. The stops in between: Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Boulogne, Beffroi de Gravelines, Landhuis met Belfort and Funerary and memory sites of the First Wor. It crosses from France into Belgium - carry ID and check the toll rules. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

The stops

  1. 1. Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Calais

    Start here.

    Most riders know Calais as the place where the ferry or the tunnel train spits them out onto French tarmac, and they head south without a backward glance. Spare it an hour. The town hall is a surprise, a grand building in Flemish Renaissance style finished in the early twentieth century, and its belfry, one of the tallest in the region, rises well above the rooftops as a landmark you can see from the port. It forms part of the UNESCO list of belfries in Belgium and France, alongside towers that are centuries older.

    Read the complete Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Calais write-up.

  2. 2. Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Boulogne

    About 41 km in.

    Boulogne-sur-Mer has two faces: the busy fishing port down by the Liane estuary, the largest in France, and the Haute-Ville on the hill above, enclosed by thirteenth-century ramparts that you can walk all the way around. The belfry stands in the upper town, attached to the town hall, its lower part dating from the thirteenth century and the upper stages added later. It is one of the belfries of Belgium and France listed by UNESCO, symbols of the freedom of medieval towns, which rang their bells to call citizens to arms, to work, or to assembly. You can climb it for a view over the rooftops, the port and the Channel.

    Read the complete Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Boulogne write-up.

  3. 3. Beffroi de Gravelines

    About 103 km in.

    Gravelines sits right where the river Aa meets the Channel, between Calais and Dunkirk, and it still wears its walls. The town is wrapped in Vauban-era fortifications, a star of brick ramparts, bastions and water-filled moats that you can walk or ride around, and in the middle of it rises the belfry, one of the towers listed by UNESCO among the belfries of northern France and Belgium. It is a solid, sober tower rather than a showpiece, built to watch, warn and ring the hours for a frontier town that changed hands more than once.

    Read the complete Beffroi de Gravelines write-up.

  4. 4. Landhuis met Belfort

    About 155 km in.

    Veurne's market square is one of the most complete in Flanders, and the old Landhuis, with its belfry rising behind it, is the reason. The Landhuis was the seat of the castellany, the regional administration that governed the surrounding countryside, and its belfry is part of the group of Belgian and French belfries protected by UNESCO. Around the square stand the town hall, the old meat hall and a row of gabled houses, many dating back to the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when Veurne was under Spanish rule, which explains the stepped gables and ornate facades.

    Read the complete Landhuis met Belfort write-up.

  5. 5. Funerary and memory sites of the First Wor

    About 194 km in.

    Every evening at eight o'clock, traffic stops under the Menin Gate in Ypres, and buglers from the local fire brigade sound the Last Post in memory of the soldiers of the British Empire who died in the Ypres Salient during the First World War. The ceremony has been held almost every evening since 1928, interrupted only during the German occupation in the Second World War, and it draws crowds of all ages, many of them descendants of the fallen. The gate itself is inscribed with the names of more than fifty thousand soldiers whose bodies were never found. It is one of the sites included in the World Heritage inscription of the Funerary and Memory Sites of the First World War, inscribed in 2023.

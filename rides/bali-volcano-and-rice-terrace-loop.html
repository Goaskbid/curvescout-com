Why this ride

Out of Ubud and back again: 75 km, roughly 1 h 13 in the saddle. It goes by way of Tegallalang, Kintamani, Mount Batur and Besakih. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Ubud

    Ubud is the place where Bali's rice paddies meet its art. Set in the hills at the island's centre, the town grew from a royal village into a centre for painters, dancers and carvers in the 1920s and 1930s, when European artists such as Walter Spies, a German, and Rudolf Bonnet, a Dutchman, settled here and encouraged local painters to explore new styles. The old royal palace, the Puri Saren, still hosts dance performances in the evenings, with gamelan music and elaborate costumes.

  2. 2. Tegallalang

    Few landscapes in Indonesia are as photographed as the rice terraces of Tegallalang, a few kilometres north of Ubud, where the green steps climb both sides of a river valley in a pattern as precise as a staircase. The terraces are not for show. They are part of a system called subak, an ancient method of irrigation and communal water management that dates back about a thousand years, and which is run by farmers' associations according to the Balinese Hindu philosophy of harmony between people, nature and the gods. UNESCO listed the subak landscape as a World Heritage Site in 2012, and the fields here are a living example.

  3. 3. Kintamani

    Kintamani sits on the rim of an enormous volcanic caldera, at around fifteen hundred metres, and the air is noticeably cooler than on the coast. The caldera is about ten kilometres across, a vast bowl formed by massive eruptions in prehistoric times, and inside it stand the cone of Mount Batur, still active, and the crescent-shaped Lake Batur, dark blue under the cliffs. The view from the rim at Penelokan, which means a place to look, is one of the great sights of Bali, and on a clear morning it is spectacular.

  4. 4. Mount Batur viewpoint

    Mount Batur is a volcano with a short temper. It rises to 1,717 metres from the floor of a vast caldera, beside the crescent of Lake Batur, and it has erupted many times in recorded history, leaving black fields of cooled lava on its flanks. Smoke and steam still seep from vents near the summit, and the ground in places is warm to the touch. The volcano is a place of deep significance to the Balinese, who believe that its spirit controls the water that feeds the island's rice terraces, and offerings are made to the lake goddess at the temple on the shore.

  5. 5. Besakih

    Besakih is the Mother Temple of Bali, the largest and holiest Hindu temple on the island, and it sits high on the southern slope of Mount Agung, the great volcano that dominates the east. It is not a single building but a complex of about twenty-three separate temples, spread across a series of terraces, linked by stairways and gateways. The split gates, called candi bentar, frame the view upward towards the mountain, and the multi-tiered thatched towers, called meru, rise in odd numbers, up to eleven roofs, each dedicated to a Hindu deity or an ancestor. The oldest parts may date back many centuries, and the complex is still in daily use for ceremonies.

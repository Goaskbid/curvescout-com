Why this ride

From Bussigny it runs 494 km to Zürich; allow about 7 h 01 of riding. The stops in between: Jura, Basel, Aarau and Lenzburg. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

  1. 1. Neuchâtel

    The label says Neuchâtel, but you are not at the lake. Neuchâtel, with its castle and its long, pale blue lake, is a good forty kilometres to the north-east, past the first folds of the Jura. This is the landscape on the way, a rolling plateau of farms, orchards and beech woods, where the road to Neuchâtel begins.

  3. 3. Basel

    Despite the label, this is Biel, or Bienne in French, a bilingual city of nearly fifty thousand at the northern tip of Lake Biel, and one of the most underrated towns in Switzerland. It sits where the lake meets the first long ridge of the Jura, and its centre is a mixture of a medieval old town, with a fountain-filled square and half-timbered houses, and the clean, grid-like modernism of the twentieth-century town that grew up around the watch factories.

  4. 4. Aarau

    Again the label misleads: this is Basel, the Swiss city where the Rhine turns north, and its position at the meeting of three countries makes it feel more European than most. France and Germany both begin within a few kilometres of the city centre, and the river carries barges, tourists and swimmers through the heart of town. In summer, Baslers float down the river in the current with their clothes in waterproof bags, and the riverbanks fill with picnics and music.

  5. 5. Near Lenzburg

    Schloss Lenzburg crowns a rounded hill above the town, so prominent that you can see it from miles away on the motorway, and it has been watching over this part of the Aargau since the eleventh century. The castle has passed through the hands of counts, the Habsburgs, the city of Bern and private owners, and today it is a museum, with gardens and views over the Seetal and the Jura.

  6. 6. Zürich

    Zurich is the polite, prosperous kind of city that keeps its history discreet. It lies at the northern end of Lake Zurich, where the Limmat river runs out of the lake and through the old town, and it began as a Roman customs post called Turicum on the hill now known as the Lindenhof. By the sixteenth century it was a centre of the Reformation under Huldrych Zwingli, who preached at the Grossmunster, the twin-towered church that still dominates the skyline. Across the river, the Fraumunster holds stained glass windows by Marc Chagall, and the light inside on a bright morning is worth a short detour.

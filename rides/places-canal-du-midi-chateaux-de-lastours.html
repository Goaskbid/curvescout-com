Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Canal du Midi

    The Canal du Midi has a reputation for being slow, and the stretch within Toulouse is no exception. The canal was dug in the seventeenth century by Pierre-Paul Riquet, a tax collector turned engineer, who spent most of his fortune and the last years of his life on a waterway that joined the Atlantic to the Mediterranean through the pass at Naurouze. He died in 1680, a few months before the water finally flowed all the way through. Today the canal is a UNESCO World Heritage site, its banks lined with plane trees whose branches meet overhead.

  2. 2. Episcopal City of Albi

    Albi is built of brick, which is unusual in the south of France, and the colour changes through the day, from salmon in the morning to a deep red at dusk. The great cathedral of Sainte-Cécile dominates the old town, and from a distance it looks more like a fortress than a church, with a massive tower and walls that have no windows at the bottom. It was begun in the late thirteenth century, soon after the crusade against the Cathars had been won, and its builders wanted the faithful, and the heretics, to see where power lay. Inside, the painted vault and a huge fresco of the Last Judgement are astonishing. The whole episcopal city, with the old bridge and the bishop's palace, was listed by UNESCO in 2010.

  3. 3. Châteaux de Lastours

    Four castles perch on a narrow rock ridge above the village of Lastours, in the Montagne Noire north of Carcassonne, and they look so improbable from the viewpoint across the valley that most people stop talking when they see them. The two streams, the Orbiel and the Grésilhou, wrap around the ridge, and the castles, Cabaret, Tour Régine, Surdespine and Quertinheux, stand along its length like beads on a string.

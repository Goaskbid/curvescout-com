Why this ride

curated scenic road. Highlands, lochs, mountain valleys. From Glasgow it runs 223 km to Inverness; allow about 4 h 03 of riding. The stops in between: Helensburgh, A82 Glencoe and Great Glen, Fort William and Drumnadrochit. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

  1. 1. Glasgow

    Glasgow starts the A82 journey, and it is a city that deserves a look before you leave. The Clyde, which built the city's wealth through shipbuilding, runs through the south, and the cathedral and Necropolis, a Victorian cemetery on a hill, lie to the east.

  2. 2. Near Helensburgh

    Luss is the village that appears on postcards of Loch Lomond: a row of neat stone and slate cottages with flower gardens, a small church among old trees and a pier stretching out into the loch, with Ben Lomond rising on the far shore. The cottages were built in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries by the local landowners, the Colquhouns, for workers in the slate quarries and the cotton mill nearby, and the village is now a conservation area, carefully kept.

  3. 3. A82 Glencoe and Great Glen point 3

    Rannoch Moor is a landscape that makes people go quiet. After the wooded shores of Loch Lomond and the winding glens, the A82 climbs onto an enormous, bare plateau of peat bog, scattered boulders and black pools, with the mountains standing back at the edges like spectators. It is among the wildest places in Britain, a bowl of wilderness about fifty square miles in extent, and the road crosses it on a raised causeway.

  4. 4. Near Fort William

    Glencoe village sits at the mouth of the glen, where the River Coe runs into Loch Leven, a small cluster of white houses, a church and a few shops below mountains that rise steeply on either side. Behind it the glen stretches east toward Rannoch Moor, one of the most famous and haunting landscapes in Scotland, with peaks like the Three Sisters and Buachaille Etive Mòr standing guard over the road.

  5. 5. Fort William

    Fort William is a working town with a mountain behind it, and the mountain is Ben Nevis. At 1,345 metres, it is the highest summit in the British Isles, and from the streets of the town you may or may not see it, depending on the cloud, which hides the top for most of the year. The town itself is not beautiful, a long, practical line of shops, hotels and outdoor stores along the shore of Loch Linnhe, but it sits in an extraordinary landscape of lochs, glens and peaks, and it is a good base for exploring the western Highlands.

  6. 6. Near Drumnadrochit

    Urquhart Castle stands on a rocky point on the northern shore of Loch Ness, a ruin of grey stone walls and a tall tower house looking out over the dark water. It is one of the largest castles in Scotland, and its history is long and violent: it was fought over by the Scots and the English in the Wars of Independence, held and lost by various clans, and finally blown up in 1692 by government troops to stop it being used by the Jacobites. What remains is a romantic shell, with the tower still standing and the views from the top stretching up and down the loch.

  7. 7. Inverness

    Inverness is where the North Coast 500 starts and ends, the circuit that has turned the top of Scotland into a bucket-list road, and the sensible traveller treats the city as a place to arrive, wash, shop and sleep before heading out. It sits where the River Ness meets the Moray Firth, and for centuries it was the capital of the Highlands in practice, a market town at the junction of roads running north, west and south. The population of about forty-seven thousand makes it by far the largest settlement for a hundred miles in any direction, and that means all the fuel, tyres, spares and supermarkets you could want.

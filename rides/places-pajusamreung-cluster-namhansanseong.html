Why this ride

A 183 km round trip from Gwangtan - roughly 4 h 19 in the saddle. On the way it passes Jangneung Cluster, Changdeokgung Palace Complex, Namhansanseong and back through Changdeokgung Palace Complex. The line favours views over speed.

The roads

Jangneung Cluster → Changdeokgung Palace Complex

It is 32 km from Jangneung Cluster to Changdeokgung Palace Complex, mostly on 자유로. Hardly any climbing; it runs between 11 and 53 m. This is the bit the ride is built around.

The stops

  1. 1. Pajusamreung Cluster

    Start here.

    Close to the most heavily fortified border in the world, three royal tombs of the Joseon dynasty lie in a quiet pine forest in Paju, north of Seoul. Pajusamreung, the three tombs of Paju, include the resting places of Queen Jangsun and Queen Gonghye, both married to kings and both of whom died young, and of King Jinjong, honoured after his early death. Like all the Joseon royal tombs, they follow a precise layout of spirit gates, stone paths, shrines and grass mounds guarded by stone officials and animals, and they are part of the World Heritage listing of the Royal Tombs of the Joseon Dynasty.

    Read the complete Pajusamreung Cluster write-up.

  2. 2. Jangneung Cluster

    31 km in.

    The Joseon dynasty ruled Korea for over five hundred years, and its kings and queens were buried in carefully chosen spots around the capital, in tombs designed according to Confucian ritual and the principles of geomancy, with a hill behind, water in front and mountains framing the view. In 2009, forty of these tombs were listed by UNESCO as the Royal Tombs of the Joseon Dynasty. Jangneung, in Gimpo, just west of Seoul, is the tomb of Wonjong, the father of King Injo, who was elevated to royal rank after his death. It is a quiet, wooded place, with a grassy burial mound, stone statues of officials and animals, and a T-shaped shrine where ancestral rites were held.

    Read the complete Jangneung Cluster write-up.

  3. 3. Changdeokgung Palace Complex

    63 km in.

    Changdeokgung was built in 1405 as a secondary palace for the Joseon kings, and it ended up being the one they actually liked living in. Where the main palace, Gyeongbokgung, was laid out on a strict axis, Changdeokgung was fitted into the foot of the hills, following the folds of the ground, and its halls, gates and courtyards feel more relaxed and more human as a result. After the Japanese invasions of the 1590s burned all of Seoul's palaces, this was the first to be rebuilt, and it served as the main royal residence for some two and a half centuries.

    Read the complete Changdeokgung Palace Complex write-up.

  4. 4. Namhansanseong

    94 km in.

    Just beyond the south-eastern edge of Seoul, the city stops abruptly against wooded mountains, and on top of them runs a stone wall with gates, watchtowers and command posts. Namhansanseong was built as an emergency capital, a mountain fortress where the king and court could retreat in time of invasion, and in the winter of 1636 to 1637 it was put to the test. King Injo fled here from the invading Qing army and held out for forty-seven days before hunger and the fall of other strongholds forced him to surrender. The fortress became a World Heritage Site in 2014.

    Read the complete Namhansanseong write-up.

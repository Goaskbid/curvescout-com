Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Heidelberg

    Heidelberg is the place that German Romantics made famous and Mark Twain made funny. The Neckar comes out of its wooded valley here and slips past a steep old town, a stone bridge with a gate, and above everything the red sandstone ruins of the castle. Visitors have been climbing up to look at it since the early nineteenth century, when poets and painters arrived and decided it was the most beautiful ruin in Germany. The damage was done by French troops in the 1680s and 1690s, in a war of succession, and a lightning strike finished the job later, so it was never a romantic wreck by design.

  2. 2. Speyer Cathedral

    Some buildings impress you with height, others with mass. Speyer Cathedral does it with both, rising from the left bank of the Rhine with six towers and a long, rhythmic nave of red sandstone. It was begun around 1030 by the emperor Conrad II, the first of the Salian dynasty, and is generally considered the largest surviving Romanesque church in the world. Its crypt, a forest of sturdy columns and striped arches, holds the tombs of several emperors and kings, which tells you how central this town once was to the Holy Roman Empire.

  4. 4. ShUM Sites of Speyer

    Speyer was one of the great Jewish centres of medieval Europe, and the evidence is still standing in the Judenhof, the old Jewish courtyard just behind the cathedral. In the early twelfth century a synagogue was built here, and beside it a ritual bath, the mikveh, reached by a narrow stair down into the cool, damp earth. Together with the sister communities of Worms and Mainz, the three towns formed a league known by the initials of their Hebrew names, ShUM, and UNESCO listed the sites in 2021. The bath is among the oldest and best preserved in Europe, and descending into it is a quiet, slightly chilling experience.

  5. 5. Speyer Jewry-Court

    Speyer is known for its cathedral, a huge Romanesque church where several medieval emperors are buried, but a few hundred metres away lies a quieter site that matters just as much. The Judenhof, the Jewry Court, was the centre of the medieval Jewish community, and it contains the ruins of a synagogue from the early twelfth century and a mikveh, a ritual bath, that is generally regarded as the oldest in Europe. The mikveh is reached by steep steps down into a cool, vaulted chamber where groundwater still collects. It is a remarkable place, and the stone is beautifully worked.

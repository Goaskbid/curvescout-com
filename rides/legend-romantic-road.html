Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Würzburg

    Würzburg lies on the Main in Lower Franconia, in the middle of a landscape of vineyards, and it is the northern start of the Romantic Road, the touring route that runs through Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg to Füssen in the Alps. The city has around a hundred and thirty thousand inhabitants, a university that dates back to the fifteenth century and a great Baroque palace, the Residenz, built in the eighteenth century for the prince-bishops by the architect Balthasar Neumann. Its staircase has a ceiling fresco by Giovanni Battista Tiepolo, said to be the largest in the world, and the whole building is a World Heritage site.

  2. 2. Tauberbischofsheim

    Tauberbischofsheim has produced more Olympic fencers than most countries. The town in the Tauber valley is Germany's fencing capital, with a club and training centre that has trained generations of champions, among them Thomas Bach, who won gold in the team foil at the 1976 Olympics and later became president of the International Olympic Committee. In a quiet place, the occasional clink of blades from a training hall is a surprise.

  3. 3. Rothenburg ob der Tauber

    Rothenburg ob der Tauber looks like a film set because, for a long time, it had no choice. The medieval town stands on a ridge above the Tauber valley in Franconia, enclosed by an almost complete ring of walls with towers and gates, and its timber houses, steep roofs and cobbled lanes have survived because the town lost its wealth after the Thirty Years War and could not afford to rebuild. It was too poor to modernise, and so it stayed old. Today it draws visitors in large numbers, and the main streets can be packed with tour groups by mid-morning.

  4. 4. Dinkelsbühl

    Dinkelsbühl is the one that makes people stop talking mid-sentence when they ride through the gate. A complete medieval wall rings the old town, with towers, a moat of lawns and a maze of lanes of gabled houses painted in butter and rose, and the whole thing came through the Second World War largely untouched. It lies on the Wörnitz in western Bavaria, a town of about twelve thousand people on the Romantic Road, which makes it a natural place to start or end a day on that famous route.

  5. 5. Nördlingen

    Nördlingen sits in the middle of a hole in the ground, and the hole was made from space. About fourteen and a half million years ago, a meteorite crashed into this part of southern Germany and left a crater about twenty-five kilometres wide, the Ries, now a gently bowl-shaped plain of fields, villages and low hills. Nördlingen was built inside it, in a perfect circle, and the medieval wall that surrounds it is almost round, the only fully preserved ring wall in Germany that you can walk along the top of. The walk takes about an hour.

  6. 6. Augsburg

    Augsburg is older than almost everything else on the Romantic Road, and it knows it. The Romans founded a camp here in 15 BC and named it after Emperor Augustus, and for much of the Middle Ages it was one of the richest cities in Europe, thanks to the Fugger and Welser banking families, who lent money to emperors and popes. The Fuggerei, a walled quarter of small yellow houses built by Jakob Fugger in 1521, is the world's oldest social housing complex still in use, and the annual rent is famously less than a euro.

  8. 8. Füssen

    Füssen is the southern end of the Romantic Road, a sunny little town on the Lech river, a stone's throw from the Austrian border and with the Alps standing straight above its rooftops. It has about fourteen thousand people and a history stretching back to the Romans, who built a road here, the Via Claudia Augusta, to carry troops over the Alps. The old town is tidy, with arcaded streets and painted facades, and the river tumbles over a small waterfall at the edge of the centre.

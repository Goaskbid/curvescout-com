Why this ride

The stops in between: Eureka and Crescent City. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Avenue of the Giants

    The Avenue of the Giants is a thirty-one-mile stretch of old highway that runs parallel to Highway 101 through the Humboldt Redwoods State Park, and the moment you turn onto it the light changes. The trees are coast redwoods, the tallest living things on the planet, with trunks as wide as a small room and tops that disappear a hundred metres overhead. The air is cool and damp, the forest floor is thick with ferns and sorrel, and sound seems to vanish into the huge trunks. It is the sort of place where riders instinctively lower the revs.

  2. 2. Eureka

    Eureka is the biggest town on California's north coast, a place of about twenty-seven thousand people on the shores of Humboldt Bay, and it is a very good place to stop and refuel before or after the Avenue of the Giants. The town grew rich on redwood in the late nineteenth century, when lumber barons built ornate houses and shipped their cargoes down the coast to San Francisco. The finest of them is the Carson Mansion, a gabled, towered Victorian pile that stands on a corner near the water and is probably the most photographed private house in California.

  3. 3. Crescent City

    Crescent City is the last real town on the California coast before Oregon, and it carries its history in an unusual way. In March 1964, the great Alaska earthquake sent a series of tsunamis racing across the Pacific, and the fourth wave, arriving hours after the first, struck Crescent City with devastating force, destroying much of the downtown and killing eleven people. It remains the worst tsunami disaster in the contiguous United States, and tsunami evacuation signs are posted along the roads. The harbour was rebuilt, and today it serves a fleet of fishing boats and a population of about six and a half thousand.

  4. 4. Brookings

    Brookings is the first Oregon town you meet coming up Highway 101 from California, and it greets you with unusual warmth. A quirk of the terrain and the warm winds that come down the Chetco River valley make it the mildest spot on the Oregon coast, nicknamed the banana belt, with winter temperatures that are several degrees above anywhere nearby. That climate suits flowers. More than ninety per cent of the Easter lily bulbs grown in the country come from fields around here, and in early summer the farms along the road are a sea of white trumpets.

Why this ride

From Sevilla it runs 819 km to Gijón; allow about 9 h 47 of riding. It goes by way of Merida, Caceres, Salamanca and Zamora. It tops out around 1,100 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

The stops

  1. 1. Seville

    Start here.

    The Ruta de la Plata sounds like a silver road, and generations of travellers have assumed it carried silver from the Americas. In fact the name probably comes from the Arabic word balata, meaning a paved way, and the road itself is much older: a Roman route running north from Seville to Mérida and on to Astorga, which pilgrims and drovers kept using for two thousand years. Seville is where it begins, and the city makes a fine place to start any ride.

    Read the complete Seville write-up.

  2. 2. Mérida

    191 km in.

    In the year 25 BC the Romans founded a colony here for veterans of their wars in Hispania, named it Emerita Augusta, and made it the capital of the province of Lusitania. Two thousand years later, Mérida has more Roman remains than anywhere else in Spain, and they are not roped off in a museum. They are in the streets. A long Roman bridge, around eight hundred metres, still crosses the broad Guadiana on dozens of arches and is walked daily by locals. A theatre and an amphitheatre sit side by side near the centre, and in summer actors perform classical plays in the theatre in the open air. On the edge of town the tall brick piers of an aqueduct, called Los Milagros, the miracles, march across a field.

    Read the complete Merida write-up.

  3. 3. Cáceres

    266 km in.

    Cáceres has two faces. The modern city spreads over the plain with shopping streets, apartment blocks and ring roads, and it is a normal busy Spanish provincial capital. Inside it, behind medieval walls, lies the old quarter, which is one of the best-preserved in Europe and has been a World Heritage Site since 1986. Here stand towers, palaces and churches of golden stone, built between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries by noble families who had made money in the wars of the reconquest and later in the Americas. Many of the towers were cut down to the level of the roofs on the order of the Catholic Monarchs, who wanted to curb the power of the nobility, and the stumps are still visible.

    Read the complete Caceres write-up.

  4. 4. Salamanca

    468 km in.

    Salamanca glows. Its buildings are built from a golden sandstone that takes on a honey colour in low sun, and the effect, especially in the late afternoon in the Plaza Mayor, is memorable. The city stands on the Tormes river in western Spain, about 800 metres above sea level, and is one of the great university towns of Europe, with a student population that gives the old streets life well into the night.

    Read the complete Salamanca write-up.

  5. 5. Zamora

    533 km in.

    Zamora is a city built on a rock above the Duero, and it is overlooked in a way that suits its quiet temperament. The old town sits on a ridge on the north bank, with a medieval stone bridge reaching across the river, and the whole place feels slightly apart from the rest of the busy plain around it. It is said to have more Romanesque churches than any other city in Europe, about two dozen, built mostly in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries when Zamora was a frontier town between the Christian north and the Moorish south. Their rounded arches and plain stone walls give the streets a sober, weathered look that is especially fine in evening light.

    Read the complete Zamora write-up.

  6. 6. León

    675 km in.

    León has been a city for nearly two thousand years, since a Roman legion made camp here, and the old rectangle of the Roman walls is still partly visible in the heart of the town. The name itself comes from the legion, not from the lion that now sits on the city's flag, though locals prefer the lion. In the Middle Ages León was the capital of a kingdom, and later it became a major stop on the pilgrim road to Santiago, so the old quarter is full of churches, monasteries and inns that pilgrims used for rest.

    Read the complete Leon write-up.

  7. 7. Gijón

    The end: 819 km.

    Gijón stands on a rocky headland on the Bay of Biscay, in the heart of Asturias, and the Ruta de la Plata, which began far to the south in the hot plains of Extremadura, ends here with a gust of Atlantic air. The route follows an old road whose name comes from the Arabic word for a paved way, and which carried Roman legions, medieval pilgrims and merchants between the south and the north of Spain. After the dry high country of the meseta, arriving at the cold, green coast is like entering another country, and in a sense it is.

    Read the complete Gijon write-up.

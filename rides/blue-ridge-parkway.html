Why this ride

From Waynesboro it runs 1390 km to Cherokee; allow about 19 h 24 of riding. The stops in between: Peaks of Otter, Mabry Mill, Doughton Park and Linn Cove Viaduct. The highest point is about 1,150 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Roadbook

  1. Shenandoah Rockfish Gap start

    Rockfish Gap is a mountain pass in name only, a low saddle in the Blue Ridge where Interstate 64, the town of Waynesboro, and two of the most famous scenic roads in America all meet. To the north runs Skyline Drive through Shenandoah National Park, and to the south the Blue Ridge Parkway begins its long, curving journey, about 470 miles in all, toward the Great Smoky Mountains.

    The Parkway was begun in the 1930s as a public works project during the Depression, and workers built it by hand and with simple machines along the ridgelines, so that every bend was designed to show a view. The idea was a road for driving slowly, which is why the speed limit hardly ever exceeds 45 miles per hour, commercial traffic is banned, and there are no traffic lights or billboards. It is the longest linear park in the US.

    For a rider, that slow pace is the point. The first stretch south of the gap is rolling, wooded and calm, with overlooks facing west across the Shenandoah Valley and east over the Piedmont. Humpback Rocks, a few miles on, has a farm museum and a short, steep hike to a rocky viewpoint. In spring, flowering dogwoods and rhododendron turn the roadside white and pink, and in October, the maples and oaks give the ridge one of the best displays of colour on the east coast. Fog is common, and it can be thick, so a clear visor and low speed matter.

    Waynesboro is the practical base. It has fuel, restaurants and motels, and you should fill your tank here, since there are very few fuel stops directly along the Parkway, and you may need to leave the road to find one. Mabry Mill, further down at about milepost 176, is a photogenic old gristmill with a restaurant famous for its buckwheat pancakes, and many riders time their day to reach it for breakfast.

    The history of the gap itself includes a gathering in 1818 where commissioners picked the site for the University of Virginia, a lasting contribution from this small pass. Winter ice can close sections of the Parkway at short notice, so check the park's road status before you set off between November and March.

  2. Peaks of Otter scenic

    Three rounded summits stand out of the Virginia Blue Ridge here, and the Parkway slides in beneath them beside a small lake that mirrors the lot. Sharp Top is the pointed one, at around 1,150 metres, with a bare rock crown that you can reach on foot or by a seasonal shuttle bus; Flat Top is slightly higher and, true to its name, flatter; Harkening Hill makes up the trio. The Peaks of Otter area is one of the most loved stops on the whole Blue Ridge Parkway, and it is easy to see why, because the setting is compact, green and calm in a way many bigger overlooks are not.

    Getting here from Rockfish Gap is the real reward. The Blue Ridge Parkway is a ridge road designed for slow travel, with a 45 mph limit, no commercial traffic and no intersections to speak of, and it follows the crest of the mountains with long, rhythmic bends and constant views down into farmland and out toward the next ridge. The surface is generally good, though tree roots and frost can leave patches rough, and fog is common, sometimes thick enough that the dividing line disappears. In autumn the hardwoods go orange and red, and the parkway fills with leaf-watchers, so aim for a weekday. Deer step out without warning, especially at dusk.

    The place has a human story too. There was a farm in the hollow in the nineteenth century, the Johnson Farm, which you can visit, and a small inn has stood near the lake for generations, serving meals to travellers. Bedford, down in the valley about fourteen kilometres away, is a gentle town best known for the National D-Day Memorial, built there because Bedford lost proportionally more young men on the Normandy beaches than almost any other American community. It is a quiet, moving place and worth an hour if you have the time.

    There is a lodge and restaurant at Peaks of Otter and a campground nearby, and a visitor centre with maps and displays on the natural history. A short loop around the lake takes only ten minutes and gives a nice reflection of Sharp Top in the water. Pack a warm layer, because even in summer the mountain air is cool, and in spring and autumn a sudden drop in temperature can arrive at sunset.

  3. Mabry Mill scenic

    Mabry Mill is the most photographed spot on the Blue Ridge Parkway, and with good reason. At milepost 176, the old red mill with its great wooden water wheel stands beside a millpond, framed by rhododendron and maple, and in autumn the reflection of the colours in the water is a postcard in itself. Edwin Boone Mabry built the mill in the early 1900s, using a gristmill, a sawmill and a blacksmith shop to serve his neighbours, and it stayed in use until the 1930s. Today the buildings are preserved by the National Park Service, and in the summer costumed demonstrators grind corn and work the forge.

    The mill sits on the Rocky Knob plateau, in the part of Virginia known as the Meadows of Dan, a high, rolling country of farms and woods in the foothills of the Blue Ridge. The area is rich in mountain music, with banjos, fiddles and flatpicking guitars, and the Crooked Road, Virginia's heritage music trail, runs through the nearby town of Floyd, where a country store hosts a Friday night jamboree that draws crowds from all over the region. The Parkway itself, built in the 1930s and completed in 1987, runs for 469 miles along the crest of the Appalachians from Virginia to North Carolina, and it is the most visited unit in the national park system.

    The riding is among the best in the East. The Parkway has a speed limit of 45 miles an hour, with long, graceful curves, wide views and no commercial traffic or traffic lights, and the surface is smooth and clean. Deer, wild turkeys and even bears wander across the road, particularly at dawn and dusk. Fog is common and can come down suddenly, and in spring and autumn, ice can form on the road in the morning, and sections close in winter. The best time is October, for colour, but the crowds are heaviest then, so weekdays are best.

    The mill has a restaurant, famous for its buckwheat and cornmeal pancakes with sorghum syrup, and the queues build up by midmorning, so come early. Mount Airy, thirty-three kilometres away, the town that inspired Mayberry in The Andy Griffith Show, is a bigger place with every service.

    Take a stroll around the pond and watch the wheel turn. It is a quiet, old-fashioned corner of America, and it is easy to see why people keep coming back.

  4. Doughton Park scenic

    Doughton Park is a high, open stretch of the Blue Ridge Parkway where the forest gives way to long grassy balds and the whole of the North Carolina Piedmont seems to lie below you. It is the biggest recreation area on the northern half of the Parkway, with an altitude of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 metres, and it is named for Robert Doughton, a congressman from the area known as Muley Bob, who helped push the Parkway through Congress in the 1930s. The Parkway itself, built between 1935 and 1987, is the longest linear park in the United States, a ribbon of road that follows the crest of the southern Appalachians for nearly 470 miles.

    The park has a gentle, pastoral feel. Old pastures, stone walls and split-rail fences recall the mountain farms that once covered the area, and the Brinegar Cabin, a small log home at the park's edge, has been preserved to show how a family lived and worked here, with a weaving demonstration in summer. Walking trails lead out along the ridge to overlooks with broad views, and in late spring the rhododendrons and azaleas are in bloom. In autumn, the hillsides turn orange and red, and the park gets busier.

    Riding the Parkway is a particular pleasure, and a particular discipline. The speed limit is 45 miles an hour, and rangers enforce it, with no commercial traffic, no signs or billboards, and no stoplights. The road curves gently with the contours of the ridge, and the surface is mostly smooth, with long views and plenty of overlooks. The main hazards are fog, which can come in thick on the ridges, slow-moving cars, deer, and wet leaves in autumn. Parts of the Parkway close in winter for ice and snow, and landslides or storms can close other sections, so check the National Park Service for the current status before you go.

    A lodge and restaurant in the park are sometimes open in the season, serving hearty Southern cooking like fried chicken, country ham and blackberry cobbler, but hours are limited, and it is wise to bring snacks and water. Mornings are often cool and misty, with the sun burning through by noon.

  5. Linn Cove Viaduct scenic

    The Linn Cove Viaduct is the last piece of the Blue Ridge Parkway to be finished, and probably the most ingenious. For decades, the Parkway had a gap on the side of Grandfather Mountain, because the owner of the land refused to allow a conventional road to scar the slope. The solution, completed in 1983, was a curving concrete bridge, about 380 metres long, that twists along the mountainside on slender piers, and was built from the top down, so that no heavy machinery had to touch the forest below. Its 153 precast segments, each slightly different, were lifted into place one by one. When it opened, the whole Parkway, begun in the 1930s, was finally complete, in 1987, after more than fifty years.

    The result is a road that looks like a ribbon draped over the rock. The viaduct curves in a continuous S-shape, and riding across it, with the mountain on one side and a drop through the trees on the other, feels like flying. Below, the forest is a mixture of hemlock, rhododendron and hardwoods, which in autumn is a blaze of colour. The visitor centre at the viaduct has an exhibit explaining the construction, and a short trail runs under the bridge so you can see the piers up close.

    Grandfather Mountain itself, a few kilometres away, is one of the oldest mountains on earth, and one of the most biologically diverse in the Appalachians, with rare plants and animals. It is a privately run park with a mile-high swinging bridge between two peaks, and there is an entrance fee. The Parkway around here is slow, tight and twisting, with a speed limit of 35 or 45 miles an hour and plenty of tourists, but the pace is the point. Fog is common, and wet roads and leaf litter in autumn need respect. This section can close in winter, so check the park's status.

    Foscoe and Banner Elk have stations and shops, and Boone, about twelve kilometres away, is the nearest sizeable town with the full range of services, as well as an appealing university atmosphere and bluegrass music in the cafes. Fill up there before the ride north or south. Bring layers, because the weather at this altitude changes fast, and a sunny day in the valley can be cold and misty on the ridge.

  6. Mount Mitchell scenic

    Mount Mitchell has a simple claim to fame: at 2,037 metres, or 6,684 feet, it is the highest point in the United States east of the Mississippi River. It stands in the Black Mountains of western North Carolina, part of the southern Appalachians, and a spur road climbs to the summit from the Blue Ridge Parkway at around milepost 355. The last stretch of the road ends at a car park a short walk below the summit, where an observation platform gives views across rows of blue ridges.

    The mountain is named for Elisha Mitchell, a professor from the University of North Carolina who measured the peak in the 1830s and argued about its height with a rival. In 1857 he fell from a cliff near a waterfall while returning to confirm his measurements, and was buried at the summit, where his grave still lies. The area became North Carolina's first state park in 1915, after logging companies had stripped much of the forest, a loss that prompted early conservation efforts in the state.

    The high slopes hold a forest more typical of Canada. Red spruce and Fraser fir grow here, cool and dark, and the air smells of resin on damp mornings. Sadly, many of the Fraser firs have been killed by an invasive insect, the balsam woolly adelgid, leaving grey skeletons among the green. Clouds often wrap the summit, and temperatures at the top can be 10 degrees Celsius lower than in the valley. Snow can fall in any month except high summer.

    The Blue Ridge Parkway is a famous motorbike road, with a low speed limit, long gentle bends and constant views, and the climb up the spur to the summit is a short, steep detour. Sections of the parkway and the spur road close in winter, with ice and fog, so check the status before setting out. Autumn colours in October draw crowds. Black Mountain, seventeen kilometres away, and Burnsville have petrol stations and places to eat. Pack a warm layer, even in July.

  7. Craggy Gardens scenic

    Craggy Gardens is a high, bald ridge on the Blue Ridge Parkway at about milepost 364, where the forest gives way to heath and thickets of Catawba rhododendron, and in the second half of June the slopes burst into purple blooms. The elevation is about 5,500 feet, high enough that the trees are twisted and stunted by wind and ice, and the view from the trails near the visitor centre stretches across the mountains of western North Carolina. A short, steep walk leads to a rocky outcrop called Craggy Pinnacle, from which the whole ridge can be seen.

    The mountains here are among the oldest on Earth, worn down over hundreds of millions of years, and the Black Mountains, a few miles north, include Mount Mitchell, at 6,684 feet the highest peak east of the Mississippi River. A spur road from the Parkway climbs to its summit. The nearby towns of Black Mountain and Weaverville are the gateways to the area, and Asheville, about twenty-four kilometres to the south-west, has a lively downtown, the Biltmore estate and a reputation for craft beer and music.

    The Parkway runs for 469 miles along the Appalachian crest, with a maximum speed of 45 miles an hour, and no commercial traffic. The riding is relaxed and absorbing, with long, graceful curves, tunnels and viewpoints, and the road is almost always clean and smooth. Fog is common at this elevation, and can come down in minutes, and the temperature can be ten degrees colder than in the valley. Deer and bears may cross the road. In September 2024, Hurricane Helene caused severe damage to the Parkway around Asheville, and sections have been closed for repair, so check the National Park Service for the current status before you go.

    The nearest stations are in Black Mountain and Weaverville, a short ride down the access roads, and Asheville has everything. The visitor centre at Craggy Gardens has a small shop and toilets, but no restaurant, so bring a lunch.

    The best time to come is mid-June for the rhododendrons, or October for the colours, and mornings are less crowded. Stand on the ridge, feel the wind and enjoy the sense of space.

  8. Asheville scenic

    Asheville is the city of the Blue Ridge, an arty, mountain-framed town at about 650 metres where the French Broad River meets the Swannanoa. The mountains surround it on all sides, soft and blue in the haze, and the air has a clean, woody smell. It has a population of nearly ninety thousand, a lively downtown of art deco buildings from the 1920s, and a reputation as a place for craft beer, folk music, galleries and good food. Locals proudly call it Beer City, with dozens of breweries, and the cafes and farm-to-table restaurants have a loyal following.

    Its best-known landmark is the Biltmore Estate, built by George Vanderbilt in the 1890s, a French Renaissance château with 250 rooms, the largest privately owned house in the United States. The grounds were designed by Frederick Law Olmsted, the creator of Central Park, and include formal gardens, forests and a working winery. The novelist Thomas Wolfe grew up in Asheville, and his boyhood home, a boarding house his mother ran, is preserved as a museum. The city's health-giving climate drew tuberculosis patients and wealthy visitors in the early twentieth century, which is why the grand old hotels were built.

    For riders, the Blue Ridge Parkway passes right by the city, with the headquarters and the Folk Art Center, a showcase for Appalachian crafts, a few minutes from downtown. North and south of the city, the Parkway climbs along the ridges through forests of oak, hickory and rhododendron, and the views at the overlooks reach for many miles. The road is slow, with a limit of 45 miles an hour, and the curves are gentle and flowing. Hurricane Helene did serious damage to the region in September 2024, and parts of the Parkway near here were closed for long periods, so check the National Park Service for the current status before you plan a ride.

    Asheville has all the services you need, with fuel, bike shops, hotels and restaurants. Parking downtown is metered, and the streets can be busy on weekends. Autumn is the most popular season, when the leaves turn and the Parkway fills with traffic, while spring brings blossom and rhododendrons. Summer evenings can be warm and humid, with thunderstorms in the afternoon. Find a place with a view and a local beer, and enjoy the music coming from the sidewalk.

  9. Waterrock Knob scenic

    Waterrock Knob stands at 6,292 feet near milepost 451 of the Blue Ridge Parkway, one of the highest and most dramatic viewpoints on the whole road, with a vast panorama of the Great Smoky Mountains to the west and the Plott Balsam range to the east. The visitor centre sits on a high shoulder of the mountain, and a steep, rocky trail climbs to the summit, rewarding the effort with a view that, on a clear day, covers dozens of ridges stacked up into the distance, blue and then paler blue. At sunset, the whole landscape turns pink and gold.

    The Parkway reaches its highest point a little to the north, at Richland Balsam, at 6,053 feet, and this section, with its spruce and fir forest, is cool and dark, with the smell of Christmas trees. The road ends at the southern terminus near Cherokee, a town on the Qualla Boundary, the land of the Eastern Band of Cherokee, which has a museum of Cherokee history, a theatre and a casino. The Great Smoky Mountains National Park, the most visited national park in the United States, begins there, and the road over Newfound Gap, with its long views, is one of the great mountain roads of the East.

    The riding on this section is superb. The Parkway climbs through high balds and spruce forests with long, flowing curves, tunnels and overlooks, and the surface is generally smooth. It is the highest part of the road, with the temperature much lower than in the valleys, and fog, rain and even snow can occur in any season, so carry warm and waterproof clothing. Wildlife, including deer, bears and wild turkeys, is common, particularly at dawn and dusk. The Parkway has a speed limit of 45 miles an hour, and rangers enforce it.

    Fuel, food and coffee are off the road. Maggie Valley, seven kilometres away, has stations, shops and restaurants, and Waynesville, fourteen kilometres away, has more, and Cherokee has everything. The visitor centre has a small shop and toilets, but no food. Check for closures before you go, since the Parkway suffers from storms and landslides, particularly after the 2024 hurricane damage in the region.

    Bring a jacket, choose a clear evening and climb the trail to the top. The wind is cool, the view is huge, and the silence is deep.

  10. Cherokee finish

    Cherokee sits at the southern end of the Blue Ridge Parkway, in the western corner of North Carolina, and it is the capital of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians. The people here are descended from those who hid in the mountains in the 1830s, when most of their nation was forced west on the Trail of Tears, and the community holds a long lease on a tract of land called the Qualla Boundary. The Museum of the Cherokee Indian in town tells the story with real depth, and the sound of the Cherokee language, spoken and printed on street signs, is a quiet fact of daily life.

    To a rider, Cherokee is also the gateway to the Great Smoky Mountains National Park, the most visited national park in the country, with its entrance a few minutes north. The Parkway ends nearby at Oconaluftee, after nearly five hundred miles of ridge-top road from Virginia, with a speed limit of fifty-five at most and lots of slower sections. The surface is excellent, the bends are long and open, and the views look across blue ridge after blue ridge. The haze that gives the Smokies their name comes from vapour released by the forests, and it hangs in the valleys on summer mornings.

    Elk were reintroduced to the Cataloochee and Oconaluftee valleys in the 2000s, and they are often seen grazing in the fields near the park visitor centre, particularly at dawn and dusk. Black bears live throughout the park, and so do salamanders, more species than anywhere else in the world. Late October, when the maples and hickories turn, brings heavy traffic, while spring has wildflowers and dogwoods. The Parkway has some closures after storms or in winter, with ice a real hazard, so check the park's road status.

    Cherokee is a small town and relies on tourism, with a large casino complex, motels, gift shops and a number of places serving fried trout and Southern barbecue. Fuel is available in town, but not much of the Parkway has any, so top up here and again at Sylva, Bryson City or Waynesville. There are no commercial services along the Parkway itself for long stretches.

    Take your time, stay a night if you can, and walk a short trail along the Oconaluftee river. The Smokies are better felt than hurried through.

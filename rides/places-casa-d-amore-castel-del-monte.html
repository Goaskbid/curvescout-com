From Alberobello it runs 295 km to Alberobello; allow about 5 h 05 of riding. The stops in between: The Sassi and the Park of the Rupestrian C, Castel del Monte and back through The Sassi and the Park of the Rupestrian C. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Casa d'Amore

    Behind the souvenir shops of Alberobello lies a story about taxes and kings that locals like to tell, and the Casa d'Amore is a handy place to start telling it. The house is one of the older buildings in the Rione Monti, the hillside quarter of conical trullo roofs, and it dates to the closing years of the eighteenth century. It is known as the first of the trulli to be built with mortar, and its interior now serves as a small exhibition with traditional furnishings and, as in many such houses, a tiny shop selling local crafts and wine.

  2. 2. The Sassi and the Park of the Rupestrian C

    Matera is one of those places where the first view genuinely changes the mood of a day. You walk to the edge of the modern town, look down, and there are the Sassi: two hollows in the limestone filled with houses stacked on top of each other, their roofs serving as streets for the houses above, churches cut into the rock, staircases and alleys tumbling down to the edge of a deep ravine, the Gravina. Across the gorge, the Murgia plateau is riddled with caves and rock churches, some with Byzantine frescoes, used by monks and shepherds for over a thousand years. People have lived in these caves since prehistoric times, which makes Matera one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in the world.

  3. 3. Castel del Monte

    You see it long before you reach it: a pale stone crown sitting alone on a hilltop above the Murge plateau, visible for miles across the olive groves and wheat fields of northern Puglia. Castel del Monte was built in the 1240s for Emperor Frederick II, the brilliant and restless Hohenstaufen ruler whom contemporaries called the wonder of the world. It is perfectly octagonal, with eight octagonal towers at the corners and an octagonal courtyard in the middle, and nobody really knows what it was for. It has no moat, no stables, no obvious defensive purpose, which has fed centuries of theories about astronomy, geometry and mysticism. UNESCO listed it in 1996, and Italians carry it in their pockets: it is on the back of the one-cent euro coin.

Why this ride

A 33 km loop that starts and ends near Dingle, about 32 min of riding. It goes by way of Ventry, Slea Head, Dunquin and Ballyferriter. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Dingle

    Dingle is a small harbour town at the western end of County Kerry's Dingle Peninsula, with fewer than two thousand people but about as many pubs as you could want. The streets slope toward the water, shops and houses are painted in strong colours, and the harbour is full of fishing boats. This is a Gaeltacht area, where Irish is still the everyday language for many families, so you may see signs in Irish first, and the town's Irish name, An Daingean, appears on the road markers.

  2. 2. Ventry

    Ventry is where the Slea Head loop really begins, a long curve of beach at the bottom of the Dingle Peninsula with the mountain Mount Eagle standing over its western end. The Irish name, Ceann Trá, means the head of the strand, and the beach is exactly that: a clean, wide sweep of sand, popular with families and swimmers in summer and often completely empty in the shoulder months. Dingle is only about seven kilometres east, which makes this the easy first stop of a circuit that gets wilder with every mile.

  3. 3. Slea Head

    Slea Head is the western end of Europe in everything but the strict geographical sense, and on a clear day it feels like it. The road clings to the side of Mount Eagle high above the Atlantic, and to the south-west the Blasket Islands lie spread across the water, with Skellig Michael faint on the horizon when the air is very clean. The Irish name is Ceann Sléibhe, the head of the mountain, and the headland drops into the sea in a tumble of rock, bracken and stone-walled fields that look too small to farm.

  4. 4. Dunquin

    Dunquin, in Irish Dún Chaoin, is the last village before the road turns north, and it has the look of a place that has been hanging on at the edge of things for a very long time. The houses are scattered down a hillside above a cove, and the pier below is reached by a steep, zigzagging track that has been a postcard staple for decades. From here the boats once crossed to the Blasket Islands, a mile or two offshore across a stretch of water that can be calm one hour and furious the next.

  5. 5. Ballyferriter

    Past Dunquin the road leaves the high cliffs and swings north into gentler country, and Ballyferriter, Baile an Fheirtéaraigh, is the first real village on that side. It sits in a green bowl of small fields between the mountain and Smerwick Harbour, and it feels like the centre of a very old district. The area has a lot of history packed into a short distance, which is part of the pleasure of riding it slowly.

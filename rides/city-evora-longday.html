Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Evora

    Alentejo is the part of Portugal that looks as if someone had ironed it. South and east of the Tagus, the land rolls out in low swells of wheat, olive and cork oak, with white villages on the hills and storks nesting on the chimneys, and Évora sits in the middle of it, about 130 kilometres east of Lisbon. It is a walled city of some fifty-five thousand, and for riders it is the natural pause in a long day of empty road.

  2. 2. Fortlet of São Domingos

    Elvas is a border town, and borders create walls. The town of Elvas, in the Alentejo, faces Spain across a short stretch of plain, with the Spanish fortress city of Badajoz only seventeen kilometres away, and for centuries the two countries glared at each other from behind stone. Portugal built what became the largest bulwarked dry-ditch fortification system in the world, and in 2012 the Garrison Border Town of Elvas and its Fortifications were inscribed on the World Heritage list. The little Fort of Sao Domingos is one of the smaller pieces in this puzzle, a fortlet set on the edge of the plain that strengthened the defence of the town.

  3. 3. Amoreira Aqueduct

    Seven kilometres of arches carried water to Elvas, and the people of the town took a century to finish building them. The Amoreira Aqueduct was begun in the sixteenth century, when the old cisterns were no longer enough for a growing garrison town, and it was not finished until the early seventeenth, after a long stop and start, and a great deal of money, including a special tax on the townspeople. The result is a structure that strides across the plain on up to four tiers of arches, rising to about thirty metres, with hundreds of arches in all. It can be seen from far off, a long grey line over the fields, and where it comes into town it dwarfs the houses.

  4. 4. Fortlet of São Pedro

    The Alentejo plain rolls away from Elvas in every direction, a country of cork oak, olive and wheat, and the little fort of São Pedro stands on a low hill outside the town as one of a ring of outworks around it. Elvas is a town ringed by star-shaped bastions built in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when Portugal and Spain fought repeatedly over this frontier, and just across the border lies Badajoz. The fortified town and its outer forts, including the fortlets of São Pedro, São Mamede and São Domingos, were listed by UNESCO in 2012 as the largest dry-ditch fortification system in the world.

  5. 5. Fort of Santa Luzia and the covered way

    From the top of the Fort of Santa Luzia you can look down on Elvas like a general planning an assault. The fort stands on a rise to the south of the old town and was built from 1641, at the start of the Portuguese Restoration War, to deny enemy artillery a hill that overlooked the walls. Its shape is a star, with four bastions and a deep dry ditch, and its engineers followed the Dutch style, which was the best defensive school in Europe at that time. The walls are thick and low, built to shed cannonballs, and the corridor called the covered way runs around the outer edge of the ditch, a protected path where soldiers could move in cover and meet an attack head on.

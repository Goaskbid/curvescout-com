  1. 1. Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump

    Imagine a wall of sandstone, ten metres high, with the plain falling away beneath it. For about five thousand years the Blackfoot and their ancestors used cliffs like this one, in the foothills of the Porcupine Hills in southern Alberta, to hunt bison. Hunters spent days guiding a herd, gently and patiently, along lines of stone cairns called drive lanes, until the animals were running without knowing it, and then the lead animals went over the edge. Below, the whole community waited with knives, and the meat and hides of one successful drive could see a band through the winter. UNESCO lists the site as one of the oldest and best-preserved examples of the method anywhere in the world.

  2. 2. Waterton Glacier International Peace Park

    In 1932, the people of Alberta and Montana decided that two parks that shared a border might as well share a name. Waterton Lakes National Park in Canada and Glacier National Park in the United States became the world's first International Peace Park, a gesture of friendship that grew from a suggestion by Rotary clubs. UNESCO later recognised it as a World Heritage Site, and the two parks are managed together for wildlife, even though visitors cross a border to get from one to the other.

  3. 3. Waterton Glacier International Peace Park

    The southern half of the Peace Park is Glacier National Park in Montana, a rugged expanse of peaks, lakes and valleys carved by ice, with the Continental Divide running through its core. The park was established in 1910 and covers more than a million acres, and in its heyday it held about a hundred and fifty glaciers. Climate change has reduced that to a few dozen, and the shrinking of the ice is one of the park's central stories.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Limessection between watchtower WP 1/87 an

    Along this part of the Limes, between Arzbach, Dausenau and Kadenbach, the Roman frontier cuts across the hills above the Lahn valley, and the numbered watchtower positions, here around the 1/24 to 1/26 group, give the section its identity. A watchtower was small, a stone base with a timber upper room, and signalled by fire or horn to its neighbours. The point of it all was surveillance rather than defence; if trouble came, the troops in the forts behind would be told. Standing at one of these positions now, in forest rather than the open ground the Romans cleared, you have to remind yourself that the trees are a later gift.

  2. 2. Limessection between watchtowers WP 1/60 a

    Höhr-Grenzhausen is a town that makes things out of mud. The Westerwald plateau here sits on one of Europe's finest deposits of stoneware clay, and for centuries potters have dug it, thrown it and fired it in salt-glaze kilns to make grey-and-blue jugs, tankards and storage jars. The area is called the Kannenbäckerland, the land of the jug bakers, and its pots were shipped down the Rhine and across the world. The Westerwald Ceramics Museum in Höhr-Grenzhausen tells the story, and several workshops in town still sell hand-made pieces. Take a mug home and you will be surprised how heavy it is.

  3. 3. Watchtowers WP 1/32 to 1/34

    Just north of Neuwied, the Westerwald begins to climb out of the Rhine valley, and the Roman frontier ran up through the woods from its starting point on the river, rising and falling over the low ridges. The Limes here is the Upper German section in its earliest stretch, where watchtowers 1/32 to 1/34 stood close together on the hillside above the Wied, a small river that runs through a pretty, wooded valley to join the Rhine. Melsbach, a village of two thousand people next to the point, lies in this valley, and the surrounding hills are a mix of beech forest, meadow and the fields of the Neuwied basin.

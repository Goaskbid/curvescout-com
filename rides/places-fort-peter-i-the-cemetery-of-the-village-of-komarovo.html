  1. 1. Fort «Peter I»

    Peter the Great wanted a fortress on the sea, and in the first years of the eighteenth century he got one, built on a sandbank and a few small islets in the shallows off Kotlin Island. Fort Peter I belongs to the chain of forts that grew from that first idea over the next two centuries, strung along the channels like beads on a thread, each with its own gun batteries, barracks and harbours. Together they formed a barrier that no enemy fleet cared to test, and during the Crimean War, when British and French ships came to the gulf, they thought better of attacking.

  2. 2. Fort «Totleben»

    Eduard Totleben was the engineer who held Sevastopol in the Crimean War, and the Russian navy named a fort after him in the ring of defences that guards Saint Petersburg from the sea. Fort Totleben, along with its neighbour Fort Peter I, belongs to the northern line of fortifications built on shoals in the Gulf of Finland, in the nineteenth century, to protect the approaches to Kronstadt and the capital.

  3. 3. The Cemetery of the Village of Komarovo

    Anna Akhmatova is buried here. The cemetery of Komarovo is a small, quiet graveyard among pines on the Karelian Isthmus, on the shore of the Gulf of Finland about forty kilometres north-west of Saint Petersburg, and her grave, a simple stone with a cross, is the one that people come to find. She died in 1966, and loved the little cottage she was given in the village, which she called her Booth. The cemetery also holds the grave of the scholar Dmitry Likhachev, and a scattering of writers, artists and scientists who spent their summers in this dacha colony, once reserved for the Soviet cultural elite.

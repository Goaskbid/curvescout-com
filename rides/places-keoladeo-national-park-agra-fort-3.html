Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Keoladeo National Park

    For decades the Maharajas of Bharatpur kept this marshland as a duck-shooting estate, and the scale of the sport is hard to believe. On a single day in 1938 the viceroy Lord Linlithgow and his party shot more than four thousand birds, a record that still shocks people today. The shoots ended after independence, and in 1971 the area became a protected sanctuary. It is now Keoladeo National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1985 and one of the finest bird sanctuaries in Asia.

  2. 2. Agra Fort

    Red sandstone walls rise out of the Yamuna floodplain at Agra, and for a moment they seem to have been put there to distract you from the Taj Mahal. Agra Fort is a very different thing from its famous neighbour, two and a half kilometres downstream. It is a palace-city, a military stronghold and a seat of government, begun by the emperor Akbar in 1565 and completed over several generations, with high double walls, a moat and gates that climb in zigzags to slow down attackers. UNESCO listed it in 1983.

  3. 3. Fatehpur Sikri

    Fatehpur Sikri rises on a sandstone ridge west of Agra, a red city of palaces, courtyards and mosques built by the Mughal emperor Akbar in the 1570s and abandoned just a few years later. Akbar built it to honour the Sufi saint Salim Chishti, who had predicted the birth of his son and heir, and for over a decade it was the capital of his empire. Then, for reasons that are still debated, perhaps a lack of water or political changes, the court moved away, and the city was left almost intact, a time capsule of Mughal architecture and a World Heritage site.

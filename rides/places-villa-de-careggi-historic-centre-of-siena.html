Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Villa de Careggi

    Cosimo de' Medici chose a quiet slope just north of Florence for his country house, and in the late 1450s he had the architect Michelozzo rebuild an older castle-like farmhouse at Careggi into a villa. It was not a grand showpiece. It was a working retreat where a banker who ran a city without holding office could think, farm, and entertain scholars. Cosimo died here in 1464, and so did his grandson Lorenzo the Magnificent in 1492, which makes the walls rather weighty with Florentine history.

  2. 2. Villa La Magia

    The Medici loved the countryside, especially when it came with hunting grounds, and Villa La Magia at Quarrata was one of their country retreats at the foot of the Montalbano hills, a few kilometres south of Pistoia. Francesco I de' Medici bought it in the sixteenth century to use as a hunting lodge, and it belongs to the World Heritage inscription for the Medici villas and gardens of Tuscany. The villa is a solid rectangular building around a courtyard, more farmhouse than palace, with a park that has become home to contemporary art installations, a rather unexpected touch for a Renaissance hunting lodge.

  3. 3. Historic Centre of San Gimignano

    Seen from the surrounding countryside, San Gimignano looks like a medieval Manhattan: a cluster of stone towers rising from a hilltop above vineyards and olive groves in the Val d'Elsa. In its heyday, in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, the town had dozens of them, built by rival families as symbols of power and wealth. Fourteen survive, enough to make it one of the most recognisable towns in Italy and a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1990.

  4. 4. Historic Centre of Siena

    Siena sits on three ridges in the heart of Tuscany, a red-brick medieval city that has hardly changed its shape in seven hundred years. At its centre is the Piazza del Campo, a sloping, shell-shaped square paved in brick and divided into nine sections, a reminder of the Council of Nine that governed the city in its golden age. The Palazzo Pubblico, with the slender Torre del Mangia, stands at the bottom of the square, and inside are frescoes by Ambrogio Lorenzetti showing the effects of good and bad government, painted in the fourteenth century.

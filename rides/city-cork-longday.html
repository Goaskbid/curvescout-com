  1. 1. Cork

    Cork calls itself the real capital of Ireland and says so with a grin. The city began as a monastic settlement on marshy islands in the River Lee, and the centre still sits on an island between two channels, with bridges at every turn. It is Ireland's second city, but a proud and independent one, with its own accent and food and a long history of rebellion. The English Market, a covered food hall that has traded since the late eighteenth century, is the best place to see it, with butchers selling spiced beef and drisheen, a local blood pudding, fishmongers laying out the day's catch and bakers offering brown soda bread.

  2. 2. Sceilg Mhichíl

    Sceilg Mhichíl, in English Skellig Michael, is a pinnacle of rock rising out of the Atlantic about twelve kilometres off the Kerry coast, and it is one of those places that makes you wonder what kind of person would choose to live there. Monks did, starting somewhere around the sixth to eighth century, building a monastery of beehive-shaped stone huts, terraces and oratories on a ledge some two hundred metres above the waves, reached by roughly six hundred stone steps. They grew what they could, caught fish and birds, collected rainwater, and held services in some of the wildest conditions in Europe, until the community moved away in the thirteenth century. UNESCO listed the island in 1996.

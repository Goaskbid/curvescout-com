Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Estes Park

    Elk wander through the streets of Estes Park in autumn as if they had a lease on the place. During the rut, in late September and October, bulls bugle on the lawns and golf course, and visitors line up with cameras at a respectful distance, while the town itself, sitting at around 7,500 feet in a mountain bowl where the Big Thompson and Fall rivers meet, carries on with its cafes, fudge shops and outdoor stores. It is the eastern gateway to Rocky Mountain National Park and the starting point for Trail Ridge Road.

  2. 2. Near Estes Park

    Trail Ridge Road begins its climb here, a few miles west of Estes Park, and by the time you reach this stretch of US 34, you have entered Rocky Mountain National Park and left the town far below. The road winds up through forests of ponderosa pine and Douglas fir, past meadows where elk graze, and around curves that open up views of the Continental Divide. Around Many Parks Curve, a parking area on a tight bend gives one of the best early views: a series of mountain meadows, or parks, spread out below, with Longs Peak, the highest summit in the park, towering over them to the south.

  3. 3. Near Estes Park

    Up here, near the treeline, the trees give up. Around Rainbow Curve and on toward Forest Canyon Overlook, the spruce and fir that lined the lower road shrink into twisted, stunted shapes called krummholz, bent and sheared by the wind, and then disappear altogether, leaving a vast, open world of tundra, rock and sky. The views are enormous: deep glacial valleys, the jagged peaks of the Continental Divide, and on a clear day the plains stretching away to the east. You are above 11,000 feet, and the air is thin and cold.

  4. 4. Trail Ridge Road point 4

    Fall River Pass sits at 11,796 feet, and the Alpine Visitor Center perched beside it, at just over 12,000 feet on the ridge, is the highest of its kind in the national park system. This is the rooftop of the drive. The forest ended some time ago, and what is left is the tundra, a low, wind-clipped world of tiny cushion plants, lichens and flowers that bloom in a few weeks of summer. It looks like grass, but it grows only a few centimetres per year, and a single footprint can leave a scar that lasts decades. Please stay on the paths.

  5. 5. Trail Ridge Road point 5

    At Milner Pass the Continental Divide runs right across the road, and a modest sign says so. The elevation is 10,758 feet, which is lower than the high ridge of the road but still above most of the Rockies' passes, and the setting is a quiet mountain lake, Poudre Lake, with a boardwalk, a pine forest and a trail climbing to the Divide. It is a spot where you can stand on a line that decides the fate of rain: water falling on the east side finds its way to the Atlantic by way of the Mississippi, while that on the west joins the Colorado River and ends up in the Pacific, or at least tries to.

  6. 6. Trail Ridge Road point 6

    Grand Lake is a small, relaxed town on the north-east shore of a big, deep glacial lake, the largest natural lake in Colorado, at about 8,400 feet. Boardwalks run along the main street, with wooden storefronts, ice cream shops and bait stores, and a cluster of old log buildings dating from the early days of the town. The lake itself is a dark, cold blue, ringed by forest, with the Never Summer Mountains standing behind. Boats, kayaks and paddle boards fill it in summer, and the local yacht club claims to be one of the highest in the world.

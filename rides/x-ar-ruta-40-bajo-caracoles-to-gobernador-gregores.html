  1. 1. Bajo Caracoles

    Bajo Caracoles is hardly a town at all, and that is exactly why it is famous. Out on the Patagonian steppe in northern Santa Cruz, on one of the most remote stretches of Ruta 40, a handful of buildings stand beside the road: an old general store and hotel, a fuel pump, a few houses and a lot of sky. Its name, meaning low place of the snails, refers to the fossil shells found in the surrounding ground, and the whole area is a dry seabed that was lifted up when the Andes formed.

  2. 2. Gobernador Gregores

    Gobernador Gregores is a small town of about three thousand people in the middle of Santa Cruz, a long way from anything else, and it is one of the few real service points on this huge stretch of Patagonia. It sits on a broad, wind-swept plateau of steppe, where sheep farming has been the main business since the early twentieth century, and the nearby estancias, some of them enormous, sprawl across tens of thousands of hectares. The town has the orderly, slightly spare look of a place laid out on a grid and built for practicality, with wide streets and low houses.

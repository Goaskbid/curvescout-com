Why this ride

curated scenic road. Pyrenees, gorges, stone towns. From Portbou it runs 338 km to Biescas; allow about 6 h 09 of riding. The stops in between: Besalú, Ripoll, Sort and Aínsa. The highest point is about 1,800 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Portbou

    Portbou is a border town in the most literal sense. It is wedged into a steep, rocky cove at the very end of the Costa Brava, a few hundred metres from France, with houses stacked up the hillside and a railway station so oversized that it looks like a cathedral beached by mistake. The station once handled all the rail traffic between Spain and France, where the track gauge changed, and passengers and goods were transferred between trains. Today the town has just fourteen hundred people and feels half-asleep, hushed by the wind and the sea.

  2. 2. Besalú

    Besalú arrives suddenly. You come round a bend on the N260 and there it is, a fortified stone bridge with a tower in the middle, seven arches crossing the Fluvià river, and behind it a cluster of tawny roofs and church towers that has hardly changed since the twelfth century. It is one of the best-preserved medieval towns in Catalonia and it is small enough, with only a couple of thousand residents, that you can walk across it in ten minutes. The bridge itself is for walkers only, so you leave the bike on the edge of town and cross on foot, which is how you should see it.

  3. 3. Ripoll

    The Catalan nation, if you believe the story, begins at Ripoll. In the year 879 Count Wilfred the Hairy, called Guifré el Pelós, founded a monastery here at the meeting of two rivers, the Ter and the Freser, and later it became the burial place of the counts of Barcelona. The monastery of Santa Maria still stands in the middle of town, and what draws visitors is its west portal, a huge Romanesque doorway covered in carved stone scenes from the Bible, so detailed that it is often compared to a stone book. Inside the cloister there is a quiet courtyard with two storeys of columns, each with its own carved capital, and you can walk around it in about half an hour.

  4. 4. Sort

    Sort sits in the heart of the Catalan Pyrenees, in the Pallars Sobirà valley, where the Noguera Pallaresa river runs fast and green between steep mountains. It is a small town of about two thousand people, with a few streets of old stone houses, a tidy square and a surprising claim to fame: the lottery. A shop here has sold so many winning tickets over the years that Spaniards drive for hours to buy a ticket from it, and the name, which means luck in Catalan, adds to the legend.

  5. 5. Aínsa

    Aínsa sits on a hilltop above the meeting of two Pyrenean rivers, the Cinca and the Ara, and the old village on top is one of those places where the medieval layout has simply been left to get on with itself. You enter through a gate into a great sloping square of rough cobbles surrounded by stone arcades, with a castle and a Romanesque church on the high side and views out over the valley. It was once the capital of the little Kingdom of Sobrarbe, whose rulers, according to legend, rallied here against the Moors, and the town still stages a spectacular festival recalling that story every few years.

  6. 6. Jaca

    Jaca is the old capital of the Pyrenees, a mountain town of thirteen thousand people at the foot of the Peña Oroel, with a cathedral, a fortress and a long history. In the eleventh century it was the first capital of the Kingdom of Aragon, and its cathedral, begun at that time, is one of the earliest Romanesque cathedrals in Spain, with a graceful apse and a cloister, and carved capitals that influenced churches along the pilgrim road.

  7. 7. Biescas

    Biescas sits in the Aragonese Pyrenees at the lower end of the Tena valley, where the Gállego river cuts through the foothills and the mountains close in. It is a small mountain town with stone houses and slate roofs, set among meadows and pine woods, and with the high peaks of the Tendeñera range standing behind it. The sense is of a place at the doorstep of the high Pyrenees, a base for skiers in winter and walkers and riders in summer.

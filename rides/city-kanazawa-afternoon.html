Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Kanazawa

    Kanazawa escaped the bombs of the Second World War and kept its old quarters, which is a large part of its charm today. The city sits on the Sea of Japan coast of Ishikawa Prefecture, between two rivers and a hill, and for centuries it was ruled by the Maeda clan, the second richest domain in feudal Japan. Their money went into art and craft, and Kanazawa is still known for gold leaf, lacquerware, ceramics and tea ceremony. Kenroku-en, the garden the clan laid out over generations, is counted among the three great gardens of Japan, with its pines held up by rope umbrellas against the heavy winter snow.

  2. 2. Suganuma Village

    Suganuma is one of the two small villages in Gokayama that kept their thatched farmhouses, and it is the quieter of them. It sits on the bank of the Sho River in the mountains of southern Toyama Prefecture, in a steep, forested valley that for centuries was cut off by deep snow for months at a time. A cluster of about nine gassho-zukuri houses, with steep thatched roofs said to resemble hands pressed together in prayer, stand together on a small terrace above the water, and the effect, particularly in morning mist, is of a village from a woodblock print.

  3. 3. Historic Villages of Shirakawa-go and Goka

    Shirakawa-go and Gokayama together form one of the most photographed rural landscapes in Japan, and for once the photographs are not misleading. Across the border between Gifu and Toyama prefectures, in the deep valley of the Sho River, farming families built houses with huge triangular thatched roofs, some of them three or four storeys high, angled at sixty degrees to shed the heavy winter snow. The roofs are made of reeds without a single nail, and they need rethatching every few decades, a task the village does collectively, in a tradition called yui, with the whole community turning out to help.

  4. 4. Ainokura Village

    Ainokura is the smaller, quieter sibling to Shirakawa-go, tucked at the head of a side valley in the Gokayama district of Toyama prefecture. About twenty houses stand in the steep thatched gassho style, a name that means hands pressed in prayer and describes the roofs, which are steeply pitched to shed heavy snow. Inside, big wooden frames are lashed together with rope and not nails, and the top floors were built for silk and, famously, for making gunpowder ingredients. The remote location mattered. The Kaga domain, which ruled the area, valued the saltpetre, and the valley was a quiet place where people could make it out of sight. The village forms part of the UNESCO listing with Ogimachi, which is the bigger one.

  5. 5. Ogimachi Village 2

    Ogimachi is the largest of the gassho villages and the one most people picture when they think of Shirakawa-go, a cluster of steep thatched farmhouses in a valley of the Sho river in Gifu prefecture. Some sixty houses survive here, dark with age, their roofs angled at about sixty degrees so that deep snow slides off. Families once lived on the ground floor, kept silkworms in the attic spaces, and shared the work of rethatching in a communal effort called yui, in which the whole village turned out to rebuild a roof in a day or two. The practice carries on in a smaller form, and a roof is replaced every few decades. The village shares its UNESCO listing with Gokayama, 1995.

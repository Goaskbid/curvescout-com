  1. 1. El Calafate

    El Calafate is a small town on the southern shore of Lago Argentino, and it is almost entirely about one thing: the ice. Eighty kilometres west, in Los Glaciares National Park, the Perito Moreno Glacier pushes out of the Andes into the lake in a wall of blue ice about five kilometres wide, rising to some seventy metres above the water. Unlike most glaciers it is relatively stable, and every few years it advances far enough to dam an arm of the lake, until the pressure of water breaks through in a roaring collapse. Watching blocks of ice calve from the face and crash into the lake, with a sound like thunder delayed by a second, is one of the great sights of Patagonia.

  2. 2. Perito Moreno Glacier

    The Perito Moreno Glacier is one of the few big glaciers in the world that is not retreating dramatically, and it is also one of the easiest to see. It flows from the Southern Patagonian Ice Field into Lago Argentino in the Los Glaciares National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and ends in a wall of blue and white ice about five kilometres wide and as high as a twenty-storey building above the water. The glacier is named for Francisco Moreno, the explorer, and the sound it makes is part of the experience: a deep, sharp crack like a gunshot, followed by a roar as a chunk of ice the size of a house peels off and crashes into the lake.

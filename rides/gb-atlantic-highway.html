  1. 1. Barnstaple

    Barnstaple lies at the head of the Taw estuary, and it claims to be one of the oldest boroughs in England, with a charter said to date from the tenth century. It is the largest town in North Devon, and it is the natural start for the Atlantic Highway.

  2. 2. Bude

    Bude is where the long Atlantic Highway comes to a stop against the sea. The town sits at the top of north Cornwall, on a coast of black, folded cliffs and long sandy beaches, with big Atlantic swells rolling in and surfers in the water at almost every hour of daylight. It is not a pretty harbour town in the manner of south Cornwall; it is a windy, bright, slightly salty place, with a canal, a sea pool and a handful of old streets behind the beach.

  3. 3. Tintagel

    Tintagel has been selling King Arthur for a long time. The twelfth-century writer Geoffrey of Monmouth first placed Arthur's conception here, in his History of the Kings of Britain, and the legend grew from there, helped by a Victorian love of chivalry and a ruined castle on a dramatic headland. Archaeologists have since found that the site was an important trading post in the fifth and sixth centuries, when imported Mediterranean pottery and glass came in by sea, so something notable did happen here, but whether it involved a king named Arthur is another matter. Cornwall, characteristically, has chosen to believe.

  4. 4. Newquay

    Newquay is the surf capital of England, though it started as something humbler. The name means new quay and goes back to the fifteenth century, when a harbour was built to replace an older one, and for a long time pilchards were the business. On the headland above the harbour stands the Huer's Hut, where a lookout watched for shoals and shouted to the fishermen through a megaphone. Victorians then brought the railway and the first visitors, and in the 1960s surfers arrived with boards from Australia and California.

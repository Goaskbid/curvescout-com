Why this ride

331 km from Bardon Mill to Bardon Mill, about 4 h 23 of riding. The stops in between: Hadrian's Wall in wall mile 7, Hadrian's Wall and vallum between the acce and back through Hadrian's Wall in wall mile 7. The line favours views over speed.

The stops

  1. 1. Twice Brewed Roman temporary camp

    Start here.

    The Twice Brewed inn gives its name to a stretch of Northumberland where the Roman frontier is at its most dramatic. Here the Great Whin Sill, a hard sheet of dark volcanic rock, breaks in a long line of cliffs facing north, and the Wall runs along the very edge of it, rising and falling like a stone roller-coaster. Haltwhistle, five kilometres off, is the nearest town, and Hexham lies a good deal further east.

    Read the complete Twice Brewed Roman temporary camp write-up.

  2. 2. Hadrian's Wall in wall mile 7

    52 km in.

    Hadrian's Wall begins its life not on a windswept crag but in the streets of Newcastle, and wall mile 7 runs through the western suburbs around Benwell and Denton, a few kilometres from the city centre. The Romans numbered the miles westward from the fort at Wallsend, and in this stretch the line of the Wall follows the ridge now taken by the West Road. Most of it lies under houses and tarmac, but fragments surface where you least expect them, which is half the fun.

    Read the complete Hadrian's Wall in wall mile 7 write-up.

  3. 3. Hadrian's Wall and vallum between the acce

    169 km in.

    Across the water from here lies Eastriggs, a quiet Scottish village with a loud past. During the First World War a huge munitions factory stretched for miles along the Solway coast from Gretna to Annan, and the cordite paste that women workers mixed by hand earned the nickname devil's porridge. Thousands lived in purpose-built townships, and the whole operation was kept secret. A museum in Eastriggs now tells the story. The reason the label brings us to the English shore, though, is the Roman frontier, because Hadrian's Wall did not simply stop at Bowness-on-Solway. Beyond it a chain of milefortlets and towers carried on down the Cumbrian coast, watching the sea rather than the hills.

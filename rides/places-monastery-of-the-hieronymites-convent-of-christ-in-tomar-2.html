Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Monastery of the Hieronymites

    The Hieronymites were a monastic order that followed Saint Jerome, and King Manuel I gave them a very grand home. He ordered the monastery built on the riverbank at Belém in 1501, on the site of a small chapel founded by Henry the Navigator, to serve as a royal pantheon and a place where monks would pray for sailors and for the king's soul, and it took most of a century to complete. The architects João de Castilho and Diogo de Boitaca worked in the Manueline style, and the south portal, a mass of carved saints and canopies, and the cloister, with its twisting columns and nautical motifs, are among the finest pieces of late Gothic in Europe.

  2. 2. Royal Building of Mafra - Palace

    King João V of Portugal had a great deal of Brazilian gold and a vow to keep, and the result is the Royal Building of Mafra, a palace, basilica and monastery so large that it seems to belong to a different scale of town altogether. Construction began in 1717, reportedly in thanks for the birth of an heir, and the project absorbed enormous resources and tens of thousands of workers. The facade stretches for some two hundred and twenty metres, with the basilica in the centre and the royal apartments in the wings, the king's at one end and the queen's at the other, a long walk apart.

  3. 3. Monastery of Alcobaça

    In 1153, Afonso Henriques, the first king of Portugal, gave a vast estate to the Cistercian monks of Bernard of Clairvaux, and they built here one of the greatest monasteries of medieval Europe. The church of Alcobaça, with its long, soaring nave of pale stone, is austere and beautiful in the Cistercian manner, and the cloisters, dormitory, chapter house and refectory survive almost intact. The kitchen is enormous, with a huge chimney and a stream diverted through it so the monks could catch fish directly. UNESCO listed the monastery in 1989. But the main reason most visitors come is a love story.

  4. 4. Convent of Christ in Tomar

    The Knights Templar chose the hill above Tomar in the twelfth century, building a castle to defend the new kingdom of Portugal against the Moors, and at its heart they raised a round church, the Charola, modelled on the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. When the Templars were suppressed in the fourteenth century, the Portuguese king transferred their property to a new order, the Order of Christ, which went on to finance much of Portugal's maritime expansion; Henry the Navigator was its governor. Over the centuries, the order added cloisters, chapels and dormitories, and the Convent of Christ became one of the great monuments of Portugal, inscribed on the UNESCO list in 1983.

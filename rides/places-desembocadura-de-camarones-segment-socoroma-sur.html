Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Desembocadura de Camarones

    The coast of northern Chile is a place where almost nothing happens, and that is its character. The Camarones river rises in the high Andes and runs west through one of the driest landscapes on Earth, cutting a narrow green valley through the Atacama before it reaches the Pacific at a small mouth south of Arica. In dry years the water gives out before it reaches the sea, and the final stretch is a mixture of reeds, sand and salt pans, a patch of green on a coast of brown and grey. Birds congregate here: gulls, pelicans, terns and, in the right season, waders that come all the way from the northern hemisphere.

  2. 2. Settlement and Artificial Mummification of

    The Chinchorro were fishers who lived on this coast, and they were doing something extraordinary with their dead at a time when nothing like it existed anywhere else. Around seven thousand years ago, they began to preserve their dead by taking the body apart, drying the skin and bones, reassembling them on sticks, packing the body with clay, plant fibre and ash, and covering the face with a mask of clay. These are the oldest known artificially mummified bodies in the world, older than the better-known Egyptian mummies by some two thousand years. The tradition included children and infants, and no one is quite sure why.

  3. 3. Segment Socoroma Sur

    Socoroma is a village high above the desert, and the road to it is a climb from the coast into another world. Starting from the sea at Arica, the road rises through the arid foothills, past cactus and rocky ridges, and up into the Andean altiplano, passing from sea level to the better part of three thousand metres in a couple of hours. The change is obvious: the air thins, the temperature drops, the light becomes harder and the sky turns a deeper blue. Socoroma sits on the old route between the coast and the high plateau, on terraced slopes cultivated for centuries.

Why this ride

From Kronstadt it runs 222 km to Kronstadt; allow about 3 h 50 of riding. The stops in between: The Palace and Park of the Znamenskaya Dat, Kievskoe Highway, Scientific Town-Institution of Physiologis and back through Kievskoe Highway. An island ride, so the sea is never far.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Tolbukhin Lighthouse on Tolbukhin Island

    Ships have been guided by a light on Tolbukhin island for about three centuries, and it is one of those places that makes more sense on a chart than on a road. The island lies in the Gulf of Finland, south-west of Kronstadt and out in the shallow channel that leads to Saint Petersburg, and the lighthouse on it marks the southern passage into the Neva Bay. The surrounding waters are shallow, and in the grey light of autumn the sea and the sky merge into a single pale band.

  2. 2. The Palace and Park of the Znamenskaya Dat

    South-west of Saint Petersburg, along the shore of the Gulf of Finland, the land is a continuous belt of imperial estates. Peterhof is the best known, with its gilded fountains and long terraces, but behind it and around it is a countryside of parks, dachas and country palaces built by the imperial family and the aristocracy from the eighteenth century onward. Znamenka was one of these. The name comes from the Znamenie, the Sign, and the estate had a palace and a park that served as a summer residence, laid out in the landscape style that was fashionable in the nineteenth century.

    South of Saint Petersburg, the flat, damp plain of Ingria runs off towards Pskov and the old western frontier, and the Kievskoe Highway is the road that heads that way, the route to Kyiv that gave it its name.

  4. 4. Scientific Town-Institution of Physiologis

    Ivan Pavlov did not do his best-known work in a lab in a big city. The Russian physiologist, who won the Nobel Prize in 1904 for his research on digestion and who became famous for the experiments on conditioned reflexes in dogs, spent his last years at Koltushi, a village on the low hills east of St Petersburg. Here, from the early 1930s, the Soviet state built him a whole scientific town, with laboratories, kennels, a monkey station and housing for his staff, in the hope that he would turn out a physiology of the mind. Pavlov, who lived from 1849 to 1936, said he wanted a place where animals and people could be studied in healthy conditions and surrounded by nature, and Koltushi was it.

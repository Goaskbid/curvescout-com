  1. 1. Kyo-o-gokoku-ji

    Kyoto has dozens of temples that could stand in for the city, but the five-storey pagoda of To-ji does the job better than any, because you can see it from the Shinkansen. Kyo-o-gokoku-ji is its formal name, and the temple was founded at the very beginning of the Heian capital, in the 790s, as one of two guardian temples placed on either side of the southern gate. Its pagoda, at about fifty-five metres, is the tallest wooden pagoda in Japan, and the version that stands today dates to the middle of the seventeenth century, rebuilt after fire by the third Tokugawa shogun, Iemitsu. Earlier ones were struck by lightning with some regularity, which shows the risk of building high in a thunderstorm country.

  2. 2. Nara Palace Site

    For most of the eighth century, Nara was the capital of Japan. The city, then called Heijō-kyō, was laid out on a grid modelled on the Chinese capital Chang'an, and at its northern end stood the imperial palace, Heijō-kyū, a vast compound of halls, offices and gardens. When the court moved away at the end of the century, the palace was abandoned and the land eventually returned to rice fields. Today the Nara Palace Site is a huge open space on the western side of the modern city, part of the UNESCO-listed Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara.

  3. 3. Fujiwara Palace Site

    Before Nara, before Kyoto, there was Fujiwara-kyō. In 694, the Empress Jitō moved the Japanese court to a new capital in the southern part of the Nara basin, the first in Japan to be laid out on a Chinese-style grid of streets, with the imperial palace at its centre. It lasted only sixteen years, until the court moved north to Nara in 710, but it marked a turning point in the history of the Japanese state.

  4. 4. Yakushi-ji

    Yakushi-ji is one of the great temples of Nara, the capital of Japan in the eighth century. It was founded by Emperor Tenmu in the late seventh century, praying for the recovery of his ill empress, and moved to its present site when the capital moved to Nara in 710. Dedicated to Yakushi Nyorai, the Buddha of healing, it is part of the UNESCO-listed Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara. The East Pagoda, which has stood since the eighth century, is one of the few surviving buildings from that era, and its layered roofs, with smaller roofs between each storey, create a rhythm described by one admirer as frozen music.

  1. 1. Leipzig

    On Monday evenings in the autumn of 1989, thousands of people gathered at the Nikolaikirche in Leipzig after the prayers for peace and walked around the ring road with candles, chanting that they were the people. The East German state could have sent in the tanks, and did not, and within weeks the Berlin Wall had fallen. Leipzig is proud of that, and rightly. The church still stands in its quiet square, with a classical interior of slender palm-like pillars in pastel colours, and a plaque outside records the demonstrations.

  2. 2. Naumburg Cathedral

    The twelve figures in the west choir of Naumburg Cathedral are among the most famous sculptures of the Middle Ages, and once you have seen them, you understand why. Carved around the middle of the thirteenth century by an unknown artist known as the Naumburg Master, they represent the founders and benefactors of the cathedral, noblemen and noblewomen who had died two centuries earlier, but they look like real people, with individual faces, expressions and gestures. The most famous is Uta, the wife of Margrave Ekkehard, standing with her cloak drawn up to her face, cool and enigmatic, who has become an icon of German art. The cathedral, with its Romanesque and Gothic architecture and its rich sculpture, was listed by UNESCO in 2018.

  4. 4. House with Balcony access - Mittelbreite 1

    In the 1920s, the German architect Walter Gropius was asked to design housing for working families, and he approached it as an experiment in how to build well and cheaply. The Bauhaus school had moved to Dessau in 1925, and Gropius used the opportunity to build a whole estate at Torten, on the southern edge of town, with rows of simple concrete houses designed for mass production. A few years later, in 1930, he built a block of balcony-access houses nearby, in which flats were reached by open walkways on the outside rather than by internal stairs, a design that saved space and money.

  5. 5. House with Balcony access - Mittelbreite 6

    Hannes Meyer was a Swiss architect and the second director of the Bauhaus, and in Dessau he designed a row of houses that put his ideas into concrete form. The Laubenganghäuser in Törten, finished around 1930, are blocks of flats reached by external walkways, the balcony access, so that each floor has a long open gallery along the front. It was a cheap, efficient way to give small flats light and ventilation, and it was an experiment in social housing, in an age when a decent flat was a political question.

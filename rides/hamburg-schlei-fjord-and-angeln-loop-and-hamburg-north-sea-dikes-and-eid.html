  1. 1. Neumünster fuel escape

    Neumünster is not a town people ride to, but it is a very good town to ride out of. It lies in the middle of Schleswig-Holstein, where the motorways from Hamburg, Kiel and the north meet, and it has the practical, working feel of a place built by industry. For centuries it was a centre of cloth and leather, and the textile mills lined the little Schwale river, though most of them closed in the twentieth century. The town grew from a monastery, founded in the early twelfth century by the missionary Vicelin, and his name is still borne by the main church.

  2. 2. Schleswig harbour sight

    Schleswig lies at the head of the Schlei, a narrow, winding arm of the Baltic that reaches forty kilometres inland and looks more like a river than a fjord. The harbour is small and cheerful, with sailing boats, a few pleasure steamers and a promenade where people stroll and eat fish rolls. Across the water you can see the roofs of the old town, and above them the slim spire of the cathedral, whose tower rises to well over a hundred metres and dominates the skyline for miles.

  3. 3. Geltinger Birk coast edge

    The Geltinger Birk is the north-eastern tip of Angeln, a low, windy peninsula that pushes out into the Baltic where the Flensburg Fjord opens to the sea. It is one of the largest nature reserves on the Baltic coast of Schleswig-Holstein, a landscape of dunes, salt meadows, lagoons and old alder woodland, with large areas given over to semi-wild grazing. Hardy cattle and small horses roam the meadows, and their grazing keeps the land open and rich in flowers and insects. On a clear day, you can see across the water to the Danish island of Als.

  4. 4. Kappeln Schlei bridge

    Kappeln lies on the Schlei, a long narrow inlet of the Baltic that reaches forty-odd kilometres inland, like a fjord that forgot to be steep. The bridge itself is the town's emblem: a lifting bridge from the 1920s that opens to let ships through, so be prepared to wait a few minutes while the roadway rises and the sailing boats glide past.

  5. 5. Back to Neumünster

    Neumünster is a town of seventy-eight thousand, the biggest between Hamburg and Kiel, and it earns its keep as a place where roads, railways and fortunes cross. It grew rich on cloth, and for much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries its mills and tanneries made it one of the great textile and leather towns of northern Germany. A museum of cloth and technology tells that story, and the old industrial buildings still stand in the quarters near the river.

  6. 6. Itzehoe fuel gate

    Itzehoe does not have a famous skyline, and that is rather the point. It is a solid town of about thirty thousand on the Stör river in the flat marshland of Schleswig-Holstein, and its job in this ride is that of a gate, the last reliable fuel and breakfast before the open country of the North Sea coast. The town sits some sixty kilometres northwest of Hamburg, and the name goes far back, with a fortress called Esesfeld said to have been built here in the early ninth century during the wars of Charlemagne against the Saxons.

  7. 7. Friedrichstadt canal stop

    Friedrichstadt looks, at first glance, like a piece of Holland that has wandered north and settled down in Schleswig-Holstein, and the resemblance is no accident. Duke Frederick III of Holstein-Gottorf founded the town in 1621 as a haven for Dutch Remonstrants, a Protestant sect that was being persecuted at home, and he hoped they would bring trade and skills to his marshy corner of the country. They brought canals, gabled brick houses and a tidy grid of streets, and the town is still laid out that way today.

  8. 8. Sankt Peter-Ording beach air

    Sankt Peter-Ording has one of the widest beaches in Europe, a vast plain of pale sand that stretches for some twelve kilometres along the western edge of the Eiderstedt peninsula and runs out so far at low tide that the sea is a thin line on the horizon. On the dunes stand the famous stilt houses, wooden restaurants and beach huts on tall legs, built that way because the North Sea sometimes comes right up to the dune foot at high water and storm tides. They look like something from a child's drawing, and they are a charming place for a coffee.

  9. 9. Eider barrage photo

    For photographers, the Eider barrage is a study in lines. The great steel gates, the concrete piers and the long road across the structure make strong geometric shapes against a sky that is usually enormous, and the water on both sides changes colour with the weather and tide. It is a very graphic subject, and it rewards a bit of planning. Morning and evening light bring out the texture of the metal, and a low sun from the west turns the water silver and the sky pink.

Why this ride

A 300 km one-way ride from near Sutton, about 4 h 29 of riding. The stops in between: near Wingrave and Morden. The climbs: near Milford and near Royal Tunbridge Wells.

The stops

  1. 1. London motorcycle escapes

    Start here.

    The Greensand Ridge runs across Bedfordshire like a low spine, and Potton and Gamlingay lie on its eastern edge, a pair of small towns of brick and clay tile that once made their living from the soil. Potton, with nearly five thousand people, was famous for its market gardens, and Gamlingay, with about thirty-two hundred, has an old heath and woodland, the Gamlingay Wood, which has been managed for nearly a thousand years and is one of the oldest in the county. Everton, a tiny village nearby, sits on the ridge with views over the Ivel valley.

    Read the complete London motorcycle escapes write-up.

  2. 2. Food stop near Wingrave

    63 km in. Food stop near Wingrave is where this day stops to eat.

  3. 3. Photo stop near Milford

    174 km in. This is where the road stops climbing.

    Milford is a village just south of Godalming in Surrey, on the old road from London to Portsmouth. The A3 runs past it, climbing south towards Hindhead and the Devil's Punch Bowl, a huge natural hollow in the sandstone hills, and that climb is the reason this is a high point on the ride.

    Read the complete Photo stop near Milford write-up.

  4. 4. Morden

    222 km in.

    The Northern Line ends here, deep in suburban South London, and Morden is the place commuters come home to. It is a district of semi-detached houses, parks and shopping streets, built mostly in the 1920s and 1930s when the Tube arrived and turned fields into suburbs almost overnight.

    Read the complete Morden write-up.

  5. 5. High point near Royal Tunbridge Wells

    285 km in. High point near Royal Tunbridge Wells tops the climb.

    Tunbridge Wells owes its existence to a rusty spring. In 1606 a young nobleman, Lord North, was resting in the woods here and noticed a pool with an orange scum; he tried the water, felt better and told the world. Within a few decades the court was coming down from London to drink it, and the Pantiles, a raised, colonnaded walk with trees along it, became the place to be seen taking the waters. The town was granted its Royal prefix in 1909 by Edward VII, and has been the butt of letters to the editor ever since, usually signed by someone disgusted.

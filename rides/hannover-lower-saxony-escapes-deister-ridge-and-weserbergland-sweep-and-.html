Why this ride

From Seelze it runs 450 km to Pattensen; allow about 9 h 44 of riding. On the way it passes Springe, Hameln old-town river stop and Rinteln Weser overlook. The climb of the day is Bad Münder ridge road. Much of it runs through woodland.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hannover west edge

    Hannover's western edge is mostly rail yards, canal bank and green fringe, and it tells the city's industrial story more honestly than the old town does. The Mittelland Canal, the long artificial waterway that links the Rhine and the Elbe, passes near Seelze, and barges move quietly along it all day, a calm, almost hypnotic sight. The Leine river is close too, with its wide floodplain, and the city's factories once clustered along both.

  2. 2. Springe fuel gate

    Springe is the small, practical town at the foot of the Deister, the long wooded ridge that rises southwest of Hanover and gives the city its nearest bit of real hill country. It is not a place most people plan a day around, and that is fine. It is where the plain ends and the hills begin, and the first sign of that is a sudden change in the road, from straight and flat to curving and shaded.

  3. 3. Bad Münder ridge road

    Between the Deister and the Weser lies a small cluster of low, forested ridges, and the road above Bad Münder runs along the edge of one of them. The land here is part of the Weser Uplands, a modest country of beech woods, limestone outcrops and small spa towns, and the name Süntel belongs to the ridge just beyond. It is not high, but it is steep enough on its flanks to make for lively riding.

  4. 4. Hameln old-town river stop

    Everyone knows the story, and in Hameln you cannot get away from it. In 1284, according to the legend, a stranger in a coloured coat came to town to rid it of rats with his pipe, was cheated of his fee, and led the children away instead. The tale is probably built on some real event, maybe an emigration or a catastrophe, but nobody knows which. The town has embraced it with enthusiasm: rats in the shop windows, rat-shaped bread, a summer play staged in the street and a mechanical glockenspiel show at the Hochzeitshaus that draws a crowd every day.

  5. 5. Rinteln Weser overlook

    Rinteln is a Weser town that sits in a narrow bend of the river, with the ridge of the Weser Hills on one side and low farmland on the other. The overlook is up on that ridge, where the road leaves the town and climbs through trees to a spot with a good view of the valley. From it you can see the river wind through the meadows, the town's church spires and red roofs in the bend, and the next line of hills in blue haze behind.

  6. 6. Bad Nenndorf coffee return

    Bad Nenndorf is a spa town that never grew into a resort, and that suits it. It sits in the Schaumburg countryside west of Hanover, at the foot of the low wooded ridge of the Deister, and its trade is sulphurous water. Springs here were developed as a bath in the late eighteenth century under the rulers of Hesse-Kassel, and a park of old trees, colonnades and bathhouses survives from that period. The smell of the water is distinctive, and the baths are still used for health treatments, particularly for joint and skin complaints. The town is quiet, tidy and slightly formal.

  7. 7. Back to Hannover

    Hannover does not seek attention, which is part of its character. The capital of Lower Saxony, with about half a million people, is a flat, green, practical city on the Leine river, famous for its trade fairs, and for speaking what Germans consider the purest standard German. Large parts of the centre were destroyed in the Second World War, and the rebuilding is mostly plain, but there are fine things inside it: the Herrenhausen Gardens, a baroque garden of clipped hedges and fountains, the Maschsee, a big lake laid out in the 1930s where locals sail and jog, and the new town hall, a copper-domed building from 1913 with a lift that rises inside the dome.

  8. 8. Hannover south edge

    Pattensen is a small town on the southern fringe of Hannover, in the Calenberger Land, where the flat plain of the city meets the first gentle swells of the hill country that runs south toward the Weser. Its old centre has half-timbered houses and a medieval church, and the streets have a sleepy, slightly farm-like atmosphere even though the suburbs of the city are barely a few kilometres away. For a rider wanting to head south, this is the right place to start, because the main roads out of the city are behind you and the quiet roads begin almost at once.

  9. 9. Hann. Münden three rivers

    Hannoversch Münden is where two rivers lose their names. The Werra and the Fulda meet here, and from this point on the water is called the Weser, which flows north for more than four hundred kilometres to the North Sea. The town sits at the meeting point, with water on three sides, and has so many half-timbered houses, more than seven hundred, that it feels like a stage set, with carved beams, painted gables and a cobbled main street.

  10. 10. Weserstein photo stop

    There is an old verse on the stone: where the Werra and the Fulda kiss, they lose their names, and from the kiss the Weser flows, German to the sea. The Weserstein stands on the tip of the land where the two rivers meet, in a small park on the edge of the old town of Hann. Münden, and it has been put up for a long time, so the stone has the patina of weather and moss.

  11. 11. Göttingen food stop

    Göttingen is a university town that has never been shy about it. The Georg-August University was founded in 1737 and became one of the leading centres of science in Europe, and the roll of people who studied or taught here includes the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss, the physicists Max Planck and Werner Heisenberg, the Brothers Grimm, and a long list of Nobel laureates. The student population gives the city a lively, young feel, with bicycles everywhere.

  12. 12. Northeim fuel gate

    Northeim is a Hanseatic town that gets passed by, which is a pity. It sits on the Leine river, in the broad valley between the Harz mountains to the east and the Solling hills to the west, and it has a long history, with its origins in the early Middle Ages and a time as a member of the Hanseatic League. The medieval counts of Northeim were once powerful in the region, and one of them, Otto, became Duke of Bavaria in the eleventh century.

  13. 13. Einbeck return road

    Einbeck is where bock beer began. In the Middle Ages the town was one of the great brewing centres of the Hanseatic League, and its strong, dark beer was exported across northern Europe. When Bavarian brewers copied it, the name Einbecker was heard as ein Bock, a billy goat, and so a beer style got its name. The town still has a brewery, and the goat appears on the labels.

  14. 14. Back to Hannover

    Hannover is not the first name that comes to mind when riders talk about great destinations, and it would probably say that is fine. It is a big, calm, practical city of half a million people on the northern edge of the German hills, where the flat North German Plain begins to roll up toward the Deister and the Weser Uplands.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cave de Mareuil-sur- d’Aÿ - Underground

    Beneath the quiet village of Mareuil-sur-Aÿ, in the Marne valley east of Épernay, lie cellars cut into the chalk, and they are where the story of champagne is really told. The wine is made in two stages. The first ferments in vats in the autumn, and the second takes place in the bottle, down in the cool and dark, for months or years, and the chalk is the perfect place for it, holding a steady ten degrees all year and breathing out a faint damp, mineral smell. The tunnels, hewn by hand centuries ago, branch and bend beneath the vineyards, lined with bottles stacked neck to base.

  2. 2. Caves Pommery

    Eighteen metres below the streets of Reims, a stone staircase of a hundred and sixteen steps leads down into one of the most remarkable wine cellars in the world. The Pommery caves were dug as Roman chalk quarries, the crayères, whose tall, pyramid-shaped galleries were later linked by tunnels into a network about eighteen kilometres long. When a widow, Madame Pommery, took over her late husband's champagne business in 1858, she saw the potential of the chalk pits and built her cellars there, as well as a huge neo-Gothic and Elizabethan-style estate above ground.

  3. 3. Coteaux d’Hautvillers

    On the hill above Hautvillers, the rows of vines run straight down to the Marne, and the colour changes with the season: bare, brown and clipped in winter, green in summer and red-gold in October. This is the cradle of Champagne, or at least one of its most famous corners. The Benedictine abbey of Saint-Pierre, in the village, was home to the monk Dom Pérignon in the late seventeenth century, who did not invent the sparkling wine, as legend says, but improved its making and blending, and whose name now belongs to a prestige cuvée.

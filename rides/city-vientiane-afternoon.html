Why this ride

A 139 km round trip from Vientiane - roughly 2 h 24 in the saddle. On the way it passes Phu Phrabat, The Phu Phrabat Historical Park, The Sīma Cultural Site at Wat Phra Phuthab and back through The Phu Phrabat Historical Park. It crosses from Laos into Thailand - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Vientiane

    Vientiane is the quietest capital you are likely to meet, a low, tree-lined city on the northern bank of the Mekong where the river forms the border with Thailand. About two hundred thousand people live in the city proper, and the pace is so gentle that visitors often forget it is a capital at all. Monks in saffron robes walk the early streets, and in the evening the riverbank fills with people jogging, eating and watching the sunset over Thailand.

  2. 2. Phu Phrabat

    Imagine a forest of sandstone mushrooms, boulders balanced on pillars and arches sculpted by wind and rain, and you are close to the setting of Phu Phra Bat. The place lies on a low plateau in Udon Thani province, in north-east Thailand's Isan region, about eighteen kilometres from the town of Nam Som. Over millions of years, erosion has carved the rock into strange shapes, and people have used the overhangs and caves for shelter and worship since prehistoric times.

  3. 3. The Phu Phrabat Historical Park

    The park of Phu Phra Bat holds more than its rocks, and what lies between them tells the story. Among the sandstone outcrops of this north-east Thai plateau, hundreds of stones have been set upright in the ground, flat slabs shaped like leaves, which are the sema that mark the boundary of a sacred space. This practice is typical of the Dvaravati period, between about the seventh and eleventh centuries, when Buddhism flourished in the region before the Khmer empire extended its power.

  4. 4. The Sīma Cultural Site at Wat Phra Phuthab

    A sima is a boundary stone, and in the north-east of Thailand, in the region called Isan, hundreds of them stand in fields and temple grounds, many of them more than a thousand years old. Monks placed them in rings around ordination halls, to mark the sacred space where the Buddhist community could meet, and in the Dvaravati period, between roughly the seventh and eleventh centuries, carvers began to decorate them with scenes from the life of the Buddha. The sima at the site in this listing are part of a UNESCO inscription that recognises this tradition, found nowhere else in such density.

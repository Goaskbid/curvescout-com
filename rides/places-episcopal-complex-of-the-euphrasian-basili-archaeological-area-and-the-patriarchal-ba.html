Why this ride

A 329 km round trip from Poreč - roughly 5 h in the saddle. It goes by way of Škocjan Caves, Archaeological Area and the Patriarchal Ba and back through Škocjan Caves. It crosses Croatia, Slovenia and Italy - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

The roads

Croatia Istrian Hill Town Route

92 km from Episcopal Complex of the Euphrasian Basili to Škocjan Caves, mostly on A9. Up about 830 m to 522 m, then 410 m back down the far side. Fill the tank before the climb.

The stops

  1. 1. Episcopal Complex of the Euphrasian Basili

    Start here.

    Poreč sits on a little limestone peninsula on the western coast of Istria, and the old town still follows the grid the Romans laid down, with the Decumanus running straight down the middle. The Euphrasian Basilica is tucked a few lanes from the main street, behind a quiet atrium, and it is easy to walk past if you are looking for something grand from the outside. Inside is the reason it earned its UNESCO listing: a sixth-century church, built under Bishop Euphrasius, that kept its Byzantine mosaics through a millennium and a half of earthquakes, Venetian rule and tourism.

    Read the complete Episcopal Complex of the Euphrasian Basili write-up.

  2. 2. Škocjan Caves

    92 km in.

    Underneath the sleepy stone villages of the Slovenian Karst, a river called the Reka plunges into the earth and carves one of the largest underground canyons known anywhere. The Škocjan Caves, a UNESCO site since 1986, are the reason the word karst became the scientific name for this kind of limestone landscape everywhere in the world. Inside, a narrow footbridge spans the Reka gorge far above the roaring water, the chamber walls disappear into darkness, and the scale is so huge that your mind struggles to accept it is all underground.

    Read the complete Škocjan Caves write-up.

  3. 3. Archaeological Area and the Patriarchal Ba

    165 km in.

    Aquileia today is a village of fewer than three thousand people in the flat farmland of Friuli, near the head of the Adriatic, but two thousand years ago it was one of the largest and richest cities of the Roman Empire. Founded as a Latin colony in 181 BC to guard the northeastern approaches to Italy, it grew into a great trading port, connected to the sea by a river and a canal, through which amber, wine, oil and glass moved between the Mediterranean and the lands beyond the Alps. Attila sacked it in 452, and many of its people fled to the lagoons, where their descendants helped found Venice and Grado.

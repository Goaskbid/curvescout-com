  1. 1. Tbilisi

    Tbilisi spreads along the Mtkvari river in a bowl of dry hills, and its first impression is of balconies. Old houses lean over narrow streets with carved wooden verandas, sulphur bathhouses sit under domes of brick in the Abanotubani district, and on the ridge above, the ruined Narikala fortress watches the city. The name Tbilisi comes from a Georgian word for warm, and the story says that a hunting king found hot springs here and decided to found a city.

  2. 2. Monasteries of Haghpat and Sanahin

    Haghpat and Sanahin sit on the opposite edges of a deep canyon in northern Armenia, and between them they hold some of the finest medieval religious architecture in the Caucasus. The two monasteries, founded in the tenth century and expanded over the next three hundred years, stand on terraces above the Debed river, near the town of Alaverdi. They are built of dark basalt and pale tuff, with steep roofs, carved crosses and cool, dim interiors, and they are on the UNESCO World Heritage list.

  3. 3. Monastery of Haghpat

    Haghpat is a monastery on a ledge, and the ledge is steep. The road from Alaverdi climbs through a series of tight bends, up from the Debed canyon to a village of stone houses and orchards on the plateau, where the monastery stands behind a low wall, its dark domes and gabled roofs catching the light. It was founded in the late tenth century, under the patronage of the Kiurikid royal family, and its main church, dedicated to the Holy Cross, was finished in the early 990s.

  4. 4. Sanahin Bridge

    The bridge at Sanahin is older than most of the countries in Europe. A single high arch of dark stone, it spans the Debed river just below the town of Alaverdi, a work of the twelfth century that has outlasted wars, floods and Soviet roadbuilding. It was built for the caravans that used the valley as a route between Tiflis and the Armenian highlands, and it is still sturdy enough to walk across, with a rough cobbled surface and a steep rise in the middle.

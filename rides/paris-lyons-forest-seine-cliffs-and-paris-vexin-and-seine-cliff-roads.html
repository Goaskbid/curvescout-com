Why this ride

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Paris north-west edge

    It is a hard-working suburban landscape of tower blocks, tram lines, small houses with allotments and the grey sheen of a river that has seen a lot of traffic. La Défense's towers lift above the horizon to the south, and the Seine runs wide and sluggish, with barges and the occasional rower. It is not a place to linger, but for a morning ride it is a useful starting point, close to the motorway and the fast roads west.

  2. 2. Vexin ridge road

    The Vexin is a frontier country, and it shows. The plateau between the Seine and the Oise was for centuries the border between the kingdom of France and the duchy of Normandy, and the river Epte, which flows along its western edge, marked the line. In 911, the Viking chief Rollo and the French king Charles the Simple agreed at Saint-Clair-sur-Epte to give Normandy to the Norsemen, and the Vexin was the buffer between them. The history gives the villages a particular character, with fortified churches and the remains of castles.

  3. 3. La Roche-Guyon Seine view

    Above La Roche-Guyon, the Seine swings through a great meander, and the view from the cliff road takes in a wide loop of the river, green islands, poplar lines and pale chalk bluffs. The valley here is cut into thick white limestone, and the river has carved long, steep walls on the outer bends, which glow almost pink in the late afternoon. Barges pass slowly below, and the fields on the flat plains inside the loops are patched with wheat, rape and maize. It is one of the most peaceful panoramas anywhere within an hour or so of the capital.

  4. 4. Lyons-la-Forêt lunch

    Beech trees are the point. The forest of Lyons, a few kilometres from the village, is one of the largest and finest beech woods in Europe, about ten thousand hectares of tall, silver-trunked trees that stand in cathedral rows, and in spring, when the leaves are just out, the light under them is green and gold. In autumn it turns copper. The roads that cross it are some of the best in Normandy for easy riding, with long, smooth curves, a good surface and little traffic, and the sense of riding through a vast, quiet hall is hard to beat.

  5. 5. Gisors return gate

    Gisors is a Norman town that stands guard on the border with the Île-de-France. It lies on the Epte, the old border river, and its great castle, a motte-and-bailey fortress with a tall octagonal keep, was built by the Normans in the late eleventh century and strengthened by the kings of England, before it fell to the French king Philip Augustus in the twelfth. It was one of the key strongholds on the frontier, and the walls and keep still dominate the town. A local legend says that the Knights Templar hid treasure in the castle, and it has kept treasure hunters busy for generations, though nothing has been found.

  7. 7. Mantes-la-Jolie fuel

    Mantes-la-Jolie carries its pretty name with a straight face, though the town of forty-four thousand is mostly practical rather than pretty, a Seine-side workhorse about fifty kilometres downstream from Paris. Tradition says Henri IV gave it the nickname, and the one thing that truly justifies it is the collegiate church of Notre-Dame, a Gothic building of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries whose façade and proportions echo the cathedral in Paris so closely that architects call it a rehearsal for it. Walk in and the light through the nave is worth five minutes even if you are in leathers.

  8. 8. La Roche-Guyon

    La Roche-Guyon is squeezed between the Seine and a wall of chalk, and the village has the look of a place that has been fitted into the only space available. The château, one of the oldest in the area, backs straight into the cliff, with rooms and a stairway hollowed from the rock, and a medieval keep on the crest above is linked to it by a long covered passage. For centuries, it belonged to the La Rochefoucauld family, whose most famous member, the seventeenth-century moralist, wrote his sharp little maxims about human vanity, and the family still has links to the house.

  9. 9. Vétheuil Seine bends

    Claude Monet moved to Vétheuil in 1878, and the village has never forgotten it. He was poor, his wife Camille was ill and the family shared a house with another household, but the Seine here, curving gently under the chalk bluff, gave him a subject that he painted again and again: the church on its hill seen across the water, the river in winter ice, the poplars and the light on the islands. Camille died in 1879 and was buried in the village cemetery, and Monet left in 1881, settling at Giverny two years later, where he would spend the rest of his life.

  10. 10. Auvers-sur-Oise

    Few villages have as much weight on their shoulders as Auvers-sur-Oise. Vincent van Gogh came here in May 1890, lodged in a small room above an inn, and in the seventy days he stayed painted around seventy canvases, among them the church, the wheat fields and the town hall bedecked with flags, before he died of a gunshot wound in July. He and his brother Theo lie side by side in the little cemetery above the fields, under a blanket of ivy. The room he rented at the Auberge Ravoux is preserved almost bare, and it is more moving for being so small.

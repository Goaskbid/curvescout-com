  1. 1. Alta Valle secchia

    The upper valley of the Secchia is the high Apennine country of Reggio Emilia, a landscape of beech woods, bare grassy ridges and deep river valleys, where Emilia gives way to Tuscany. The river rises near the Passo del Cerreto, at about 1,260 metres, and flows north through sparsely populated hills before dropping to the plain and the Po. The region is part of the Tuscan-Emilian Apennine National Park, with wolves, golden eagles and red deer in the high country.

  2. 2. Cathedral

    Modena's cathedral is one of the masterpieces of Romanesque architecture in Europe, and the inscription on its facade still names its makers with pride: the architect Lanfranco and the sculptor Wiligelmo. Work began in 1099 to house the relics of Saint Geminianus, the city's patron, and the result is a building of pale stone, with arcaded galleries, lions supporting the columns of the portal, and carved panels telling the story of Genesis with an expressive power that still surprises. The Ghirlandina, the cathedral's bell tower, leans slightly and rises high above the rooftops, and together with the Piazza Grande they form a UNESCO site.

  3. 3. Sabbioneta

    Sabbioneta is a Renaissance experiment: a whole town built from scratch in the second half of the sixteenth century by Vespasiano Gonzaga, a soldier, scholar and minor prince of the great Mantuan family, who wanted a capital laid out according to the ideals of the age. He got it. Inside its star-shaped walls the streets run in a regular grid, and in a few hundred metres you pass a ducal palace, a long gallery built to show off his collection of ancient sculpture, a summer palace, and one of the earliest purpose-built theatres in Europe, the Teatro all'Antica, designed by Vincenzo Scamozzi. Sabbioneta and Mantua were listed together by UNESCO in 2008 as two contrasting visions of the Renaissance city.

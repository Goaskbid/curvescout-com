  1. 1. Cinque Terre and Portovenere

    Five villages cling to the cliffs of the Ligurian coast, Monterosso, Vernazza, Corniglia, Manarola and Riomaggiore, and a little further south the old harbour of Portovenere guards the entrance to the Gulf of La Spezia, often called the Gulf of Poets because Byron and Shelley both lived around it. The whole coast is a World Heritage Site, and what UNESCO honours is the work of generations of farmers who built stone terraces on slopes so steep that they are almost vertical, with thousands of kilometres of dry-stone walls holding vines and olives. The sweet Sciacchetrà wine from these terraces is rare and expensive.

  2. 2. Evaporitic Karst and Caves of Northern Ape

    Gypsum dissolves in water far faster than limestone, and in the upper valley of the Secchia, in the Reggio Emilia Apennines, that simple fact has produced a strange landscape of white cliffs, sinkholes, caves and underground rivers. The Evaporitic Karst and Caves of the Northern Apennines were added to the World Heritage list in 2023, and this area near Castelnovo ne' Monti and Villa Minozzo holds some of the most striking outcrops, ancient Triassic gypsum that glitters in the sun and forms steep walls above the river. Near the Secchia, the springs of Poiano gush out of the rock with a strong flow of slightly salty water, fed by the karst system.

  3. 3. Cathedral

    Modena's cathedral is one of the masterpieces of Romanesque architecture in Europe, and the inscription on its facade still names its makers with pride: the architect Lanfranco and the sculptor Wiligelmo. Work began in 1099 to house the relics of Saint Geminianus, the city's patron, and the result is a building of pale stone, with arcaded galleries, lions supporting the columns of the portal, and carved panels telling the story of Genesis with an expressive power that still surprises. The Ghirlandina, the cathedral's bell tower, leans slightly and rises high above the rooftops, and together with the Piazza Grande they form a UNESCO site.

  1. 1. Vilnius

    Vilnius is a city that takes a few hours to like and a few days to love. The old town, one of the largest surviving medieval centres in Eastern Europe, is a tangle of cobbled lanes and courtyards on the banks of the Neris and Vilnia rivers, and it is crowded with churches, more than forty of them by most counts, in Gothic, Renaissance and especially Baroque styles. From the top of Gediminas Hill you see red roofs, green domes and the dark line of forest beyond.

  3. 3. Kaunas

    Kaunas sits where Lithuania's two greatest rivers, the Nemunas and the Neris, meet, and that gives the city its shape: an old town on a tongue of land between the waters, a newer town running east along a long straight pedestrian avenue, Laisvės alėja, and green hills on every side. Between the two world wars, when Vilnius was held by Poland, Kaunas served as the provisional capital of independent Lithuania, and in those twenty years it filled with ambitious modernist buildings, whose white, clean lines have now been recognised by UNESCO as a World Heritage site.

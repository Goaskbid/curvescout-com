Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Wien - Legionslager Umwehrung

    Vienna began as a Roman legionary camp called Vindobona, built around the end of the first century on a bluff above the Danube and a small tributary. The camp held a legion, first the Thirteenth and later the Tenth, and it was a large rectangle surrounded by a wall and ditch, with gates on each side. A civilian settlement grew outside it. The Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius died in this region in 180, and one tradition says he died at Vindobona, though the evidence is not certain.

  2. 2. Zeiselmauer - Kastell Hufeisenturm

    Zeiselmauer is a village that grew up inside a Roman fort, and it never quite moved out. The late Roman fort of Cannabiaca guarded this stretch of the Danube near Tulln, part of the frontier of the province of Noricum, and its walls, gate and towers were absorbed into the village over the centuries. One of the best-preserved features is a horseshoe-shaped tower, typical of late Roman fortifications, and a later fortlet within the fort, with walls standing several metres high, is one of the most impressive Roman buildings in Austria. Zeiselmauer is part of the Danube Limes, listed by UNESCO in 2021.

  3. 3. Zwentendorf - Kastell

    The Danube flows broad and brown past Zwentendorf, a quiet village on the Tullnerfeld plain west of Vienna, and for once the most famous building in the area is not the oldest one. Zwentendorf is where Austria built a nuclear power station in the 1970s and then, after a national referendum in 1978, decided never to switch it on. The plant still stands by the river, complete and unused, now a training site and a curiosity.

  4. 4. Mautern - Kastell Ostbereich

    In the fifth century, as Roman authority along the Danube collapsed, a holy man named Severinus lived at a place called Favianis, organising food supplies, ransoming captives and negotiating with barbarian kings on behalf of the remaining Roman population. His biography, written by his disciple Eugippius, is one of the most vivid accounts of the end of Roman rule in central Europe, and Favianis is generally identified with Mautern, a small town on the south bank of the Danube opposite Krems. The Roman fort here, parts of which survive in the town walls, is part of the Danube Limes World Heritage listing.

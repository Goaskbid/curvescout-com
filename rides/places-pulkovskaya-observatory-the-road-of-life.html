Why this ride

A 268 km round trip from Shushary - roughly 3 h 47 in the saddle. On the way it passes Zelenogorsky, The Road of Life and Pulkovskaya Observatory. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

The roads

Saint Petersburg Imperial Shore

From Pulkovskaya Observatory to Zelenogorsky is 66 km, mostly on КАД. Hardly any climbing; it runs between 4 and 76 m. Watch for sand and spray near the water.

The stops

  1. 1. Pulkovskaya Observatory

    Start here.

    On the Pulkovo heights, the low ridge south of St Petersburg, an observatory has been watching the sky since 1839. It was founded by Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve, the German-born astronomer who became its first director, and it quickly became one of the leading observatories in the world, with the largest refracting telescope of its day. The Pulkovo meridian, running through the observatory's main building, served as the reference for Russian maps for many years. Struve also led the measurement of the Struve Geodetic Arc, a chain of survey triangulations stretching from the Arctic Ocean to the Black Sea, which helped determine the size and shape of the earth and is now a UNESCO site.

    Read the complete Pulkovskaya Observatory write-up.

  2. 2. Zelenogorsky

    66 km in. The ride passes about 6 km from it.

    The northern shore of the Gulf of Finland, west of Saint Petersburg, was for centuries the summer playground of the capital, and Zelenogorsk, which the Finns called Terijoki, was the best known of its resort towns.

    Read the complete Zelenogorsky write-up.

  3. 3. The Road of Life

    186 km in.

    On the south-eastern shore of Lake Ladoga, east of Saint Petersburg, a road ends at the water. This is the start of the Road of Life, the supply route across the ice that kept Leningrad alive during the siege of 1941 to 1944. The siege lasted 872 days, from September 1941 to January 1944, and it cut the city off from nearly everything except the lake. In the first winter, when Ladoga froze, trucks and horse-drawn sledges began to cross the ice with food and fuel, and on the return journey they carried out women, children and the sick. Lorries broke through the ice, and bombing and artillery attacked the route, but it kept going.

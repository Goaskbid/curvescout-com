Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Guadalajara

    Guadalajara is Mexico's second city in many ways, and the heart of its mariachi and tequila traditions. Mariachi music, the charro horsemanship and the tequila spirit all trace their cultural roots to the state of Jalisco, and the capital is where they come together. The historic centre has big colonial buildings, wide plazas and a cathedral with two towers that were rebuilt after an earthquake in the nineteenth century. Murals by José Clemente Orozco, one of the great Mexican muralists, cover the walls and dome of the Hospicio Cabañas, a vast neoclassical complex that is a UNESCO site.

  2. 2. Zona nucleo 01

    The blue agave takes seven or eight years to ripen, and in all that time it does nothing but sit in the red volcanic soil and look like a giant spiky pineapple. Then a jimador arrives with a long-handled blade called a coa, strips the leaves, and cuts out the heart, which looks like a huge pineapple and weighs as much as a small child. The hearts are baked, crushed and fermented, and what comes out of the still is tequila. The landscape that makes this possible, the agave fields and distilleries around the town of Tequila in Jalisco, was listed as a World Heritage site in 2006.

  3. 3. Zona nucleo 02

    The landscape of blue agave around Tequila is the best-known piece of this part of Jalisco, but its deeper story lies in a hillside near the village of Teuchitlán, where a civilisation built circles. Between about 300 BC and AD 900, the people of what archaeologists call the Teuchitlán tradition raised round pyramids with an outer ring of platforms, in a pattern that looks from the air like a target, and the locals call them guachimontones. They did not leave a written record, and no one knows what they called themselves, but the architecture suggests ritual and perhaps astronomy. The site's main group, with several of these circles, has been excavated and restored, and it sits on a lava slope above a small lake with the cone of the Tequila volcano on the horizon.

  4. 4. Agave Landscape and Ancient Industrial Fac

    Rows of blue-grey agave stretch across the red volcanic hills of Jalisco in lines so regular they look drawn with a ruler, and they climb the slopes of the Tequila volcano as far as the eye can see. This is the heart of the agave landscape, where the blue Weber agave has been grown for tequila for more than three hundred years, and UNESCO recognised it in 2006 with the old distilleries and towns around it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Subak Landscape of Catur Angga Batukaru

    Water is the organising principle of Bali, and the subak is how people organise water. A subak is a farmers' cooperative that manages irrigation for a group of rice fields, with its own rules, its own officials and its own temple, and the system has been working for roughly a thousand years. UNESCO listed the Subak Landscape in 2012, with the temple of Pura Luhur Batukaru on the slopes of Mount Batukaru, the terraced fields around Jatiluwih and the lake temple on Lake Batur among its elements. The philosophy behind it is called Tri Hita Karana, harmony among people, nature and the divine.

  2. 2. Royal Water Temple Pura Taman Ayun

    Pura Taman Ayun means the temple of the beautiful garden, and it is a royal temple surrounded by a wide moat. The rajas of Mengwi built it in 1634, as the main temple of their small kingdom, and the broad lawns, canals and pavilions give it the air of a palace garden as much as a shrine. Across the inner courtyard rise the merus, shrines with tiered thatched roofs of black ijuk fibre, some with as many as eleven levels, which represent mountains and the homes of the gods. The temple lies in the middle of a district of rice fields, and it is part of the Subak cultural landscape that UNESCO listed in 2012, because the water for those fields is managed by village irrigation societies, the subak, which have been sharing it for a thousand years.

  3. 3. Supreme Water Temple Pura Ulun Danu Batur

    The temple stands on the rim of a caldera, and the caldera is vast. Mount Batur, in the north-east of Bali, sits inside a ring of cliffs some ten kilometres across, left by two huge eruptions tens of thousands of years ago, and in the bowl lies Lake Batur, a crescent of dark water. Pura Ulun Danu Batur is the second most important temple on the island, dedicated to Dewi Danu, the goddess of the lake, whose waters feed the springs and irrigation canals that run down the mountains and water the rice fields of the south. For Balinese farmers, this is where the water comes from, and offerings are made here to keep it flowing.

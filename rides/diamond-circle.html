Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Husavik

    Húsavík is the town that decided to make whales its trade and has done very well from the decision. It sits on the shore of Skjálfandi bay in north Iceland, facing across the water to the snowy peaks of the Tröllaskagi range, and a row of brightly painted wooden boats waits at the harbour for visitors. The bay is one of the best places in Europe to see humpback whales, along with minke whales, porpoises and sometimes blue whales, and tours run from spring to autumn, sailing out on old oak fishing boats. Many boats provide warm overalls, and you will be glad of them.

  2. 2. Asbyrgi

    Ásbyrgi is a horseshoe-shaped canyon in the north-east of Iceland, about three and a half kilometres long and a kilometre wide, with vertical cliffs of around a hundred metres on three sides. The old story says it was made when Sleipnir, the eight-legged horse of Odin, put one hoof down on the earth, and from the air the shape does look like an enormous print. The scientific explanation is just as dramatic: catastrophic floods from the glacial river Jökulsá á Fjöllum carved it out in the aftermath of eruptions beneath the ice, in at least two huge episodes long before anyone lived here.

  3. 3. Dettifoss

    Dettifoss is not the highest waterfall in Iceland, but it is the most powerful in Europe by volume, and standing next to it you quickly believe the claim. The glacial river Jökulsá á Fjöllum, grey with silt, tips over a rim about a hundred metres wide and drops some forty-five metres into a canyon, sending up a cloud of spray that hangs over the plateau and soaks anyone who gets too close. The noise is a constant roar, and the ground trembles slightly under your boots.

  4. 4. Myvatn

    Mývatn means midge lake, and the name is honest, because in early summer clouds of small, harmless flies hang above the water and rise from the shore in a shimmering haze. They are the foundation of an ecosystem that feeds one of the richest populations of breeding ducks in Europe, and in June the lake is crowded with them. Bring a face cover in still weather, and keep your visor closed.

  5. 5. Godafoss

    Goðafoss is the waterfall of the gods, and its name comes from a story. In the year 1000, when the Icelandic parliament decided that the country should adopt Christianity, the lawspeaker Þorgeir Ljósvetningagoði is said to have thrown his statues of the old Norse gods into the falls on his way home, and the name has stuck ever since. Whether or not it happened exactly that way, the story fits the place, where the river Skjálfandafljót drops in a wide, curving horseshoe over a basalt ledge, about twelve metres high and thirty metres across.

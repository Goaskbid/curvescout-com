  1. 1. San Cristóbal de las Casas

    San Cristóbal de las Casas sits at about 2,200 metres in a bowl of pine and oak forest in the Chiapas highlands, and the altitude shapes everything. The mornings are cold, the afternoons bright and the evenings smoky with wood fires, and the old streets, cobbled and low, are lined with houses painted in ochre and red. Diego de Mazariegos founded the town in 1528, and it was later renamed for Bartolomé de las Casas, the Dominican bishop who spoke up for the Indigenous population. On New Year's Day 1994 the Zapatista rebels briefly took over the town hall, and the uprising still shapes the politics of the region.

  2. 2. Ocosingo

    Toniná is the reason to linger in Ocosingo. A few kilometres east of town, the ruins of this Maya city rise up a hillside in a stack of terraces, platforms and temples that climbs some seventy metres from the valley floor, one of the tallest built structures in the Maya world. In the seventh and eighth centuries Toniná was a fierce military power, and its carvings show bound captives, including, according to its inscriptions, a king of Palenque. It is far less visited than Palenque, and you may have it almost to yourself.

  3. 3. Palenque

    Howler monkeys start the day in Palenque. Their roars echo through the jungle before dawn, and if you reach the ruins when the gates open, the mist still clings to the trees and the temples emerge slowly from the green. This Maya city, which flourished between the fifth and eighth centuries, stands on a shelf of hills above the plains of Tabasco, backed by dense rainforest, and it is one of the most beautiful archaeological sites in the Americas.

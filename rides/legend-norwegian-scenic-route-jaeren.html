  1. 1. Near Bryne

    Jæren is the flattest piece of Norway's west coast, and on the shore near Bore it feels almost like another country. A long beach of pale sand runs south behind low dunes and marram grass, the North Sea rolls in on broad lines of surf, and inland the land is a patchwork of green fields divided by grey stone walls. There are no fjords here, no mountains close to the sea, just a wide sky and the constant presence of wind.

  2. 2. Near Bryne

    Orre is little more than an old church, a lake and a beach, but on the Jæren coast that is plenty. The church, a medieval stone building, stands on a strip of land between the freshwater lake Orrevatnet and the dunes of Orrestranden, one of the longest beaches in Norway, and the setting is a lesson in how thin the line between land and sea can be here. Storms have shifted the dunes many times over the centuries, and the church has stood its ground.

  3. 3. Near Vigrestad

    Kvassheim lighthouse stands on a low, stony point on the southern Jæren coast, a short white tower attached to a keeper's house, looking out over a sea that has wrecked more ships than anyone could count. This coast has no natural harbours and no shelter, just a long, flat shore of boulders and sand, and before the lights were built, ships driven onto it in storms had little chance. The lighthouse was taken out of service in the late twentieth century and is now a visitor centre and cafe for the protected coastal landscape around it.

  4. 4. Egersund

    Egersund is a harbour town on the south-west coast of Norway, with about nine and a half thousand people, a working fishing fleet and a layout of streets and quays that is easy to like. The old centre is a grid of white wooden houses, mostly low and neat, along a main street named Strandgata, and the harbour is lined with boats, cranes and fish processing plants. The town grew rich on herring and later on a famous faience factory, which produced fine ceramics from the 1840s until the late twentieth century, and a museum in town shows examples. Egersund pottery is still collected and sold in antique shops.

  5. 5. Flekkefjord

    Flekkefjord is a town on the south coast of Norway, built around a narrow tidal inlet, and it is known as Little Holland, thanks to the old Dutch timber trade. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Dutch merchants bought timber here, and the old quarter, Hollenderbyen, is a district of white wooden houses with narrow lanes and small gardens, built by wealthy merchants and sea captains. The town has about five and a half thousand people, with a harbour, a bridge, a few shops and a pleasant waterfront. Fuel and supplies are easy, and Liknes and Moi are small towns nearby.

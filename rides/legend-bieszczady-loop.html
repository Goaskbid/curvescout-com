  1. 1. Sanok

    Sanok is the last proper town before the Bieszczady, the wild, empty corner of south-eastern Poland where the country runs into Ukraine and Slovakia. About forty thousand people live on the banks of the San River, in a valley of wooded hills, and the town has a calm, dignified feel, with a castle on a rise, an old market square and a river that is wide and clear in summer.

  2. 2. Lesko

    Lesko is the quiet gatekeeper of the Bieszczady, a small town of around five and a half thousand on the River San, where the flat farmland of the Carpathian foothills starts to crumple into forested ridges. Its market square is wonderfully unhurried, with an old town hall in the middle, and the town carries a deeper past than its size suggests: before the Second World War a large share of the population was Jewish, and the stone synagogue from the seventeenth century survives, now a gallery, while a Jewish cemetery on a hillside above the town still draws visitors. The castle, rebuilt after fires, belonged for a long time to powerful noble families.

  4. 4. Cisna

    Cisna lies in the far south-east of Poland, where the Carpathians push up against the borders of Slovakia and Ukraine, and the land turns wild and empty. This is the Bieszczady, a range of rounded, grassy ridges called połoniny rising above dense beech forest, and it is among the least populated parts of the country. Cisna itself is a hamlet of a few hundred people, with a scattering of wooden guesthouses, a handful of cafes and one famous bar. The nearest large town is Snina, across the Slovak border, and on the Polish side, Lesko and Ustrzyki Dolne are the places to stock up.

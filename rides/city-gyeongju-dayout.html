Why this ride

A 385 km round trip from Gyeongju - roughly 6 h 38 in the saddle. The stops in between: Tongdosa Temple, Gyo-dong and Songhyeon-dong Tumuli, Gaya Tumuli and Daeseong-dong Tumuli. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Gyeongju

    For almost a thousand years, from 57 BC to AD 935 by the traditional count, Gyeongju was the capital of the Silla kingdom, and the city still behaves like a place that remembers it. The old centre is flat and green and quiet, with the great grass-covered burial mounds of Silla kings and queens rising among the houses and shops like a row of soft hills. Nothing in the city is allowed to climb too high, which keeps the skyline low and lets the mountains behind it stay visible.

  2. 2. Tongdosa Temple

    Most Korean Buddhist temples have a statue of the Buddha in their main hall. Tongdosa does not. Instead, a window behind the altar looks out onto a stone stupa that is said to contain relics of the historical Buddha, brought back from China by the monk Jajang, who founded the temple in 646. That makes Tongdosa one of the Three Jewel Temples of Korea, representing the Buddha himself, alongside Haeinsa, representing the teaching, and Songgwangsa, the community of monks. It is part of the UNESCO-listed Sansa, Buddhist Mountain Monasteries in Korea, inscribed in 2018.

  3. 3. Gyo-dong and Songhyeon-dong Tumuli

    The rounded grassy mounds rising behind Changnyeong look almost like natural hills until you notice how regular they are. These are the Gyo-dong and Songhyeon-dong tumuli, burial mounds of the rulers of Bihwa, one of the small kingdoms of the Gaya confederacy that flourished in the south of the Korean peninsula between roughly the first and sixth centuries before being absorbed by Silla. In 2023 they became part of a UNESCO listing that brings together seven Gaya tomb clusters, and they are among the most atmospheric of the group.

  4. 4. Gaya Tumuli

    Right in the middle of Gimhae, a busy city just west of Busan, a low ridge of grassy mounds rises between apartment blocks and roads. These are the Daeseong-dong tumuli, the burial ground of the rulers of Geumgwan Gaya, one of the small kingdoms of the Gaya confederacy that flourished in the south of the Korean peninsula from the first centuries AD until it was absorbed by Silla in the sixth century. In 2023 a group of Gaya burial sites, including these, was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list.

  5. 5. Daeseong-dong Tumuli

    The tumuli of Daeseong-dong lie on a low hill above the Gimhae plain, close to the Nakdong river delta in the south-east of Korea. They are the royal cemetery of Geumgwan Gaya, one of the small kingdoms of the Gaya confederacy, which flourished alongside the better-known Three Kingdoms of the Korean peninsula, and for centuries after it was swallowed up by its neighbour Silla in 532, it was largely forgotten. Archaeology has changed that. Excavations show a wealthy society, strong on iron, which was exported to Japan and to the Chinese colonies to the north, and the burials contain armour, horse gear, pottery and iron ingots that served almost as currency. UNESCO listed the Gaya Tumuli in 2023.

Roadbook

  1. Sakhyurta start

    Sakhyurta is better known to most travellers by the initials MRS, for the small fish-processing plant that once stood here. It is the jumping-off point for Olkhon, the largest island on Lake Baikal, and on a summer day there can be a long queue of cars waiting by the shore for the ferry across the narrow strait. The settlement itself is a handful of houses, guesthouses and cafes strung along a bay of the Maloye More, the Little Sea, the stretch of water between the island and the western shore of the lake.

    The landscape here is quite unlike the forested shores further south. This is the Tazheran steppe, a dry, open country of grass, bare hills and rocky outcrops, with patches of larch on the slopes and wide views across the water to the island. It is Buryat land, and you will see shamanic shrines, with poles wrapped in coloured ribbons, at passes and sacred places along the road. The light is clear and sharp, the sky enormous, and the wind can be ferocious. The sarma, a violent wind that pours out of a valley to the north, can whip the lake into dangerous waves in minutes.

    As the start of a ride onto Olkhon, Sakhyurta sits at the end of a long road from Irkutsk, about two hundred and fifty kilometres through forest and then steppe. The final section winds over low hills, with long views of the lake ahead. The surface on the main road is paved, but watch for potholes and gravel on the edges. On the island itself, roads are mostly unpaved, sandy and rutted, with washboard surfaces that shake everything loose. Tyre pressures and a steady pace matter.

    There are cafes, small shops and guesthouses at Sakhyurta, but fuel is limited. Yelantsy, about twenty-six kilometres back toward Irkutsk, has a station, and the smart move is to fill up there and carry extra, because fuel on Olkhon is not always available. The ferry is the next step, and the wait can be long in peak season. Use it to watch the water, the gulls and the island rising across the strait, and to feel the edge of the steppe meeting the lake.

  2. Olkhon Ferry stop

    The crossing to Olkhon takes only a quarter of an hour or so, but the wait can stretch to several hours on a July weekend, when cars, minibuses and motorcycles queue along the road down to the landing. The strait here, known as the Olkhon Gate, is narrow, a couple of kilometres across, but deep and sometimes rough, and the small ferries that shuttle back and forth are a lifeline for the island. In winter, when the lake freezes, an ice road replaces them, and in the uncertain weeks of freeze-up and thaw, hovercraft take over.

    Standing on the deck, you get one of the best views on the lake. To the north stretches the Maloye More, the Little Sea, between the island and the mainland, with bare hills and steppe on both sides. To the south, the main body of Baikal opens up, vast and blue, with mountains on the far shore. The island ahead looks dry and brown, with rocky headlands and patches of forest, and the air smells of water and wild thyme. Gulls follow the ferry, and on calm days the water is so clear you can see deep into it.

    The ride to the ferry comes after a long road from Irkutsk through forest and the Tazheran steppe, the final stretch descending to the shore with views of the strait. On the island side, the road to Khuzhir, the main village, is unpaved, sandy and rutted, with long washboard sections that are tough on bikes and riders. Lower tyre pressures, a steady speed and plenty of patience help. Dust can be a serious problem in dry weather, so keep a distance from the vehicle ahead.

    Services around the ferry landing are limited to cafes and small shops, with fuel back in Yelantsy, about thirty kilometres away. On the island, fuel can be scarce, so arrive with a full tank and extra if you plan to explore. Check the ferry schedule before setting out, especially in shoulder seasons, when crossings can be cancelled due to wind or ice. Then sit back, enjoy the crossing, and watch Olkhon come closer; there is something special about arriving on an island by water.

  3. Khuzhir stop

    Khuzhir is the one real village on Olkhon, the biggest island in Lake Baikal, a long, wind-scoured strip of steppe and pine forest about seventy kilometres long, lying close to the western shore. Baikal is the deepest lake on Earth, over sixteen hundred metres at its greatest depth, and holds about a fifth of the world's unfrozen fresh water. It is also very old, perhaps twenty-five million years, and its waters are astonishingly clear, so that you can see stones many metres down in summer.

    The island is the centre of the Buryat shamanic tradition, and the rocky headland just outside Khuzhir, Shaman Rock, is a sacred place with colourful ribbons tied on posts. Sunsets here, with the light turning the water gold and the cliffs red, draw crowds in summer. Seals, the Baikal nerpa, live in the lake, and eagles and gulls ride the wind. The island's roads are mostly sandy and unpaved, and the main way of getting around is by old Soviet vans, which bounce over the ruts in clouds of dust.

    Getting here is part of the trip. A ferry runs across the narrow strait, the Olkhon Gate, from the mainland near Sakhyurta, and queues for it can be long in summer, when everyone wants to cross at once. Ferries sometimes stop when ice is forming or breaking, so check the schedule. For riders, the riding on the island is a test, with soft sand, corrugations and washouts, and heavy bikes can be tricky. A trail leads north to Cape Khoboy, the island's northern tip, with big views over open water.

    Khuzhir has just over a thousand people, with guesthouses, cafes and small shops, and fish from the lake, especially smoked omul, is the local treat. Fuel is sold in the village, but supplies can be limited, so fill up on the mainland before crossing. Summer is short and bright, with cool nights, and in winter the lake freezes so thickly that cars drive across it. Foreign riders should check current conditions and rules before planning travel.

  4. Cape Burkhan stop

    Shaman Rock rises from the water at the edge of Khuzhir like a pair of pale marble towers joined at the base, and for the Buryats it is one of the most sacred places in Asia. They call the cape Burkhan, after a deity said to live in a cave inside the rock, and for centuries shamans held rituals here, while ordinary people approached with caution, or not at all. Today the headland is crowded with visitors in summer, but the poles wrapped in coloured ribbons, the scattered offerings of coins and the sense of a place that matters to people are all still there.

    The rock is made of marble and crystalline limestone, streaked with lichen in shades of orange and green, and it stands at the end of a short peninsula that juts into the Maloye More. The cave runs through it, and the views from the cape take in the water, the bare hills of the mainland across the strait, and the long sweep of sandy beach curving away to the north. At sunset the rock glows warm and golden, and the lake turns silver, which is when most photographers gather. In winter, the ice around the rock forms extraordinary shapes and icicles.

    Khuzhir, the main village on Olkhon, is a dusty, sprawling place of wooden houses, guesthouses and cafes, with unpaved streets that turn to sand in summer and mud after rain. The ride from the ferry follows rough, sandy tracks across the island, with washboard sections and soft patches that demand care. Around Khuzhir, tracks lead north along the island toward Cape Khoboy at its tip, a rough, rewarding ride through steppe and forest with views of the lake on both sides.

    Khuzhir has shops, cafes and plenty of guesthouses, but fuel can be hard to find on the island, so arrive with a full tank and spare. Try buuzy, the steamed meat dumplings of the Buryats, or smoked omul from the lake. Treat the rock with respect, as locals ask: do not climb it, and keep your voice down. Then find a spot on the cliffs nearby, watch the light fade over the water, and feel the quiet power of this place.

  5. Cape Khoboy finish

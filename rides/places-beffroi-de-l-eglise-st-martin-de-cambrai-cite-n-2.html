Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Beffroi de l’église St-Martin de Cambrai

    Cambrai's belfry is the tower of the church of Saint-Martin, a stout, plain-faced tower that rises over the roofs of a town of about thirty-six thousand people in the flat, open Cambrésis. Belfries in northern France and Belgium were symbols of civic freedom, built by town councils to hold the bells and the charters, and a group of them is on the World Heritage list. This one has a more modest charm than the giants of Bruges or Ghent, but it still announces the town from a long way off, across fields of sugar beet and wheat.

  2. 2. Cités du Champ fleuri et du Garage

    Coal made this corner of northern France, and coal left it with a landscape unlike anywhere else in the country: slag heaps rising like black pyramids from the fields, pithead frames standing over old shafts and long rows of brick miners' houses lining the streets of every town. The Nord-Pas de Calais Mining Basin was listed by UNESCO in 2012, and the Cités du Champ Fleuri et du Garage, near Masny and Lewarde, are among its typical housing estates, built by mining companies to house workers and their families close to the pits.

  3. 3. Cité Foch

    Row upon row of identical brick houses, each with its small garden, outbuilding and front door painted in the colour of the mining company: that is a cité minière, and Cité Foch, between Hénin-Beaumont and Montigny-en-Gohelle, is one of the many hundreds that the coal companies built across northern France. They were designed to house miners and their families close to the pits, and they came in different forms over the decades, from the long terraces of the nineteenth century, the corons, to the garden cities of the early twentieth century, with semi-detached houses, wide streets and green spaces. Many of these neighbourhoods are included in the UNESCO listing of the mining basin.

  4. 4. Cité n°2

    Mazingarbe is a small town on the northern edge of the mining basin, with the terraced rows of a miners' cité running down streets of red brick, and nearly twelve thousand people live in neighbouring Bully-les-Mines. The Cité Foch and the Cité Bellevue are two of the estates that mark this stretch of the basin on the map. The cité, built by the coal company for its workers, usually came with a school, a church, a company shop and a community hall, so that the pit shaped a whole life.

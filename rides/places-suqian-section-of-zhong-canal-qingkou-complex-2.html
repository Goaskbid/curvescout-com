Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Suqian Section of Zhong Canal

    The Zhong Canal, the Middle Canal, was dug in the late seventeenth century for a very practical reason: the Yellow River kept getting in the way. Boats on the Grand Canal had to cross that unruly river in northern Jiangsu, and the crossing was dangerous and often blocked by floods and silt. Engineers of the Qing dynasty cut a new channel that avoided the worst of it, and the Suqian section is a reminder of that effort. It belongs to the Grand Canal's UNESCO listing, and the canal here is a broad, working waterway with barges, embankments and farmland on either side.

  2. 2. Si County Section of Tongji Canal

    The Grand Canal of China is the longest artificial waterway in the world, a chain of channels linking Beijing in the north with Hangzhou in the south, and the section through Si County in northern Anhui is part of its oldest and most important layer. The Tongji Canal, built in the early seventh century under the Sui dynasty, linked the Yellow River with the Huai and eventually the Yangtze, and made it possible to move grain and troops from the rich south to the capital. Hundreds of thousands of labourers are said to have dug it, and the project was so costly that it contributed to the collapse of the dynasty.

  3. 3. Qingkou Complex

    Qingkou is the place where China's engineers tried, for centuries, to make two very different rivers behave. Here, near Huai'an in northern Jiangsu, the Grand Canal met the Huai River, and from the twelfth century until the middle of the nineteenth, the Yellow River also took over the Huai's lower course, bringing enormous loads of silt. The canal crossed this unstable junction, and getting boats safely across it, while controlling floods and keeping the water level right, was among the hardest tasks in the entire waterway.

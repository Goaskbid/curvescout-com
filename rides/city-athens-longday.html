Why this ride

From Athens it runs 280 km to Athens; allow about 4 h 50 of riding. The stops in between: Sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus, Archaeological Sites of Mycenae and Tiryns, Archaeological Site of Mycenae and back through Archaeological Sites of Mycenae and Tiryns. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Athens

    Athens has more motorbikes per street than almost any city in Europe, and the drivers are used to them. Scooters thread through every gap, couriers weave between buses, and the whole basin between Mount Hymettus and the sea hums at a pitch that a visitor from the north needs a day to tune into. At the centre of the din the Acropolis stands on its limestone rock, the Parthenon's marble glowing gold at sunset, and it makes the whole mess of concrete around it seem like a footnote.

  2. 2. Sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus

    Drop a coin in the centre of the orchestra at Epidaurus and people sitting in the top row, fifty-odd rows above, will hear it. The theatre of the sanctuary of Asklepios, built in the fourth century BC, is famous for its acoustics and its almost perfect state of preservation, and it still hosts performances of ancient drama during the summer festival. But the theatre was only part of the sanctuary. Epidaurus was the most famous healing centre of the ancient Greek world, where the sick came to sleep in the sacred buildings and hope that the god Asklepios would visit them in a dream with a cure.

  4. 4. Archaeological Sites of Mycenae and Tiryns

    The plain of Argos is a wide, flat basin of orange groves and olive trees tucked into the Peloponnese, and on its edge, a few kilometres apart, stand the two strongholds that gave the Greek Bronze Age its name. Mycenae sits on a rocky spur between two ravines, with Argos eleven kilometres to the south-west and the sea beyond, and Tiryns lies lower down near the shore, on a low rocky mound that once stood by the water.

  5. 5. Archaeological Site of Mycenae

    The Lion Gate is the thing everyone remembers. Two stone lionesses, facing each other across a central column, stand over the entrance to the citadel, carved some three and a half thousand years ago in the Bronze Age, and the walls beside the gate are built of boulders so huge that later Greeks thought only the Cyclopes could have lifted them. Inside is a royal burial ground, the Grave Circles, where in 1876 the German archaeologist Heinrich Schliemann found gold masks and weapons, and declared that he had looked upon the face of Agamemnon. The mask is older than Agamemnon would have been, but the name stuck.

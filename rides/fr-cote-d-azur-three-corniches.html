Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Nice

    Nice rises from a wide bay of pebbles and blue water, with a ridge of hills behind it, and it has been a seaside resort since the English began wintering here in the eighteenth century. The Promenade des Anglais, the seafront boulevard they paid for, still runs for several kilometres along the shore, lined with palms, pale hotels and deckchairs, and the old town, Vieux Nice, behind the Cours Saleya flower market, is a tangle of ochre and pink houses with laundry hanging out of windows. Castle Hill, a wooded bluff at the eastern end of the bay, gives a view over the whole sweep of the coast. Italian influence is strong, as the city only became French in 1860, and the food shows it. Try socca, a chickpea pancake, a pan bagnat sandwich or a proper salade niçoise.

  2. 2. Èze

    Èze is a village built like an eagle's nest, perched on a sharp rock about four hundred and twenty metres above the Mediterranean, with the sea spread out below on three sides. A tangle of stone lanes, stepped passages and vaulted alleys climbs the rock to the summit, where the ruins of a medieval castle have been turned into an exotic garden full of cacti and succulents, with views that stretch along the coast from Cap Ferrat to the mountains of Italy on a clear day.

  3. 3. La Turbie

    Look up at La Turbie and you see a ruin standing over Monaco like a broken tooth. It is the Trophée des Alpes, a huge monument built by the Roman emperor Augustus in 6 BC to celebrate the conquest of the Alpine tribes, and it stands at the highest point of the Via Julia Augusta, the Roman road that connected Italy with Gaul. Once crowned with a statue of the emperor and inscribed with the names of the defeated peoples, the monument was partly dismantled and later used as a fortress and quarry, and what you see today was largely reconstructed in the twentieth century with funds from an American donor.

  4. 4. Menton

    Menton is the last town in France before Italy, the finish of the Route des Grandes Alpes, and after seven hundred kilometres of mountains it feels like being tipped into a warm bath. The old town tumbles down a hill to the harbour in tones of ochre, apricot and cream, topped by the baroque Basilica of Saint Michael with its glowing facade. The climate is the mildest on the French Riviera, sheltered by the mountains behind, and the local lemons are famous, celebrated each February in the Fête du Citron, when huge sculptures made of fruit and flowers fill the gardens and people parade through the streets.

Why this ride

A 67 km round trip from Sakai - roughly 1 h 09 in the saddle. It goes by way of Higashiumazuka Kofun, Horyu-ji Area and back through Higashiumazuka Kofun. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Shichikannon Kofun

    Haniwa are the clue to this place. These clay cylinders and figures, shaped as houses, horses, warriors and dancers, were set around the slopes of the great Japanese tombs to guard the dead, and replicas of them now stand in parks and museums in Sakai. Shichikannon is a mound from that world, one of the numerous burial sites in the city that became a World Heritage property in 2019, and as with its neighbours you appreciate it from outside the moat. The lawn, the trees and the water do most of the talking.

  2. 2. Higashiumazuka Kofun

    The Yamato basin, in the middle of Nara prefecture, is where Japan's early rulers raised their great earthen tombs, and Kashihara sits at the heart of it. Around the third and fourth centuries and for several hundred years afterward, the leaders of the Yamato court were buried under mounds, many in a distinctive keyhole shape, a round tomb joined to a trapezoidal front. These are the kofun, which simply means old mound. They were ringed with clay cylinders called haniwa, some formed into the shapes of houses, horses, warriors and dancers, and many were surrounded by moats.

  3. 3. Horyu-ji Area

    The buildings at Hōryū-ji are around thirteen hundred years old, and some of them are among the oldest wooden structures anywhere in the world. Founded in the early seventh century by Prince Shōtoku, a regent remembered as a champion of Buddhism and a founding figure of the Japanese state, the temple was rebuilt after a fire later in the same century, and parts of that rebuilding still stand. In 1993 it became one of the first sites in Japan to be listed by UNESCO, as the Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Alba Iulia - Apulum / Cetate

    Apulum was a metropolis on the Mureș, and today it is mostly under Alba Iulia's streets and fields. In Roman Dacia, which was conquered by Trajan in 106 AD, the legion XIII Gemina was stationed here, and around the camp grew two towns, one for the soldiers' families and traders and a second, grander one that gained the rank of colony. Apulum was the wealthiest city of the province, and probably its capital for a time, thanks in part to the gold mines of the nearby Apuseni Mountains.

  2. 2. Village of Câlnic

    Câlnic, a small village south of Sebeș in Alba county, is home to one of the strangest fortresses among the Saxon sites of Transylvania. Unlike most of its neighbours, it began not as a fortified church but as a noble residence. In the thirteenth century a local count built a stone keep here, surrounded by walls and a moat. Later the villagers bought it, added a chapel and storerooms, and turned it into a peasant refuge, where families could shelter from raids behind the walls. The result, still ringed by water on parts of its perimeter, is part of the Villages with Fortified Churches in Transylvania World Heritage site.

  3. 3. Pianu de Jos - Muncelu-Lăutaorea

    High above the Sebeș and Cugir valleys, where the Șureanu foothills fold into long grassy shoulders, the Romans kept watch over the southern approaches to their province of Dacia. The name on the map, Muncelu-Lăutaorea, belongs to the ridge country above Pianu de Jos, and it is one of the many small frontier points that archaeologists have tied to the Roman defensive system here. Do not expect walls to lean on. What survives on these hilltops is usually a faint platform, a ditch you notice only when the light is low, and a view that explains everything about why a sentry was posted there.

Why this ride

A one-way ride of 96 km, Dmitrov to Dubna - roughly 1 h 51 in the saddle. The line favours views over speed.

Roadbook

  1. Dmitrov start

    Dmitrov is an old town north of Moscow, set above the Yakhroma River where the Moscow Canal begins its run to the Volga. It was founded in 1154 by Prince Yuri Dolgoruky, the same prince credited with founding Moscow, and named for his son Dmitry, and its earthen ramparts, the remains of a medieval fortress called the Kremlin, still encircle a hill in the middle of town. Inside stands the white Assumption Cathedral, a sixteenth-century church with a wide dome, a calm landmark above the lower streets.

    The canal is the other half of the story. The Moscow-Volga Canal was dug in the 1930s, largely by prisoners of the Gulag, with Dmitrov as the headquarters of the building administration, and it opened in 1937. The waterway runs north from here past locks, pumping stations and monumental stone portals, and its grim history sits oddly with the calm water and pleasure boats of today. The anarchist thinker Peter Kropotkin lived out his last years in Dmitrov, and his house is now a small museum, a quiet footnote to the town's story.

    For riders, Dmitrov is a gateway to the quiet country north of the capital. The roads east and west of the canal pass through low, rolling farmland, birch woods and small villages with wooden houses, and the Klin-Dmitrov Ridge, the highest ground in the Moscow region, rises to the west. The surface in the district varies, with good main roads and rougher secondary ones, and potholes after the winter thaw can be deep. The countryside is gentle, and the pace is slow, with plenty of stops for a roadside samovar or a cup of tea.

    Dmitrov is a town of sixty thousand, so fuel, food and shops are plentiful, and the road to Moscow is fast. Yakhroma, a few kilometers north, has a large textile factory and a canal viewing point. The best time is late spring, when the birches are in leaf, and September, when the woods turn gold, but July can be hot and the roads busy with city dwellers heading for dachas. Winter brings deep snow and hard frost, and the roads, though plowed, are slick and cold, so it is a season for the brave.

  2. Yakhroma scenic

    Yakhroma sits on a canal, and the canal is what matters. The Moscow Canal, which links the capital to the Volga, was dug in the 1930s by tens of thousands of workers, many of them prisoners, and when it opened in 1937 it brought the river to the city and gave Moscow the water supply and the port it had lacked. The canal passes through Yakhroma, and the locks and the pumping stations that serve it were built in a grand style, with towers and ornamental gates, so that even the industrial architecture has a ceremonial feel.

    The little river that gave the town its name runs through the old centre, and the town grew up around textile mills that processed cotton and linen. Today it is a modest place of about twelve thousand people, with a mix of Soviet apartment blocks, old brick factory buildings and wooden houses with carved window frames. The waterfront by the lock is a pleasant place to sit and watch the barges. In summer, people come here to swim and fish, and anglers sit along the banks of the canal at all hours.

    Nearby Dmitrov, seven kilometres away, is much older, and has a better story. It was founded in 1154 by Yuri Dolgoruky, the prince credited with founding Moscow, and the earthen ramparts of its kremlin still encircle the cathedral at the centre of town. The anarchist thinker Peter Kropotkin spent his last years here, and died in the town in 1921. The roads between the two towns run through flat, farmed country with birch woods and wide fields, and the road is usually well kept along the main route, with narrower lanes in the villages.

    For a rider, this is gentle country, a place for a relaxed ride rather than an exciting one. The road along the canal has a quiet charm, with long, straight stretches broken by bridges and locks, and in the early morning, the mist rising off the water is lovely. Traffic is heavier on the approaches to Dmitrov and on weekends, when Muscovites head for their dachas. Fuel is available in Yakhroma itself and in Dmitrov, which has everything else you could need, including a good choice of cafes. Autumn and early spring are the best times, when the birches are bare or turning and the canal is calm.

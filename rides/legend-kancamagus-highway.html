Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Near Woodstock

    The Kancamagus Highway crosses the White Mountains of New Hampshire for some fifty-five kilometres between Lincoln and Conway, and for all that distance there is not a single gas station, restaurant or shop. That is what makes it special: one of the most beautiful roads in New England, through unbroken forest, along rivers and over a mountain pass, with nothing commercial to interrupt it. This stop lies near its western end, close to Lincoln and North Woodstock, where the road leaves the valley and begins to climb.

  2. 2. Kancamagus Highway point 2

    Midway along the Kancamagus, the road has settled into its stride. The forest is thick on both sides, hardwood and spruce running up steep slopes, and the Hancock Branch of the Pemigewasset runs close alongside, clear and brown, running over granite boulders. The valley feels deeper and more enclosed here, with cliffs, ledges and the high ridges of the Sandwich Range somewhere out of sight to the south. In places, the trees close over the road, and the light turns green.

  3. 3. Kancamagus Highway point 3

    The high part of the Kancamagus is the part that makes you glad you came. Near the middle of the route, the road crests the Kancamagus Pass, at around 870 metres, the highest point on the highway, and the Swift River begins its run toward the east. The road here is a long climb and a long descent, with fine views over rolling forested ridges that go on forever, a big, quiet landscape of the sort that New England does particularly well.

  4. 4. Conway

    Conway is the front door to the Kancamagus, and the road starts quietly, with a few motels and a gas station, and then plunges into the White Mountain National Forest. The Kancamagus Highway, New Hampshire Route 112, runs about thirty-four miles west to Lincoln, following the Swift River up a narrow valley and then climbing over a pass at nearly 2,900 feet, with forest on both sides and almost no buildings. The name comes from Kancamagus, a sachem of the Pennacook people in the late seventeenth century, who tried to keep the peace with English settlers before leaving the region.

Why this ride

From Chitose it runs 363 km to Chitose; allow about 6 h 16 of riding. On the way it passes Kitakogane Site, Ofune Site and back through Kitakogane Site. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Kiusu Earthwork Burial Circles

    Kiusu sits within the city limits of Chitose, but you would never guess it from the gentle forest and the soft ground underfoot. This is one of the strangest ancient monuments in Japan: circular graves enclosed by a raised embankment of earth, made by Jomon people about three thousand years ago. They dug a great ring pit, piled the soil into a bank around it, and placed their dead inside. The largest circles are many metres across, and the earthworks are still easy to read as mounds, grassy and a bit like a stage set. It forms part of the northern Jomon listing, which UNESCO inscribed in 2021. The reason for such labour is still discussed, but the effort tells you the dead mattered to the group.

  2. 2. Kitakogane Site

    Hokkaido does not usually come to mind when people talk about ancient Japan, yet the shell heaps around Uchiura Bay are some of the oldest signs of settled life in the archipelago. Kitakogane sits on the edge of the bay near Date, roughly halfway between Muroran and the volcanic country around Lake Toya. The Jomon people who lived here gathered shellfish, hunted deer and fished, and they threw the shells onto the same pile for centuries, which is why a modern visitor can read a kitchen midden like a diary. It belongs to the group of northern Jomon sites that UNESCO listed in 2021.

  3. 3. Ofune Site

    Ofune is a Jomon settlement on the southern tip of Hokkaido, in the hills above Hakodate, and it is a lot bigger than the signboards make it look. The Jomon were hunter-gatherers who lived in the northern forests of Japan for something like ten thousand years, long before rice farming, and they built a settled life around chestnuts, fish, deer and the odd well-travelled trade good. Ofune was inhabited for centuries and is now part of the Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan, which UNESCO recognised in 2021. Reconstructed pit houses hold the grass in place, and the scale of the pits tells you someone intended to stay.

Why this ride

A 264 km round trip from Ḩūsān - roughly 4 h 33 in the saddle. The stops in between: Masada, Tel Beer Sheba and back through Masada. It crosses from Palestinian Territories into Israel - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Area 2

    Battir is a village of terraces. On the slopes of the hills south-west of Jerusalem, a system of stone-walled terraces and irrigation channels has been watered for centuries by a spring that is shared out in turn among the village families, according to a rota that locals still keep. The terraces grow vegetables, olives, and aubergines for which Battir is known, and the system, with its Roman-era origins and later improvements, was listed by UNESCO in 2014 as a cultural landscape. The little railway line from Jaffa to Jerusalem, built by the Ottomans in the 1890s, runs along the valley floor below the village.

  2. 2. Masada

    The Dead Sea lies more than four hundred metres below sea level, the lowest point on land, and Masada rises above its western shore as a vast flat-topped rock with sheer cliffs on every side. King Herod built a palace fortress here in the first century BC, with storerooms, cisterns, bathhouses and a three-tiered palace hanging from the northern cliff. After the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem in AD 70, a group of Jewish rebels held out on the rock against the Roman army. The Romans built a siege ramp up the western side, and according to the historian Josephus, when the walls were breached, the defenders chose to die rather than be captured.

  3. 3. Tel Beer Sheba

    Tel Beer Sheba is a mound on the edge of the modern city of Beersheba, in the northern Negev desert of southern Israel, and it holds the remains of an Iron Age town that has been excavated and partially reconstructed. With Megiddo and Hazor, it forms the UNESCO site of the Biblical Tels, recognized for their importance in understanding the cities of the biblical era. The name Beersheba appears many times in the Bible, associated with Abraham and the wells he dug in the desert.

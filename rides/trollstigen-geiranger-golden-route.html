Why this ride

Trollstigen climbs a near-vertical wall with the Stigfossen falls spilling across it, and the viewing platform at the top looks straight back down the stack. Link it with the Geiranger ferry and Ørnesvingen and you have the most photographed day of riding in Norway. Book nothing tightly - the ferries set the pace.

Scenic road chapters

Trollstigen

A steady rhythm suits this better than bursts. The height arrives whether you hurry or not. The ground tilts up and stays tilted for 17 km. 848 m of it, taken steadily rather than in one wall.

Geiranger-Trollstigen Norwegian Scenic Route

The valley floor is behind you and the road starts working for its height at about 26.1%. Corners stack above one another where the ground allows nothing straighter. First and second gear more often than not. Look through each corner to the next one and let the engine do the holding.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Åndalsnes

    Åndalsnes is a small town at the head of the Romsdalsfjord, hemmed in by some of the most vertical mountains in Norway. The Romsdalshorn, a sharp pyramid of rock, rises above the valley to the south, and the Trollveggen, Europe's tallest vertical rock face at around a thousand metres, stands a short way up the valley. The town itself is modest, with a few thousand people, a railway terminus, a harbour and a handful of cafes, and its main business is welcoming people who come for the mountains.

  2. 2. Trollstigen viewpoint

    The Trollstigen Plateau is a bare, glacier-scoured saddle at 858 metres, ringed by peaks with names like Bispen, the Bishop, and Kongen, the King, and the viewing platform here, a sculptural structure of steel and concrete that hangs over the drop, is the best place to understand what the road does. Below, the Trollstigen, the Troll's Ladder, zigzags down the side of the valley in eleven hairpins, with the long white thread of the Stigfossen waterfall falling beside it, and beyond lies the broad green Isterdalen and the far peaks of Romsdalen.

  3. 3. Valldal

    Valldal is a small town at the head of the Norddalsfjord, in the green and orderly farmland that lies at the foot of the great Sunnmøre mountains, and it is the first proper settlement you reach after the long descent from the Trollstigen. Valldal is famous in Norway for its strawberries, which ripen in July in the long summer light and are sold in roadside stands, and the local people take a great deal of pride in them.

  4. 4. Geiranger

    The Geirangerfjord is a narrow cut, around fifteen kilometres long, between cliffs that rise over a thousand metres from the water, and it is among the best-known sights in Norway. The village at its head, Geiranger, is tiny, with only a few hundred residents, but in summer, cruise ships anchor in the fjord one after another and the pier fills with people. UNESCO listed the fjord in 2005, along with Naeroyfjord, and a short walk along the shore brings you to the base of the Seven Sisters waterfall, which pours straight into the fjord from a high ledge.

  5. 5. Ørnesvingen Eagle Road

    There is a bend in the road above the Geirangerfjord where the whole country seems to drop away in front of you. Ørnesvingen, the Eagle Bend, is a hairpin on the road from Eidsdal, and the viewpoint at its edge, at about six hundred and twenty metres, looks straight down onto the fjord, a long, narrow slot of green water between cliffs with waterfalls pouring from the rim. Ships look like toys at the village of Geiranger below, and the air smells of cold stone and wet moss.

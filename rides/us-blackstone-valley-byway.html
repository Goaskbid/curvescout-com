  1. 1. Providence

    Providence is the sort of city that rewards an early start and a loose plan. Roger Williams founded it in 1636 after being banished from the Massachusetts Bay Colony, and the idea he built it on, freedom of conscience, shaped the whole of Rhode Island. The city grew rich on shipping, jewelry and machine tools, and the result is a compact downtown of brick mills, church spires and one of the biggest marble domes in the country on the State House, which you can see from the highway as you approach.

  2. 2. Pawtucket

    Pawtucket is a mill city, and a particularly important one. In 1793, Samuel Slater, an English apprentice who had memorised the design of the new textile machinery in his home country, built the first successful water-powered cotton-spinning mill in America beside the falls of the Blackstone River here. The brick Slater Mill still stands beside the water, and is considered the birthplace of the American Industrial Revolution. The idea of using water, machines and a paid workforce spread rapidly down the valley and across New England, changing the way the country worked.

  3. 3. Woonsocket

    Woonsocket is the northern end of the Blackstone Valley in Rhode Island, right up against the Massachusetts line, and it has a character that sets it apart from every other New England mill town. In the late nineteenth century thousands of French-speaking families from Quebec came south to work in its textile mills, and by the early twentieth century Woonsocket was the most French city in the United States, with French-language newspapers, Catholic parishes, mutual aid societies and credit unions. The accent and surnames are still strong, and the city was nicknamed Little Quebec.

  4. 4. Uxbridge

    Uxbridge is a Massachusetts mill town that made its place in the history of American industry. It sits in the Blackstone Valley, on the upper reaches of the Blackstone River, in the south-central part of the state close to the Rhode Island border. The river fell steeply enough to drive water wheels, and in the early nineteenth century that was all it took: textile mills went up along its banks, a canal was dug to carry goods to Providence, and the valley became one of the first industrial landscapes in the country. Today the whole area is protected as the Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park, which tells the story of those early years with surviving mill buildings, locks and villages.

  5. 5. Worcester

    Worcester is the city people drive around on the way to somewhere else, which is a pity, because it has more going on than its reputation suggests. It is the second-largest city in New England, a hilly place of triple-decker houses, brick factories and church towers, and the old industrial heart of central Massachusetts. Its nickname is the Heart of the Commonwealth, and the pronunciation, Wuss-ter, trips up every newcomer.

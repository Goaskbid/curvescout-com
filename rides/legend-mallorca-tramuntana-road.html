Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Andratx

    Andratx is the south-western gateway to Mallorca's Serra de Tramuntana, the mountain range that runs like a spine along the island's north-western coast, and the start of the Ma-10, the island's most famous road. The town is a quiet inland place of about twelve thousand people, with a church fortified against pirates, narrow lanes and a lively Wednesday market, and the sea is a few kilometres away at Port d'Andratx, a smart harbour lined with restaurants and yachts.

  2. 2. Banyalbufar

    Banyalbufar clings to a slope above the sea on Mallorca's west coast, and the first thing you see is stairs. Thousands of stone terraces, built by the Moors and kept going ever since, step down from the road toward the water, holding up vines and tomatoes and a few lemon trees. The name is thought to come from Arabic, something like "little vineyard by the sea". The Malvasia wines grown here were once exported widely, and the village still makes a little. The terraces and the watchtower on the cliff, the Torre del Verger, give the place its character.

  3. 3. Valldemossa

    The Ma-10 is the road that made Mallorca's north-west famous with riders, and Valldemossa is its most photographed village. It lies in a fold of the Serra de Tramuntana, a long limestone ridge that runs down the island's western flank and earned UNESCO World Heritage status for its terraced hillsides and centuries-old water systems. Cobbled lanes, honey-coloured stone and green shutters crowd the village, and the hills behind are dark with holm oak and pine.

  4. 4. Deià

    Robert Graves, the English poet and author of I, Claudius, lived in Deià for much of his life, and he is buried in the little cemetery on the hill beside the church. That is the quickest way to understand the village: it has been a refuge for writers and painters since the early twentieth century, drawn by the light, the cheap rent of the time, and the stone houses stacked up a conical hill. Today the rent is no longer cheap, but the stack of golden houses against the dark backdrop of the Tramuntana is still lovely.

  5. 5. Sóller

    Sóller lies in a basin of orange and lemon trees, enclosed by the highest peaks of the Serra de Tramuntana, and it is the nearest thing in Mallorca to a green bowl. For centuries the only way in or out was a mountain pass or a boat, and the isolation made the valley rich and strange. Local merchants sold oranges to France and returned with money and ideas, and some of the town's buildings show it, including a modernista church front and a bank in a style borrowed from Barcelona. The old wooden train from Palma, which opened in 1912, still runs through tunnels and viaducts to the station in the middle of town, and a small tram carries passengers down to the harbour at Port de Sóller.

  6. 6. Near Port de Pollença

    Port de Pollença is the seaside half of a partnership, with the old hill town of Pollença six kilometres inland. The port sits on a wide, sheltered bay in the north of Mallorca, with the Tramuntana mountains on one side and the Formentor peninsula curving round the other, and the water is calm, shallow and a clear green-blue. A long, pine-shaded promenade runs along the waterfront, lined with cafés and ice-cream shops, and sailing boats and fishing vessels bob in the harbour. It has about six thousand people in winter, and many more in summer, and it feels like a holiday town without being too brash.

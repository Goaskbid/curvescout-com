Why this ride

From Posillipo it runs 316 km to Posillipo; allow about 5 h 27 of riding. It goes by way of Ancient Capua, The Via Appia from Sinuessa to the Pagus S, The Via Appia from 83rd mile to Formiae and Appia. Regina Viarum. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. District of Villa Manzo

    Posillipo is the hill that Naples looks at when it wants to feel it lives somewhere grand. It is a long ridge of soft volcanic tuff that runs out into the bay on the western side of the city, with villas, gardens and terraces, and views over the water to Vesuvius and the islands of Capri and Ischia. The Greeks called it Pausilypon, which means a pause from pain, and the Romans agreed: the rich built villas here, and the ruins of one of them, that of Vedius Pollio, can still be visited in a park above the sea. Virgil is said to be buried in the park near Mergellina, though the evidence is slim and the scholars are polite about it.

  2. 2. Ancient Capua

    Santa Maria Capua Vetere does not look like much as you ride in, a busy Campanian town of tangled traffic and apartment blocks, but under and around it lies ancient Capua, once one of the richest cities in Italy. The Via Appia passed straight through, and its great amphitheatre, second in size only to the Colosseum, still dominates the edge of town. This was where the gladiator schools trained their fighters, and it was from a school in Capua that Spartacus broke out in 73 BC with a band of fellow slaves, beginning the revolt that shook Rome for two years. The amphitheatre and a small gladiator museum nearby are the obvious stops, and the Mithraeum under the town, with its painted scene of the god slaying a bull, is one of the best preserved in Italy.

  3. 3. The Via Appia from Sinuessa to the Pagus S

    The Via Appia, the Queen of Roads, ran from Rome to Brindisi, and in 2024 UNESCO inscribed long stretches of it as a single World Heritage site. Near Mondragone, on the coast of northern Campania, the road passes the site of Sinuessa, a Roman colony that once guarded the route where it reached the sea after crossing the coastal plains from the north. Not a great deal stands above ground today, so this is a place where you read the landscape rather than tour the ruins.

  4. 4. The Via Appia from 83rd mile to Formiae

    The queen of roads, the Romans called it: the Via Appia, begun in 312 BC by the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, ran from Rome to Capua and later on to Brindisi, the gateway to Greece and the East. Near Formia, the old road comes down from the hills of Itri to the coast of the Gulf of Gaeta, and a stretch of it, with its paving stones and roadside monuments, survives in the countryside. In 2024, UNESCO listed the Via Appia as a whole, and this section between the eighty-third mile from Rome and the ancient town of Formiae is one of its pieces.

  5. 5. Via Appia. Regina Viarum

    Terracina is the point where the Via Appia, the queen of roads, meets the sea after its long straight run from Rome across the Pontine plain. The Romans built the road in 312 BC under the censor Appius Claudius, and for its time it was a marvel: paved, drained and as straight as engineering allowed, linking Rome to Capua and later to Brindisi. The stretch through the Pontine marshes was notorious for mosquitoes and malaria, and travellers often took a canal boat alongside the road instead, as the poet Horace describes in one of his satires.

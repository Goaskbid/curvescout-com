Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Valkenburg-Centrum

    A Roman fort is a surprising thing to find in a Dutch village, but Valkenburg has one buried underneath it, and the old Rhine still runs past its doorstep. The centre of the village is a small cluster of houses, a church and a few shops around a bend of the Oude Rijn, a few kilometres from the dunes of the North Sea coast. Roman soldiers built a fort here, Praetorium Agrippinae, to guard the river frontier of the empire, and the site is part of the Lower German Limes, which is now a UNESCO World Heritage property.

  2. 2. Fort along the Pampus

    Out in the open water of the IJmeer, a few kilometres off the old fortified town of Muiden, a low circular fortress sits on an artificial island. This is Pampus, one of more than forty forts that make up the Defence Line of Amsterdam, a ring of fortifications built around the city in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The idea was simple and very Dutch: in case of attack, large areas of land around the city would be flooded to a depth that was too shallow for boats and too deep for soldiers to wade, and the forts would guard the roads, railways and dykes that crossed the flooded zone. Pampus guarded the approach to Amsterdam by water. The line was listed by UNESCO in 1996.

  3. 3. Schokland and Surroundings

    Schokland was once an island in the Zuiderzee, a long, narrow strip of peat and clay surrounded by an inland sea that constantly threatened to swallow it. For centuries, its inhabitants lived on raised mounds and fought a losing battle against the water, as storms ate away at the island's edges. By the mid-nineteenth century, life had become so precarious that in 1859 the Dutch government ordered the island to be evacuated. Then, in the twentieth century, the Dutch did something extraordinary: they enclosed the Zuiderzee with a dam, the Afsluitdijk, completed in 1932, and drained large parts of it to create new land. The Noordoostpolder, drained in 1942, swallowed Schokland, and the former island now rises slightly above the surrounding fields, a long green ridge in a flat landscape of farmland. In 1995, Schokland became the first Dutch site to be listed by UNESCO, as a symbol of the Netherlands' heroic struggle against the water.

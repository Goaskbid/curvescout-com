Why this ride

From Velykyi Bereznyi it runs 600 km to Velykyi Bereznyi; allow about 11 h of riding. The stops in between: Smolnik-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Archa, Drohobych-Tserkva of Saint George\t and back through Smolnik-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Archa. It crosses Slovakia, Poland and Ukraine - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

The roads

Bieszczady Loop

From Ruská Bystrá to Smolnik-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Archa is 179 km, mostly on road 897. It climbs 640 m to the top at 789 m and drops 500 m again. Split it with a break around the middle.

The stops

  1. 1. Ruská Bystrá

    Start here.

    Hidden in a fold of the Vihorlat hills near the Ukrainian border, the little village of Ruská Bystrá has a wooden church built in the early eighteenth century, dedicated to Saint Nicholas, with a tower and a roof of wooden shingles and an iconostasis of painted icons inside. It is one of the Greek Catholic churches included in the World Heritage listing of the Wooden Churches of the Slovak Part of the Carpathian Mountain Area, inscribed in 2008, along with Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox churches across the country.

    Read the complete Ruská Bystrá write-up.

  2. 2. Smolnik-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Archa

    179 km in.

    In the Bieszczady mountains of south-eastern Poland, close to the border with Ukraine, a wooden church stands on a gentle rise above the San valley at Smolnik. It is dedicated to St Michael the Archangel and built in the style of the Boyko people, with three log sections stepping up from east to west under shingled roofs. Since 2013 it has been part of the World Heritage Site of Wooden Tserkvas of the Carpathian Region in Poland and Ukraine.

    Read the complete Smolnik-Tserkva of Saint Michael the Archa write-up.

  3. 3. Drohobych-Tserkva of Saint George\t

    300 km in.

    Bruno Schulz was born in Drohobych in 1892, spent most of his life here and was shot in its streets by a German officer in 1942. The writer and painter, whose strange, dreamlike stories turned this small Galician town into a place of myth, drew on the old market square, the narrow lanes and the attic rooms of a Jewish merchant family, and the town today is part of his legacy. Drohobych, with a population of nearly eighty thousand, grew rich on salt and oil, and the oil wells of nearby Boryslav gave the region the nickname of Galicia's Texas in the late nineteenth century. Ivan Franko, the great Ukrainian writer, went to school here.

Roadbook

  1. Murmansk start

    Murmansk is the largest city north of the Arctic Circle, a port of over three hundred thousand people on the eastern shore of Kola Bay, and it owes its existence to the sea. The water stays open all year, kept free of ice by a final tongue of the Gulf Stream, which made this a vital harbour during the First World War, when the city was founded in 1916, and during the Second, when Allied convoys brought supplies here from Britain and America. The city was heavily bombed, and its character today is dominated by blocks of Soviet housing, stern monuments and ships tied up along the waterfront.

    The most striking monument is Alyosha, a huge concrete soldier standing on a hill above the bay, raised in 1974 to honour the defenders of the Arctic. The city has the atomic icebreaker Lenin, the world's first nuclear-powered surface ship, which is now a museum moored at the harbour. In midwinter the sun does not rise for about six weeks, and the city lives under the glow of streetlights, auroras sometimes flickering above them. In summer, the sun does not set for several weeks, and the long evenings are bright and cool.

    For a rider, Murmansk is the start of the Arctic coast. The road to Teriberka, on the Barents Sea, is about a hundred and twenty kilometres of tundra, rock and water, with low birch scrub, reindeer and cold light. The coast is bare and wind-beaten, with surf crashing on dark rocks, and the old fishing village has a ship graveyard that has become well known from films and photographs. The surface is mostly good, though sections can have frost damage, and weather is a serious matter, with sudden fog, snow even in June and strong wind. The season for riding is short, roughly June to early September.

    Fuel is available in the city and in the nearby suburbs, such as Roslyakovo, and you should fill the tank before leaving, since services on the coast road are scarce. Warm clothing is essential, even in summer, and spare layers are wise. Mobile coverage is patchy once you leave the main roads.

    Try the local fish, salmon, cod and the sweet king crab that was introduced from the Pacific, and warm up in a cafe with tea and pastries. The city is not pretty, but it has a strong, stubborn character that suits the north.

  2. Tumanny stop

    Tumanny means foggy, which is fair warning. This is a small settlement on the road from Murmansk to Teriberka, built in Soviet times to house workers for the hydroelectric stations on the Voronya River, which drops through a chain of reservoirs and dams on its way to the Barents Sea. Much of the place has emptied since then, and the apartment blocks, some occupied and some abandoned, stand in the tundra with a look of having been set down by mistake. It is a strange, rather haunting stop.

    The landscape around Tumanny is the real reason to pause. The forest thins out here to dwarf birch and scrub, then gives way to open tundra, rolling hills of rock and moss, and lakes in every hollow. The colours change dramatically through the short season: green and white with cotton grass in July, then a riot of red, orange and gold in late August and September, when the tundra turns and the berries ripen. In winter the northern lights dance above it all, and the road becomes a white tunnel between snow walls.

    The road to Teriberka is the only way in and out, and for a long time it was notorious, a mix of rough gravel and broken asphalt. It has been much improved in recent years, though it is still exposed and can be affected by wind, snow and fog at any time of year. The riding is open and flowing, with long views, rolling hills and sweeping bends, and very little traffic outside summer weekends. Watch for reindeer on the road, and be prepared for sudden gusts of wind across the high ground.

    There is little in Tumanny in the way of services, perhaps a small shop, and fuel is not something to count on. Fill up in Murmansk or at the junction with the main road before heading out, and carry water and food. Teriberka, about forty kilometres further on, has cafes and guesthouses now, thanks to the tourism boom. Treat Tumanny as a moment to stop, take in the scale of the tundra, and feel how far north you have come.

  3. Teriberka stop

    For years Teriberka was a dying fishing village at the end of a bad road, and then a film made it famous. Andrey Zvyagintsev shot his drama Leviathan here, released in 2014, and the images of rusting boats, wooden houses and a grey Arctic sea brought a wave of curious visitors. Today the village of about thirteen hundred people has guesthouses, cafes and tour operators offering whale-watching trips and northern lights excursions, but it has kept its raw, end-of-the-world feeling, especially when the wind blows hard off the Barents Sea.

    The village sits at the mouth of the Teriberka River, where it meets a wide bay. There are two parts, the old village near the sea and a newer settlement upriver, and between them you will find the famous ship graveyard: old fishing boats lying on their sides in the tidal flats, slowly rusting away. Pomor fishermen settled here centuries ago, and in Soviet times there was a fish processing plant and a collective farm. The decline that followed the 1990s left many buildings empty, and some are now being repaired for tourism.

    The road from Murmansk, about a hundred and thirty kilometres away, crosses open tundra and rolling hills, and is in much better shape than it once was. The last stretch descends toward the sea with wide views over the bay and the coast. Weather here is unpredictable: summer days can be bright and clear, with the sun never setting, or cold, wet and foggy. Riding in from late June through August is the most comfortable, but September brings autumn colours and the first northern lights.

    Teriberka has a few cafes serving fresh fish and seafood, including cod, halibut and king crab, which has spread along this coast since it was introduced in the Soviet era. There are guesthouses and simple hotels, but book ahead in summer. Fuel is limited, so arrive with a full tank from Murmansk and enough for the return. Walk down to the shore, watch the waves, and wander among the old boats; the place has a stark beauty that stays with you long after you leave.

  4. Barents Sea Coast stop

    Standing on the shore just west of Teriberka, you are looking at the Barents Sea, and beyond it, a very long way away, the ice of the Arctic Ocean. It is an unexpectedly lively sea. Thanks to the last warm reach of the North Atlantic current, this stretch of coast stays free of ice all winter, which is why Murmansk could be built as a year-round port and why fishermen have always come here. The water teems with cod, haddock and capelin, and in summer you may see humpback or killer whales feeding offshore, with seabirds wheeling above them.

    The coast itself is rocky and wild. Dark, ancient granite and gneiss rise in low cliffs and headlands, the beaches are made of pebbles and boulders rounded by the waves, and the tundra comes right down to the shore, green and brown and splashed with flowers in the brief summer. One beach near Teriberka is famous for its oval stones, smooth and egg-shaped, which visitors call dragon's eggs. Driftwood, kelp and the odd bit of fishing gear lie along the tideline.

    The riding around here is short and rough. From the village, tracks head along the coast and over the hills, some passable on a capable adventure bike, others better left to walkers. Gravel, rock, sand and mud all appear within a few hundred metres, and the wind can be relentless, strong enough to push a bike sideways on exposed ground. Fog can roll in quickly, cutting visibility to a few metres. Ride slowly, stay on existing tracks to protect the fragile tundra, and do not venture too far alone.

    Services are in Teriberka, a kilometre or so away, where there are cafes, guesthouses and a shop, but fuel is limited, so arrive with enough from Murmansk. Dress warmly even in summer: the temperature by the sea rarely climbs much above the low teens, and the wind chill can be sharp. Find a sheltered rock, sit for a while, and listen to the waves grinding the pebbles. In the soft light of the midnight sun, this is one of the most elemental places you can reach on a motorcycle in Europe.

  5. Teriberka Waterfall finish

    A few kilometres beyond Teriberka village, past the boulder beaches and over a rise of bare tundra, a stream drains a chain of small lakes and drops over a ledge of dark rock almost straight toward the sea. Locals usually call it the Batareyny or Battery waterfall, after old coastal defences nearby. It is not huge, but the setting makes it unforgettable: white water against black rock, a pool below, the open Barents Sea just beyond, and the tundra rolling away in every direction under a vast sky.

    The area around it shows the coast at its wildest. The lakes above the fall are clear and cold, ringed by moss, lichen and low shrubs that turn crimson and gold in late summer. Cloudberries ripen in the boggy patches in August, and sea birds patrol the cliffs. In June and July the sun circles the sky without setting, and the light at midnight is soft and golden. In autumn the first northern lights appear, and some visitors come back in winter to see the waterfall frozen into columns of ice.

    As the finish of a ride along the Teriberka coast, this is an off-road end. The track from the village is rough and rocky, with mud, sand, standing water and some steep, loose sections. On a light adventure bike with good tyres it is manageable for an experienced rider, but many people park near the village and walk, which takes an hour or so each way. Keep to the existing tracks, because the tundra scars easily and recovers slowly. Weather can change fast, so carry waterproofs and warm layers even on a sunny day.

    There are no services out here. Teriberka, about six kilometres back, has cafes, guesthouses and a shop, but fuel is limited, so arrive with a full tank from Murmansk and enough to get back. Bring water and a snack, and allow time to sit by the falls and take it all in. With the sound of falling water, the sea beyond, and no one else around on a quiet day, it is the kind of place that makes the long ride north entirely worth it.

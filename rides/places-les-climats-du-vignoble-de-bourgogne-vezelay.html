  1. 1. Les climats du vignoble de Bourgogne

    The Côte de Beaune and the Côte de Nuits are two ridges of gentle limestone, barely a hundred metres above the plain of the Saône, and they hold some of the most expensive agricultural land on earth. The phrase Les Climats du vignoble de Bourgogne refers to the small named vineyard plots, each with its own character of soil and exposure, that Burgundians have mapped and cultivated for centuries; the monks of Cluny and Cîteaux did much of the early work, noticing that wine from one plot tasted different from the next and writing it down. UNESCO recognised the whole system as a cultural landscape, and driving through it you can see why, because the walled vineyards, stone huts and village squares are all part of the same story.

  2. 2. Les climats du vignoble de Bourgogne - Dij

    Dijon sits at the top of the Côte d'Or, where the Burgundian plain meets the long limestone escarpment that carries some of the most expensive vineyards in the world. The UNESCO listing called the Climats of Burgundy covers those vineyards and the towns that grew around them, and the label's last word, cut short, means Dijon itself, the old ducal capital where the Valois dukes ruled a territory that rivalled the French crown. The Palace of the Dukes and Estates, now a museum and town hall, still dominates the central square, and the pedestrian streets around it are full of half-timbered houses and fine stone doorways.

  3. 3. Cistercian Abbey of Fontenay

    The Abbey of Fontenay lies in a narrow, wooded valley in northern Burgundy, a few kilometres from the small town of Montbard. It was founded in 1118 by Bernard of Clairvaux's circle and became one of the earliest and best-preserved Cistercian houses in Europe. The Cistercians wanted a life of simplicity, work and silence, and they built accordingly: plain stone, no carvings of saints, no coloured glass, and a site chosen for water and seclusion. The result is a place of extraordinary calm.

  4. 4. Vézelay

    You see Vézelay from a long way off, a hill crowned with a basilica, with a village strung along the ridge below it. It is one of the great Romanesque churches of France, the Basilica of Sainte-Marie-Madeleine, begun in the early twelfth century to house relics said to be those of Mary Magdalene. Pilgrims came in thousands, and in 1146 Bernard of Clairvaux preached the Second Crusade here, to a crowd so large that he spoke in the open air. It is also a starting point for one of the Camino de Santiago routes, so you often see walkers with scallop shells on their packs.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Diamantina

    Diamantina earned its name the hard way. In the early 1720s, prospectors in the hills of Minas Gerais found diamonds in the gravel of the streams, and for the next hundred years this high, rocky corner of Brazil supplied most of the world's stones. The Portuguese Crown clamped down at once, declaring a restricted diamond district, with a monopoly on trade, permits, soldiers and informers, and the town that grew up at the centre of it, on a cold plateau in the Serra do Espinhaço at around 1,200 metres, became rich and strange. Today it has about thirty-seven thousand people, and the colonial centre was inscribed by UNESCO in 1999.

  2. 2. Mariana

    Mariana is where Minas Gerais begins, at least on paper. It was the first town the Portuguese founded in the gold country, in 1696, and later the first capital and first bishopric of the whole captaincy. It was named for Maria Anna of Austria, the queen of João V, which tells you who the town was meant to impress. Today it sits at around seven hundred metres in a bowl of green, forested hills, a calm university and mining town of some forty-five thousand people, and it is the natural place to begin the Estrada Real, the old colonial road along which gold and diamonds went to the coast and everything else came back up.

  3. 3. Ouro Preto

    Ouro Preto means black gold, after the dark, iron-stained nuggets that set off the rush here at the end of the seventeenth century. For a while this town, then called Vila Rica, was among the richest and most populous places in the Americas, and its money went into churches, bridges, fountains and the steep, tilted streets you will be climbing and descending. UNESCO listed it in 1980, among the very first Brazilian sites, and walking in the centre you can see why: hardly a modern building disturbs the skyline of terracotta roofs and whitewashed towers climbing the hillsides.

  4. 4. Tiradentes

    Tiradentes is a small colonial town in the hills of Minas Gerais, in south-eastern Brazil, where the paving is cobbled stone, the church is full of gold, and the streets run uphill toward the Serra de Sao Jose. It has about four and a half thousand people and a calendar crowded with festivals, and the bigger town next door, Sao Joao del Rei, with nearly eighty thousand, is where you find supermarkets and a wider choice of fuel stations.

  5. 5. Paraty

    Paraty is a small colonial town on a bay of the Costa Verde, where the Serra do Mar mountains drop in green folds to a sea scattered with islands. It was a port of the gold rush in the eighteenth century, when gold from Minas Gerais came down a mule trail through the forest, the Caminho do Ouro, and was loaded here for the voyage to Rio and then Lisbon. The town is remarkably well preserved, with whitewashed houses with coloured door frames, a handful of churches, and streets paved with big uneven stones that are laid, so they say, to let the sea flush them clean at the highest tides.

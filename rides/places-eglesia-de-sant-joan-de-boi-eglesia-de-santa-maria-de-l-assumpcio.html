Why this ride

A 118 km round trip from Vielha - roughly 2 h 44 in the saddle. It goes by way of Balma de les Ovelles, Eglésia de Santa Maria de l'Assumpció and back through Balma de les Ovelles. It tops out around 2,000 m.

The roads

35 km from Eglésia de Sant Joan de Boí to Balma de les Ovelles, mostly on L-500. It climbs 420 m to the top at 1,278 m and drops 410 m again.

The stops

  1. 1. Eglésia de Sant Joan de Boí

    Start here.

    Boí is the lowest and quietest of the Romanesque villages in this Pyrenean valley, and its church of Sant Joan stands among old stone houses a little above the road. It is one of the nine churches in the Vall de Boí listed by UNESCO, and unlike the better-known Taüll it has a quieter, more domestic feel. The nave is plain, the tower is square and slim, and a porch along the side gives the building the look of a country chapel rather than a monument. Painted decoration survives inside, including scenes on the walls, though much of what you see is a careful reproduction while the original fragments are kept in museums in Barcelona and Lleida.

  2. 2. Balma de les Ovelles

    35 km in.

    The Balma de les Ovelles, the Sheep's Shelter, lies in the high country where Catalonia meets Aragon, between the valleys of the Noguera Ribagorçana and the Noguera Pallaresa. This is the southern edge of the Pyrenees, a land of limestone ridges, deep gorges, reservoirs and scattered villages, many of them half empty. Rock shelters like this one, used by shepherds and their flocks for thousands of years, are found throughout the region, and some hold traces of prehistoric painting or occupation. The name tells you what it was most recently used for, and the landscape around still carries the sound of sheep bells.

    Read the complete Balma de les Ovelles write-up.

  3. 3. Eglésia de Santa Maria de l'Assumpció

    61 km in.

    Valleys in the high Pyrenees of Catalonia and Aragon are full of small Romanesque churches, and the area around the Alta Ribagorça is among the richest. They were built in the eleventh and twelfth centuries by craftsmen working in what is called the Lombard style, with plain stone walls, round arches and tall, slender bell towers decorated with blind arcades, and they stand in villages that often have only a few houses.

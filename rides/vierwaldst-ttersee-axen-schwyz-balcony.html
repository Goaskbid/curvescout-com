Why this ride

Lake walls, tunnels and a scenic route that demands attention. A 108 km loop that starts and ends near Luzern, about 2 h 05 of riding. The stops in between: Brunnen water stop, Axenstrasse cliff road and Schwyz. The climb of the day is Sattel passlet. It is a high-mountain day.

Roadbook

  1. Lucerne lakefront gate photo · 436 m

    Lucerne works best if you arrive by water, but a motorcycle at the right hour comes close. The city sits where Lake Lucerne narrows into the river Reuss, with the Pilatus massif standing guard on one side and the Rigi on the other, and the old town spills along both banks like a stage set that happens to be real. The wooden Chapel Bridge, first built in 1333, crosses the river at an angle, with painted panels under the roof, and its stone water tower has served as a prison, an archive and a treasury. A fire in 1993 destroyed much of the bridge, and what you walk on today is mostly a careful reconstruction.

    The Lion Monument, carved into a sandstone face in the early nineteenth century, commemorates the Swiss Guards who died defending the Tuileries Palace in Paris in 1792. It is a surprisingly moving piece, a dying lion with a spear in its flank, and Mark Twain famously called it the saddest and most moving piece of stone in the world. A short walk from the lake brings you to it, and it is quiet and slightly eerie even in the tourist season.

    From a rider's perspective, Lucerne is an arrival rather than a ride. The traffic can be heavy, especially in the summer, and the lakefront is busy with pedestrians, coaches and cyclists. But the setting is superb, and the early evening light on the water and the mountains is spectacular. Park the bike in a designated motorcycle bay, because the city enforces parking, and walk the promenade. The modern concert hall by the station, with its huge floating roof, stands as a counterpoint to the medieval bridges.

    Fuel is not a concern in a city of fifty-seven thousand, with large stations on the approach roads, though they can be crowded at peak hours. Coffee and lunch are everywhere, from bakeries to lake terraces. Prices are high, so choose with that in mind.

    If you have time, pick a table with a view of the water and watch the paddle steamers pull in. The mountains often wear a cap of cloud, which can lift suddenly and reveal the full scene. That moment, when Pilatus appears from nowhere, is the reason people come back.

  2. Brunnen water stop swim · 435 m

    Brunnen sits on a bend in Lake Lucerne where the water turns the corner toward the Urner arm, and from the promenade you can look straight across to the meadow of the Rütli, the spot where the founders of Switzerland are said to have sworn their oath in 1291. The town has a place in the story itself: in 1315, after the victory at Morgarten, the confederates met in Brunnen to renew their alliance, and a stone on the shore commemorates the writer Friedrich Schiller, whose play on William Tell made the legend famous.

    The lake is the star. The water is cold, clear and a deep green, and the surrounding mountains drop steeply into it, so the atmosphere is almost fjord-like. The Föhn wind, when it blows from the south, sweeps down the Urner arm with dramatic force and turns the surface white, and the sky takes on a dazzling clarity. Brunnen has a lively waterfront, with boats, ferries and paddle steamers calling at the quay, and a long walkway with benches where it is pleasant to sit and eat a sandwich.

    Swimming is a local pastime in summer, and there are public bathing places along the shore where the water is bracing even in August. A short dip is a good way to cool off after a long day in leathers.

    Riding out of Brunnen on the Axenstrasse is among the great short drives in Switzerland. The road clings to the cliffs above Lake Uri, passing through tunnels and galleries blasted into the rock, with windows that open onto the water. It was built in the nineteenth century as a link between the cantons, and the engineering is still impressive. Be aware that rockfall can close sections of the road, so check conditions, and expect heavy traffic on summer weekends. Brunnen and the neighbouring municipalities have fuel and food in abundance, and the town is a convenient base before the climb or the run toward Altdorf.

  3. Axenstrasse cliff road scenic · 440 m

    If the Axenstrasse had been built today, it would probably be declared impossible. It runs along the cliffs of the Urnersee, with the lake directly below and rock walls climbing above, and it was cut in the 1860s when engineers still believed in pickaxes and gunpowder. Its main purpose was to link the Gotthard corridor to central Switzerland without relying on lake steamers. The tunnels and galleries along the route were chiselled out by hand, and the carved portals are among the nicest bits of old infrastructure you will see.

    This is the stretch where the mountain wins. The road clings to ledges, dips into short tunnels with windows opening onto the water, and bursts out into sunshine with a view straight across to the Uri Alps. Near the northern end the famous chapel dedicated to William Tell sits at the water's edge, which can be reached by boat or on foot. Whether Tell existed is a matter for historians. The scenery here is real and the legend belongs to the place, and in Altdorf, a few minutes down the road, a statue of the archer and his son stands in the square.

    The riding is slow and thoughtful. The speed limit is low, the bends are tight, and the tunnels demand attention. Mid-morning sun lights the opposite shore, and evenings give a golden glow on the western cliffs. Winter ice can linger in shaded galleries, and spring rains sometimes loosen stones from the slope above. Treat any warning sign seriously, and expect that the road can close for rock-clearing.

    For fuel and supplies, Ingenbohl and Brunnen at the southern end of the lake are the nearest, and the Uri side has stations around Flüelen and Altdorf. There are viewpoints and a few restaurants along the way, though some are seasonal. If you want a quiet moment, look for a lay-by where you can stop out of the traffic, remove your helmet and listen. The lake is so deep and still that sound seems to fall away.

    The return is as good as the outward leg. With the sun in a different position, the whole view looks new, and you will catch details you missed on the first pass.

  4. Schwyz food stop food · 516 m

    Schwyz is the village that gave Switzerland its name. The country's flag grew from the banner of this canton, and the oldest surviving federal charter, dated to 1291, is kept in a museum in town, written on parchment with seals that have survived seven centuries. If history is the thing for you, this is as close to the source as you can get, and a short walk in the old centre shows how modest the beginnings were.

    The town itself is lively without being busy: a broad square with a baroque church, a town hall with painted facades showing a battle scene, and stately patrician houses built by men who had made fortunes as mercenary officers in foreign armies. Behind it all rise the Mythen, two sharp limestone peaks that look from the village like a pair of horns and dominate every photograph. The cloud sits on them often, and on a clear morning they are a fine sight with coffee in hand.

    With fourteen thousand people, Schwyz has fuel, bakeries and sit-down restaurants, so as a start or a food stop it is easy. The start of the loop is the main reason to be here: from Schwyz the road climbs south and east into the hills, toward the Ibergeregg and the Pragel, a pair of passes that make a rewarding day. The first miles run through meadows dotted with fruit trees and farmhouses with deep-eaved wooden roofs, and the gradient builds gradually, so there is time to warm up the tyres and your rhythm.

    Weather can be unpredictable here, because the Alps' northern edge catches the moist air coming from the lowlands, and a bright start can become grey by lunchtime. Plan accordingly, with a waterproof near the top of the luggage. The Föhn wind also blows through this region, warm and dry from the south, and on those days the air is incredibly clear but the gusts can be strong on the open stretches. In winter, the high passes close, but the town stays lively, and a bowl of something hot in a Gasthaus makes a fair reward for the effort.

  5. Sattel return passlet pass · 790 m

    Coming back over Sattel is a different experience from going out. In the late afternoon the light comes in low from the west, the Rigi is a long dark shape against the glow, and the saddle at about 790 metres feels quieter. The road here winds along gentle slopes with farms dotted across the meadows and the occasional chapel at a bend. Traffic is light, and if you time it right you will share the road mainly with a few cyclists and the odd tractor.

    This is also the corner of Switzerland where the old story of the Confederation is told in battlefields. A few kilometres to the south and east lies the Rothenthurm moor, which became an unlikely symbol of Swiss politics in 1987, when voters approved an initiative to protect the moors of the country and stopped a planned army training ground. It was one of the first times environmental protection won in a national referendum in such a striking way. Close to the Ägerisee, the monument at Morgarten marks the 1315 battle, where mountain men from Schwyz ambushed Habsburg troops on a narrow shore path.

    For the ride, the return over Sattel is a chance to settle into a rhythm. The corners are open, the surface is generally smooth, and the gradients are steady. Many riders use it as the final climb of the day before descending toward the lake of Zug, so the pace tends to be relaxed. Early evening in September is lovely, with the smell of cut grass and a faint chill that makes the warm tarmac feel kind.

    Unterägeri, about seven kilometres away, is the nearest sizeable town for fuel and food, and the villages of Rothenthurm and Steinen are alternatives. There is often a small restaurant or bakery in Sattel itself, but do not rely on late opening. If you need something to eat, plan it for the valley.

    The hills here catch weather from the west, so watch the sky. A shower can sweep in with little warning, and wet leaves on the tarmac get slick in autumn. Take the descent gently, and use the lay-bys if you want to look back at the way you came.

  6. Back to Lucerne finish · 436 m

    Lucerne is where central Switzerland likes to show off. The town sits at the end of its lake, with the Reuss river sliding out through the middle, old houses with painted gables along the quay, and mountains on every side, Pilatus to the south and the Rigi to the east. The best-known sight is the Kapellbrücke, the Chapel Bridge, a covered wooden footbridge from the fourteenth century with a stone water tower beside it, and painted panels under the roof. A fire in 1993 burned much of it, and the bridge was rebuilt, so the paintings you see are mostly reconstructions, but the shape is old. Nearby the Lion Monument, carved into a sandstone cliff in 1821, commemorates Swiss guards who died in Paris in 1792, and Mark Twain called it the saddest piece of stone in the world.

    For a motorcyclist, Lucerne is both a hub and a reward. From here the roads fan out toward the Brünig, the Susten, the Furka, the Grimsel and the Gotthard, and in the other direction toward the Entlebuch and the Emmental. The town itself is not for riding, with a dense one-way system, many bus lanes and fines that are issued without sympathy, so park in one of the garages and walk. Speed limits are strictly enforced across Switzerland, and cameras are plentiful.

    The best time is late spring or early autumn, when the light is soft and tour buses are fewer. In July and August the lakefront is full and the Alpine passes are open, with the highest ones usually closed from late autumn into early summer. Lake fog in winter can be thick, though the mountains above are often sunny. The air smells of water and woodsmoke when you arrive by the lake road in the evening.

    At a little over four hundred metres above sea level, the town has everything, with fuel stations on every approach, workshops, hotels and restaurants. Try a Luzerner Chügelipastete, the local pastry filled with veal and mushrooms, or just a coffee at the lakeside. Prices are high, and a vignette is needed on motorways, though you will rarely use them in the mountains. Walk the old town in the evening, cross both bridges, and leave the bike for a ferry ride across the lake.

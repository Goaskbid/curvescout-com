Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. ‘Amārat Ali Rashed Fakhro

    Muharraq was the capital of Bahrain before Manama took over, and it is the part of the country where the old pearl trade still shows in the buildings. For centuries, before cultured pearls and oil, Bahrain lived on pearls. Hundreds of boats left each summer for the oyster beds, men dived without equipment, holding their breath and wearing a nose clip, and merchants in Muharraq bought the catch and sold it on to dealers in Bombay and Paris. The wealth shaped the town, and UNESCO listed the Pearling Path, a group of merchants' houses, shops and fortifications, in 2012.

  2. 2. Madinat Hamad 2 Burial Mound Field

    Flat, pale and apparently empty, the central plain of Bahrain hides one of the oldest cemeteries on earth. Some four thousand years ago the island of Dilmun, as it was known to the Sumerians, buried its dead under thousands upon thousands of low stone mounds, and in places like Madinat Hamad and A'ali the mounds still stand in dense, grey fields. At one time there may have been several hundred thousand of them, which would have made Dilmun a famous necropolis in its day, and many have since been flattened by roads, housing and quarrying.

  3. 3. Al Zubarah Archaeological Site

    Out on the north-west coast of Qatar, where the desert slides into a shallow turquoise sea, stand the low ruins of Al Zubarah, once one of the busiest pearling and trading towns in the Gulf. In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the town thrived on pearls dived from the oyster beds offshore, traded on to India, Persia and beyond. Then it declined, was abandoned in the early twentieth century, and the sand covered it. Archaeologists have since uncovered streets, houses, a souk, mosques and a long town wall running in an arc around the old harbour. UNESCO inscribed the site in 2013.

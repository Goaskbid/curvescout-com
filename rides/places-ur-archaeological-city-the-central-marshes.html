Why this ride

A 350 km round trip from Nasiriyah - roughly 6 h 02 in the saddle. The stops in between: Tell Eridu Archaeological Site, The West Hammar Marshes, The Central Marshes and back through The West Hammar Marshes. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Ur Archaeological City

    Ur stands on the flat Mesopotamian plain in southern Iraq, close to Nasiriyah, among the oldest cities in the world. Its great ziggurat, a stepped brick platform built by King Ur-Nammu around 2100 BC, still rises out of the desert, restored and shaped like a huge brick wedding cake with stairs up the front. It was a temple to the moon god Nanna, and the city around it was a major port on what was then the Euphrates, before the river shifted course and the sea retreated. Tradition names Ur as the birthplace of Abraham, which is why Pope Francis held a prayer meeting here in 2021.

  2. 2. Tell Eridu Archaeological Site

    Eridu is where the Sumerians said civilisation began. According to their own king lists, kingship came down from heaven and landed first at Eridu, and the city's god, Enki, ruler of the sweet waters beneath the earth, was the keeper of the arts of civilised life. Archaeologists found a sequence of temples stacked one above another, beginning in about 5400 BC, which makes it one of the oldest settlements in Mesopotamia. On top of it all rises the ruined ziggurat of Ur-Nammu, a mud-brick stepped tower built around 2100 BC, now a worn brown stump in the desert.

  3. 3. The West Hammar Marshes

    The marshes of southern Iraq lie where the Tigris and Euphrates come together on their way to the Gulf, a vast network of reed beds, lagoons and channels that was, until recent decades, home to one of the oldest ways of life on earth. The West Hammar Marshes are the westernmost, south of the city of Nasiriyah, and this stop lies about fifteen kilometres from the small town of Al Fuhud. UNESCO inscribed the Ahwar of Southern Iraq in 2016, along with the ancient Sumerian cities of Ur, Uruk and Eridu, which stood on the edge of this same wetland.

  4. 4. The Central Marshes

    Between the Tigris and the Euphrates, in the far south of Iraq, lies a world of water, reeds and sky: the Mesopotamian Marshes, home for thousands of years to the Ma'dan, the Marsh Arabs, who build houses and great arched guest halls, the mudhif, entirely from reeds, travel by narrow boats called mashoof and keep herds of water buffalo. The Central Marshes are one of the main areas of this wetland, listed in 2016 with the archaeological cities of Ur, Uruk and Eridu as the Ahwar of Southern Iraq, a World Heritage Site that joins nature and the earliest cities of Sumer.

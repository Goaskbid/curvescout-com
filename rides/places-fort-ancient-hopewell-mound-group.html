Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Fort Ancient

    Fort Ancient is not a fort in the military sense, and it is not as old as its name might suggest. It is a huge earthwork built by the Hopewell people about two thousand years ago on a bluff above the Little Miami River, in the Ohio hill country between Cincinnati and Dayton. Its walls run for roughly three and a half miles, and they enclose about a hundred acres of high ground. Gaps in the walls, called gateways, appear to align with the movement of the sun and moon, which suggests a ceremonial rather than a defensive purpose.

  2. 2. Seip Earthworks

    The Hopewell people built in earth the way others build in stone. About two thousand years ago, in the valleys of southern Ohio, they raised enormous banks, circles, squares and octagons, some enclosing many hectares, and aligned them to the sun and moon. Seip Earthworks, in the valley of Paint Creek in Ross County, is part of the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks, which were inscribed as a World Heritage site in 2023, and the group also includes the Octagon Earthworks at Newark and the High Bank Works near Chillicothe. They were not forts or farms, but meeting places for gatherings, feasts and funerals.

  3. 3. Hopewell Mound Group

    Among the fields of the Paint Creek valley in southern Ohio, a few kilometres from Chillicothe, lies a patchwork of low grassy ridges that once formed one of the largest earthwork enclosures in North America. The Hopewell Mound Group is the site that gave its name to the whole Hopewell culture, which flourished in the Ohio valley from about 200 BC to AD 500. It was named after Mordecai Cloud Hopewell, on whose farm it stood in the nineteenth century. The enclosure originally surrounded about a hundred hectares, with walls and many mounds, and archaeologists have found rich deposits of ceremonial objects within.

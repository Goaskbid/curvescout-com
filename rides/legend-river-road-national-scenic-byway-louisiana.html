Why this ride

National Scenic Byway. Mississippi River, plantation landscapes, historic towns. 135 km from New Orleans to Baton Rouge, about 2 h 27 of riding. On the way it passes Destrehan, Paulina and Donaldsonville. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. New Orleans

    New Orleans exists because of a bend. The French founded the city in 1718 on a crescent of high ground in the Mississippi River, a spot that gave them a portage to Lake Pontchartrain and control of the great river's mouth, and the bend still shapes everything, from street names to the way people give directions by river and lake rather than by compass. The city is mostly below sea level, kept dry by levees and pumps, and after Hurricane Katrina in 2005 the whole country learned how fragile that arrangement can be. People here talk about it plainly and carry on.

  2. 2. Destrehan

    Driving upriver from New Orleans, Destrehan is the first place where the old Mississippi plantation country comes into view, and it does so in a very ordinary way: between a refinery, a levee and a row of suburban houses. Then you see the long avenue of oaks, the white gallery and the deep roof of Destrehan Plantation, a house begun in the 1780s that is among the oldest surviving plantation homes in the lower Mississippi valley. It has been restored, and visitors can tour the house and its grounds.

  4. 4. Donaldsonville

    Donaldsonville sits where Bayou Lafourche meets the Mississippi, and that confluence has shaped its fortunes ever since the French and Spanish colonial period. It is one of the oldest towns on the river, laid out in the early nineteenth century by a man named Donaldson, and for a short time, in 1830 and 1831, it served as the capital of Louisiana. Today it is a quiet town of about eight thousand people with a historic district of wooden and brick buildings, iron balconies and live oaks, and a slightly faded, thoughtful atmosphere.

  5. 5. Baton Rouge

    Baton Rouge sits on the first high bluff upriver from the Gulf of Mexico, and the Mississippi bends around it, huge, brown and slow. French explorers noted a red post on the bank marking the boundary between tribal hunting grounds and called the place by that name, red stick, and the city has kept it ever since. Today it is the state capital of Louisiana, a city of over two hundred thousand, with a big university, the oil and chemical industry, and a food culture that rivals New Orleans without the tourist crowds.

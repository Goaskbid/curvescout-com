Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. La Roca dels moros

    El Cogul is a village of a few dozen houses in Les Garrigues, the low, stony plateau of western Catalonia where the whole landscape seems to be one big olive grove. Just outside it, a wall of rock above a small valley holds one of the most important prehistoric paintings in the country. The shelter is known as the Roca dels Moros, the Moors' Rock, which is simply the Catalan habit of attributing anything old and mysterious to the Moors, and it was brought to scientific attention in the early twentieth century by a local priest.

  2. 2. Abric del Barranc de Sant Jaume

    Mequinenza had to move. In the 1960s and 1970s, two dams built on the Ebro created a huge reservoir known as the Mar de Aragón, and the old town at the meeting of the Ebro and the Segre was abandoned and largely demolished, its people resettled in a new town above. The story is told in the town's museums and in the novels of Jesús Moncada, who grew up in the old Mequinenza. In a ravine nearby, the Abric del Barranc de Sant Jaume holds prehistoric paintings, part of the World Heritage Site of Rock Art of the Mediterranean Basin.

  3. 3. Val de Caballé I

    Here the Ebro stops being a river and becomes a lake. Behind the great dams at Mequinenza and Riba-roja, the water backs up for kilometres into the dry hills of eastern Aragon, and locals call the reservoir the Mar de Aragón, the sea of Aragon. Val de Caballé is a gully in the hills south of the water, between Mequinenza, Nonaspe and Fabara, part of a scatter of prehistoric rock shelters in this corner where Aragon, Catalonia and the Matarranya meet.

  4. 4. Abric del Barranc de Canà o de la mina fed

    Before the reservoir and the anglers' boats, Mequinenza was a coal town. The seams of lignite that run under the hills around the confluence of the Segre and the Ebro were dug for generations, and the coal was shipped down the river to feed the furnaces and power stations of a growing Spain. When the mines faded and the old town was submerged, a good part of the population left, and the people who remained are proud of a heritage that is half underground and half underwater. The Barranc de Canà shelter lies five kilometres out, in dry hills between the town and the road to Fraga.

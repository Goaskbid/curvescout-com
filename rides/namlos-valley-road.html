Why this ride

A 49 km one-way ride from near Stanzach, about 49 min of riding. On the way it passes Namlos and Berwang. The highest point is about 1,700 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Stanzach

    Stanzach is a small village in the Lechtal, the long valley of the Lech river in Tyrol, and the river is the making of it. The Lech here is still wild, with a broad bed of pale gravel, braided channels and shifting banks that change after every flood, and conservationists have protected it as part of the Tyrolean Lech Nature Park, because few Alpine rivers still run free in this way. Kingfishers, dippers and the rare little ringed plover nest on the gravel, and in spring the water is the colour of milky jade. Hikers on the Lechweg, a long-distance footpath, pass through the village.

  2. 2. Namlos

    Namlos is a village at the head of a long, quiet side valley in the Lechtal Alps of Tyrol, and the road that leads to it is the reason to come. It is one of the smallest places on the map, a handful of farms and houses at an altitude of more than a thousand metres, with steep forested slopes and limestone peaks around. There is no through traffic, because the valley ends in a cul-de-sac of mountains, apart from the road across to Berwang, which makes it a ride that few visitors know about.

  3. 3. Berwang

    Berwang sits on a high, sunny terrace above the Lech valley, at about 1,300 metres, a village of wooden chalets and green meadows, with the long ridge of the Lechtal Alps behind and the great bulk of the Zugspitze, Germany's highest mountain, visible to the north-east. It is part of the Zugspitz Arena, a ski and hiking region that spans the border of Tyrol and Bavaria, and the village is a good deal busier than its neighbour Namlos, with hotels, a few shops and lifts.

  4. 4. Reutte

    Reutte lies in a broad basin where the Lech valley opens and the mountains step back, a town of under seven thousand in the Außerfern, the part of Tyrol that is cut off from the rest of the province by the Alps and is connected more easily with Bavaria than with Innsbruck. For centuries the town was a stop on the trade route between Italy and southern Germany, and it grew rich on the carriage trade and on the road that crossed the Fern Pass. Its long main street, Untermarkt, still has old merchants' houses with painted facades and ornate oriel windows, and the market feel remains.

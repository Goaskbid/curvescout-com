Why this ride

341 km from Nerpio to Nerpio, about 5 h 53 of riding. On the way it passes Solana del molinico, Cueva del Niño, Cavidad II and Cueva del Peliciego. The highest point is about 1,300 m. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cañaica del Calar lII

    Past the village of Nerpio, the land climbs toward the Calar, a high limestone plateau with thin soil, pine woods and open pasture, and the name Cañaica del Calar tells you where you are. Cañaica sounds like a diminutive of cañada, a drove road, and old tracks of that kind cross the sierra, used for centuries by shepherds moving flocks between summer and winter pastures. The shelter here, the third of a group, lies eleven kilometres from Nerpio, among those quiet uplands, which is farther than most of the other sites in the district and gives a stronger sense of isolation.

  2. 2. Solana del molinico

    Socovos is the sort of village you only find by getting a little lost: a cluster of white houses below a ruined castle in the far southeast of Albacete province, where the plains of La Mancha give way to the tangled ridges of the Sierra de Segura. Solana del Molinico, close by, is one of the rock-art shelters in this corner of Spain that belong to the UNESCO listing for Levantine rock art. The word solana means a sun-facing slope, and many of these painted overhangs face south, catching the warmth on winter days just as they must have done when hunters and gatherers sheltered here.

  3. 3. Cueva del Niño

    Ayna is a village of white houses clinging to the walls of a deep gorge carved by the Mundo river, in the Sierra de Alcaraz in the southwest of Albacete province. Its setting is so dramatic that it is sometimes called the Little Switzerland of La Mancha, and it was the backdrop for the cult Spanish film Amanece, que no es poco, a surreal comedy shot here in the late 1980s. A few kilometres away, in a rocky ravine, the Cueva del Niño holds something rare for this part of Spain: Palaeolithic paintings of deer, ibex and horses, much older than the Levantine figures painted later on the same walls. It is part of the UNESCO listing of Rock Art of the Mediterranean Basin on the Iberian Peninsula, and access is restricted to protect the paintings.

  4. 4. Cavidad II

    South of Hellín the land opens into the dry, high steppe of southern Albacete, a country of esparto grass, almond terraces and bare limestone ridges, and it is in these ridges that the small painted cavities of the area hide. Cavidad II is one of them, a modest hollow in the rock with prehistoric pigment on its walls, part of a dense scatter of rock art sites around the Minateda area that archaeologists have studied for more than a century. Like most of the Levantine sites it is fragile and frequently protected by a grille, so it rewards patience and imagination more than a quick photograph.

  5. 5. Cueva del Peliciego

    Jumilla is wine. This town in the north of Murcia sits in a high, dry valley surrounded by bare mountains, and its vineyards, planted with the Monastrell grape, produce powerful red wines under the Jumilla designation of origin, one of the oldest in Spain. The Cueva del Peliciego, a rock shelter in the hills near the town, holds prehistoric paintings in the Levantine style, and it has been known since the early twentieth century, when it was one of the first rock art sites studied in the region.

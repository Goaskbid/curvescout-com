Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Ancient city of Ephesus

    Ephesus is the sort of place where the scale arrives slowly, one marble block at a time. It lies in the plain beside Selçuk, less than three kilometres from the town centre and about twelve from the resort of Kuşadası, and once stood on the sea. The Cayster river kept dropping silt until the harbour became fields, and the city that had been one of the great ports of the Roman province of Asia was left stranded a few kilometres inland.

  2. 2. Pythagoreion and Heraion of Samos

    Two things that the ancient Greeks did better than anyone are on show on this corner of Samos, and one of them is a tunnel. In the sixth century BC, the tyrant Polycrates ruled the island, and he commissioned an engineer from Megara, Eupalinos, to bring water to his capital through a mountain. The tunnel is about a kilometre long, and Eupalinos had his workers dig from both ends at once, meeting in the middle with an error of only a few metres, which is a stunning achievement for the time. Visitors can walk through a part of it today, in a hard hat, and the sloping channel for water still runs beside the walkway.

  3. 3. The Historic Centre

    Patmos is a small island, only about thirteen kilometres long, in the Dodecanese, and it is famous for one reason. Tradition holds that the apostle John was exiled here by the Roman authorities in the first century and received the visions recorded in the Book of Revelation in a cave on the slope below the town. The Cave of the Apocalypse, with its rock cleft where the voice of God was said to speak, is still a place of pilgrimage. Above it, the fortress-like Monastery of Saint John the Theologian was founded in 1088 by the monk Christodoulos, with a charter from the Byzantine emperor, and it rises over the white houses of Chora like a castle.

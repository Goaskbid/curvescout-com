Why this ride

From Yoshino-chō it runs 81 km to Yoshino-chō; allow about 1 h 24 of riding. The stops in between: Tachibana-dera Temple Site, Horyu-ji Area and back through Tachibana-dera Temple Site. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Yoshino and Ômine - Yoshimizu-jinja

    Yoshino is the most famous cherry-blossom mountain in Japan, and it earns the fame. Tens of thousands of trees, planted over centuries as offerings by pilgrims, climb the ridge in four zones, so the bloom moves up the mountain through April like a slow pink tide. But Yoshino is older and stranger than a flower festival. It is the gateway to the Kii mountains, a vast tangle of steep forested ranges where mountain ascetics of the Shugendo tradition have walked for more than a thousand years, and the pilgrimage path of Omine runs along the high ridges to Kumano, passing places so sacred that women were long banned from entering. The Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range were inscribed by UNESCO in 2004.

  2. 2. Tachibana-dera Temple Site

    Asuka is where Japan as a state began to take shape, and Tachibana-dera sits right in the middle of it, among rice paddies and low hills in a small valley south of Nara. Tradition holds that Prince Shōtoku, the regent who promoted Buddhism and wrote Japan's first constitution in the early seventh century, was born here, and the temple claims to have been founded by him. The present buildings are much later, but the site is ancient, and the atmosphere of the quiet temple grounds, with their wooden halls, gravel courtyards and a curious stone carved with two faces, one good and one evil, is very special.

  3. 3. Horyu-ji Area

    The buildings at Hōryū-ji are around thirteen hundred years old, and some of them are among the oldest wooden structures anywhere in the world. Founded in the early seventh century by Prince Shōtoku, a regent remembered as a champion of Buddhism and a founding figure of the Japanese state, the temple was rebuilt after a fire later in the same century, and parts of that rebuilding still stand. In 1993 it became one of the first sites in Japan to be listed by UNESCO, as the Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area.

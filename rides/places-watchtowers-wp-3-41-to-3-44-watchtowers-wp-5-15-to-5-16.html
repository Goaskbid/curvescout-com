Why this ride

227 km from Glashütten to Glashütten, about 3 h 31 of riding. On the way it passes Watchtowers WP 4/1 to 4/19a including fort, Fortsite and civil settlement of Echzell, Watchtowers WP 5/15 to 5/16 and back through Fortsite and civil settlement of Echzell. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

The roads

German Fairy Tale Route

It is 43 km from Fortsite and civil settlement of Echzell to Watchtowers WP 5/15 to 5/16, mostly on A 45. Hardly any climbing; it runs between 109 and 168 m. This is the bit the ride is built around.

The stops

  1. 1. Watchtowers WP 3/41 to 3/44

    Start here.

    The Taunus is the low, forested mountain range that closes in the north of the Frankfurt basin, and the Romans put their frontier along and over it. Here, near Glashütten, the line passes through high woodland at roughly four hundred metres above sea level, in an area that is cold and often foggy in winter. Towers 3/41 to 3/44 belong to the third section of the Upper German-Raetian Limes, the one that UNESCO recognised in 2005 together with the rest of the frontier. The Romans cleared a swathe through the forest, set up a palisade, and watched the open ground to the north, where tribes of the Chatti lived. The name Glashütten itself recalls glassmaking, once common in these forests because wood, sand and potash were all to hand.

    Read the complete Watchtowers WP 3/41 to 3/44 write-up.

  2. 2. Watchtowers WP 4/1 to 4/19a including fort

    27 km in.

    Kaiser Wilhelm II took a personal interest in the Roman frontier, and at the beginning of the twentieth century, he ordered the complete reconstruction of the Saalburg, a Roman fort on the crest of the Taunus near Bad Homburg. The result, with its walls, gates and buildings rebuilt on the original foundations, is the most complete reconstruction of a Roman fort in Germany and houses a museum of Roman life on the frontier. A few kilometres north-east, near Rosbach vor der Höhe, the ruins of the Kapersburg fort lie in the forest, and the watchtowers numbered WP 4/1 to 4/19a, on the section of the Limes running from the Taunus ridge toward the Wetterau, are part of the UNESCO listing of the Upper Germanic-Raetian Limes.

    Read the complete Watchtowers WP 4/1 to 4/19a including fort write-up.

  3. 3. Fortsite and civil settlement of Echzell

    70 km in.

    Echzell is a village of a few thousand people in the Wetterau, the broad, fertile basin north-east of Frankfurt, where loess soil has been farmed since the Stone Age and the horizon rarely rises above a gentle swell. The Romans liked it for exactly that reason. They built a fort at Echzell in the later first century as part of their push into the Wetterau, and a civil settlement grew up around it in the usual way, with traders, craftsmen and families outside the gates. The fort later became part of the Upper German-Raetian Limes, which UNESCO listed in 2005. Almost nothing of the walls stands now, and the outline lies mostly under fields and houses, so the Echzell stop is about reading a landscape rather than visiting a monument.

    Read the complete Fortsite and civil settlement of Echzell write-up.

  4. 4. Watchtowers WP 5/15 to 5/16

    113 km in.

    Great forts are rare on the Limes; most of the frontier was watchtowers, small timber and later stone posts within sight of each other, each with a handful of men and a fire for signalling. The towers numbered 5/15 and 5/16 stood near Großkrotzenburg, at the point where the land frontier coming down from the Wetterau met the Main, and from here southward the river itself became the border as far as Miltenberg. Little of them remains to see, but the location tells the story: the Romans used water where they could and built palisades only where they had to.

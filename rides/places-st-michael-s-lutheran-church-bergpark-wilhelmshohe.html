Why this ride

A 292 km round trip from Hildesheim - roughly 5 h 02 in the saddle. On the way it passes Fagus Factory in Alfeld, Carolingian Westwork and Civitas Corvey, Bergpark Wilhelmshöhe and back through Carolingian Westwork and Civitas Corvey. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. St Michael's Lutheran Church

    St Michael's in Hildesheim is a church that was built to a vision. Bishop Bernward, a teacher and craftsman as well as a churchman, began it around the year 1010 and died in 1022, and the church was consecrated in 1033. He designed it with a double apse, with a choir at each end, and with two transepts and six towers, a symmetrical plan that is one of the best examples of Ottonian architecture. The layout reflects a view of the world in which the church was a model of heaven.

  2. 2. Fagus Factory in Alfeld

    Stand across the railway tracks and look at the Fagus works, and you are looking at one of the buildings where modern architecture started. In 1911 the shoe-last maker Carl Benscheidt hired a young Walter Gropius and his partner Adolf Meyer to design a new factory in Alfeld, in the Leine valley of Lower Saxony. They gave it walls of glass that seem to wrap around the corners with no column to hold them, a radical trick at the time, and the result was so influential that it became a UNESCO site in 2011. The best part: it is still a working factory making shoe lasts, not a museum frozen in place.

  3. 3. Carolingian Westwork and Civitas Corvey

    Corvey sits in a loop of the Weser just outside Höxter, a baroque abbey palace set among meadows with the wooded hills of the Solling and the Weser Uplands closing in around it. The baroque part is handsome but not why people come. Hidden behind it, forming the entrance to the church, is a westwork built between 873 and 885, and it is one of the very few Carolingian buildings in the world still standing more or less as it was built.

  4. 4. Bergpark Wilhelmshöhe

    There is a giant on the hill above Kassel, and he has been there since the early eighteenth century. The Hercules statue, a copper figure on top of an octagonal structure and a huge stepped pyramid, crowns the Bergpark Wilhelmshöhe, which the Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel began laying out around 1700 as a statement of his power. Below it, a long cascade of steps and basins sends water tumbling down the hillside, and on certain afternoons from May to early October the water features run for the public in a spectacle that takes well over an hour to reach the bottom. UNESCO listed the park in 2013, and it remains one of the largest hillside parks in Europe.

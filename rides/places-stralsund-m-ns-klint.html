Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Stralsund

    Stralsund's skyline tells you at once that this was a rich merchant town. Red-brick Gothic gables, pointed spires and the enormous bulk of the Marienkirche, whose tower rises more than a hundred metres, crowd the old town island, which is surrounded by water and linked to the mainland by causeways. In the Middle Ages, Stralsund was one of the leading members of the Hanseatic League, the trading alliance that dominated the Baltic, and together with Wismar its old town is a World Heritage site. In the harbour, the three-masted Gorch Fock I, a sail training ship, is moored, and the Ozeaneum, a striking modern museum of sea life, stands beside it.

  2. 2. Jasmund

    White cliffs rise out of a dark beech forest on the north-east corner of Rügen, which is a strange thing to see in Germany. Jasmund is a peninsula of chalk, laid down in the Cretaceous sea some seventy million years ago and then pushed and folded by the ice that covered the Baltic coast. Its best-known point is the Königsstuhl, the King's Chair, where the cliffs reach more than a hundred metres over the water, and the scene was made famous by Caspar David Friedrich's painting of the chalk cliffs, in which small figures peer over the edge in a mixture of awe and fear.

  3. 3. Møns Klint

    The chalk cliffs of Møn rise out of the Baltic in a long white wall, with beech woods growing on top, and it is one of the few places in Denmark where the land stands up and makes an impression. At its highest the cliff is about 120 metres, and it runs for around six kilometres along the island's eastern coast. The chalk was laid down on the sea floor in the Cretaceous period, about 70 million years ago, from the skeletons of tiny marine organisms, and glaciers in the last Ice Age pushed it up and folded it into the shape you see.

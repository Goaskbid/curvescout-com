Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. The Babolovsky Park

    Inside a ruined palace in a quiet park south of Saint Petersburg sits one of the strangest objects in Russia: a gigantic bath carved from a single block of granite, more than five metres across, made in the early nineteenth century by the stonemason Samson Sukhanov for the Babolovsky palace. The palace, a small Gothic style residence built for Catherine the Great's favourite Prince Potemkin, was later rebuilt and then ruined during the Second World War, but the bath was too heavy to move and remains inside the shell of the building.

  2. 2. Fort «Kronshlot»

    Fort Kronshlot is a stone fortress sitting on an artificial island in the middle of the water, and it has been there, in one form or another, since 1704. Peter the Great ordered it built to guard the shallow channel to the new city of Saint Petersburg, and the first version was a wooden tower on piles driven into the seabed and filled with stone. The name comes from the German for crown castle. The current structure is mostly of the nineteenth century, a long oval of brick and granite with gun casemates facing the water.

  3. 3. The Road of Life

    On the south-eastern shore of Lake Ladoga, east of Saint Petersburg, a road ends at the water. This is the start of the Road of Life, the supply route across the ice that kept Leningrad alive during the siege of 1941 to 1944. The siege lasted 872 days, from September 1941 to January 1944, and it cut the city off from nearly everything except the lake. In the first winter, when Ladoga froze, trucks and horse-drawn sledges began to cross the ice with food and fuel, and on the return journey they carried out women, children and the sick. Lorries broke through the ice, and bombing and artillery attacked the route, but it kept going.

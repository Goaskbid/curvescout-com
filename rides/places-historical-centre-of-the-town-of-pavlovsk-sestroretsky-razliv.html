  1. 1. Historical Centre of the Town of Pavlovsk

    Pavlovsk was a gift. In 1777 Catherine the Great gave her son Paul, the future emperor, a stretch of forest and meadow south of Saint Petersburg, and he and his wife Maria Feodorovna turned it into one of the most elegant country residences in Russia. The Scottish architect Charles Cameron designed the palace, a pale yellow building with a shallow dome and a colonnade, and the park, which spreads over some six hundred hectares, was laid out in the English landscape manner, with winding paths, little bridges and pavilions half hidden among the trees. It is among the largest landscape parks in Europe, and walking in it on a quiet morning is a joy.

  2. 2. The ensemble of the Oreshek fortress

    Where the Neva leaves Lake Ladoga there is a small island in the current, and for seven centuries whoever held it controlled the river and the trade route to the Baltic. The Novgorodians built the first wooden fort here in 1323 and called it Oreshek, the Little Nut, because of the island's shape. The Swedes took it in 1611 and renamed it Nöteborg, and in 1702 Peter the Great stormed it, and renamed it Schlüsselburg, the Key Fortress. The old stone walls and towers stand now on a low island reached by a short boat ride from the town, which is now called Shlisselburg.

  3. 3. Sestroretsky Razliv

    Sestroretsk is a small town on the north shore of the Gulf of Finland, in a landscape of pine-covered dunes, shallow bays and lakes, and it has an industrial origin that surprises visitors. In 1721 Peter the Great founded an arms factory on the Sestra river here, and a dam created a broad reservoir to power it. That reservoir, Razliv, which means flood or spill, is the lake that gives the place its name. The water, ringed by reeds and birches, is a favourite spot for locals to swim, fish and walk.

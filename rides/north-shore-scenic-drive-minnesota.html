Why this ride

From Duluth it runs 536 km to the finish; allow about 7 h 15 of riding. On the way it passes Two Harbors, Gooseberry Falls, Split Rock Lighthouse and Grand Marais. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Duluth

    Duluth is built on a hillside so steep that the streets look as if they were drawn by someone who had never seen a slope. The city climbs from the western tip of Lake Superior, the largest freshwater lake in the world by area, up a long ridge that gives a view of the harbour, the ore docks and the gleaming water from nearly every neighbourhood. At the foot of the hill, in Canal Park, the Aerial Lift Bridge rises to let ocean-going freighters pass in and out of the harbour, and the sound of its horn is as much a part of the city as the gulls. Tourists crowd the pier to watch the ships come in, some of them a thousand feet long.

  2. 2. Two Harbors

    Two Harbors exists because of iron. When the Mesabi Range in northern Minnesota proved to hold some of the richest ore deposits in the world, the mining companies needed a way to get it to the steel mills of the lower Great Lakes, and a railway from the mines to Agate Bay on Lake Superior, completed in the 1880s, made the little port the first ore-shipping harbour in the state. The huge ore docks still stand on the waterfront, long black steel structures from which the red ore was loaded into the holds of lake freighters, and you can watch the great ships come and go. The town has about three and a half thousand people and is the first real stop on the North Shore after Duluth, which lies roughly forty kilometres south-west.

  3. 3. Gooseberry Falls

    Gooseberry Falls is where the Gooseberry River tumbles over the edge of an ancient lava plateau on its way to Lake Superior, and it is the first big waterfall you meet on the North Shore. The river drops in three stages, the Upper, Middle and Lower Falls, over steps of dark basalt, and the water is stained the colour of tea by the tannins of the forest. The rock here is the product of an extraordinary event. About 1.1 billion years ago, the continent began to split along a rift that ran from what is now Lake Superior south-west toward Kansas, and enormous volumes of lava poured out, forming thick layers of basalt. The rift failed, but the rock remained, and the lake now fills the depression it left behind.

  4. 4. Split Rock Lighthouse

    Split Rock Lighthouse stands on a cliff of dark rock one hundred and thirty feet above the lake, and it is one of the best-known landmarks on the Great Lakes. It exists because of a storm. In November 1905 a ferocious gale swept across Lake Superior and wrecked or damaged some thirty ships, and the shipping companies, after counting the cost, demanded a beacon on this treacherous, rocky shore. The light was first lit in 1910, and for nearly sixty years it guided ore carriers along the coast, until modern navigation made it redundant and it was switched off in 1969. Today it is a state historic site, and visitors can climb the tower, walk through the keeper's house and see the Fresnel lens.

  5. 5. Grand Marais

    Grand Marais is where the road runs out of tame country. The little harbour town, tucked into a natural bay on the Lake Superior shore with the pine-covered hills behind it, is the end of the North Shore Scenic Drive for most visitors, and it feels like an outpost, a place that has made peace with long winters and short summers. The name is French, and means great marsh, though no marsh is evident, and the town was first a fur-trading and fishing post. The population is about thirteen hundred, but in July it is full of tourists, painters, canoeists and walkers, all drawn by the wild country to the north.

  6. 6. Grand Portage

    Grand Portage is the far north-eastern tip of Minnesota, where the highway runs out of country against the Canadian border and the Pigeon River. The name is literal. For more than a century, the French and then the British traders who dominated the fur trade carried their canoes and bales around the impassable rapids and falls of the Pigeon River along an eight-and-a-half-mile trail, which they called the Grand Portage, or great carrying place. In the 1780s the North West Company built a depot on the shore of Lake Superior, where canoes from Montreal met those from the interior, and every summer there was a rendezvous with trading, feasting and bargaining.

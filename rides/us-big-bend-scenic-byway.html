  1. 1. Tallahassee

    Tallahassee became the capital of Florida in 1824 for a reason that had nothing to do with beauty: it lay roughly halfway between the two old colonial towns of Pensacola and St. Augustine, which made it a fair meeting point for legislators travelling by horseback. The result is a capital that feels a world away from beach Florida. It sits among red clay hills and pine woods in the Panhandle, closer in spirit to Georgia than to Miami, and its population of nearly two hundred thousand is dominated by government workers and students from Florida State and Florida A&M universities.

  2. 2. Apalachicola

    For a long time, if you ate an oyster in the southern United States, there was a fair chance it came from Apalachicola Bay. The shallow, brackish bay, where the Apalachicola River meets the Gulf of Mexico, produced the great majority of Florida's oysters, and the town of about two thousand people lived by them. Then the reefs collapsed, after years of drought, reduced river flow and overharvesting, and in 2020 the state closed the wild oyster harvest for five years. The oyster boats and the shucking houses are fewer now, and the town is working out what comes next, but the seafood restaurants still serve shrimp, fish and farmed oysters.

  3. 3. Carrabelle

    Carrabelle claims to have the world's smallest police station, and it is not a joke: for years the local police took calls from a telephone booth on the main road, and the booth is still there, now a roadside curiosity where people stop for a photograph. That sense of humour suits the place, a fishing town of a few thousand people at the mouth of the Carrabelle River on the Gulf coast, with shrimp boats tied up at the docks and a slow, unpretentious pace.

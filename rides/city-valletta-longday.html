Why this ride

A 398 km round trip from Valletta - roughly 6 h 52 in the saddle. On the way it passes Scicli, Modica, Ragusa and Late Baroque Towns of the Val di Noto. It crosses from Malta into Italy - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Valletta

    Valletta is smaller than most people expect, a city on a peninsula, built on a grid and finished almost in one go. It is named for Jean de Valette, the Grand Master of the Knights of St John, who led the defence of Malta during the Great Siege of 1565, when an Ottoman fleet landed and fought for months before withdrawing. Afterwards the Knights decided to build a new fortified capital on the bare ridge between two deep harbours, and construction began in 1566. The streets run straight, sloping down to the water in steps, and bastions rise from the sea. The whole city is on the UNESCO list, and it was a European Capital of Culture in 2018.

  2. 2. Scicli

    In January 1693 an earthquake flattened much of south-eastern Sicily, and the towns that rose from the rubble were rebuilt in a confident, theatrical late baroque style. Scicli is one of the eight towns of the Val di Noto listed by UNESCO for that reason, and it may be the most charming. It sits at the meeting of three limestone gorges, with a rocky hill above crowned by the ruins of the old church of San Matteo, and a centre of honey-coloured churches and palaces whose balconies are held up by carved grotesque faces, horses, sirens and monsters.

  3. 3. Modica

    Modica was destroyed in 1693, rebuilt in a hurry, and the result is one of the most dramatic baroque towns in Europe. A great earthquake that January killed tens of thousands across south-eastern Sicily, and the towns of the Val di Noto rose again in a new style, with curved facades, carved balconies and grotesque stone faces looking down from the corners. UNESCO listed eight of them together in 2002, and Modica is one of the finest. It occupies two steep ridges and the gorge between them, and the houses climb up the sides in layers, so that a walk through the streets is mostly a matter of steps.

  4. 4. Ragusa

    In 1693 a terrible earthquake destroyed much of south-eastern Sicily, and the towns of the Val di Noto were rebuilt in the baroque style of the time, creating a group of towns of extraordinary beauty, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Ragusa is one of them, a town divided into two parts: Ragusa Superiore, the newer town on the ridge, built after the earthquake, and Ragusa Ibla, the old town on a lower hill, rebuilt on its medieval street plan.

  5. 5. Late Baroque Towns of the Val di Noto

    On 11 January 1693, a catastrophic earthquake struck southeastern Sicily, destroying dozens of towns and killing tens of thousands of people. What happened next is one of the great chapters in the history of town planning. Noto, which stood on a hill some kilometres away, was abandoned, and a new town was built from scratch on a gentler slope, with a grid of streets, grand stairways, churches and palaces, all in the soft, honey-coloured local limestone that glows gold and pink at sunset. Together with seven other towns rebuilt after the earthquake, including Ragusa, Modica and Scicli, Noto was listed by UNESCO in 2002 as the Late Baroque Towns of the Val di Noto.

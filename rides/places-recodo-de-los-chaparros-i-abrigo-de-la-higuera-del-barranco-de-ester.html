Why this ride

From Albalate del Arzobispo it runs 73 km to Albalate del Arzobispo; allow about 1 h 16 of riding. On the way it passes Covacho Ahumado, Abrigo de la Higuera del Barranco de Ester and back through Covacho Ahumado. The highest point is about 1,100 m. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Recodo de los Chaparros I

    There is a moment on the road to Albalate del Arzobispo when the plateau seems to open and a river gorge appears below, and that is the valley of the Martín, one of the hidden corners of Teruel. The river has cut a deep, winding canyon through the limestone of the Bajo Aragón, and the villages that line it, Albalate, Ariño, Alloza, have been farming and mining here for centuries. The Recodo de los Chaparros, the bend of the holm oaks, is a shelter in this country, and it belongs to the group that makes the Río Martín one of Aragon's cultural parks.

  2. 2. Covacho Ahumado

    The name means the smoky little shelter, and it fits: a modest overhang darkened by soot, one of the rock art sites around Alacón in the Río Martín Cultural Park in Teruel province. The Martín river has carved canyons through limestone here, and its walls hold dozens of painted panels, from naturalistic Levantine hunting scenes to the later schematic style of stick figures and symbols. Alacón alone has several groups of shelters, and the park has an interpretation centre in the area to explain them.

  3. 3. Abrigo de la Higuera del Barranco de Ester

    The Río Martín country of Teruel is a place of deep gorges and bare, honey-coloured hills, and Oliete and Estercuel are two of its tiny villages. The Abrigo de la Higuera del Barranco de Ester is a small shelter in a side ravine, named for the fig tree that grows at its mouth, and it holds paintings that belong to the rock art of the Mediterranean Basin, as protected by UNESCO. The pictures include the small red figures of the Levantine tradition, and the setting, under an overhang of golden rock, is a good place to sit and think about how few things have changed in the landscape.

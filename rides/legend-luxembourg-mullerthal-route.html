Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Luxembourg

    Luxembourg City is a fortress town built on a rock, and it has the sort of dramatic geography that makes you stop the bike and look. The old town sits on a sandstone promontory surrounded on three sides by the deep gorges of the Alzette and Petrusse rivers, so that the walls rise straight from the valley floor. For centuries it was one of the strongest fortresses in Europe, fought over by Spanish, French, Austrian, Prussian and Dutch armies, and its tangle of tunnels, the Bock Casemates, ran for kilometres inside the cliff. Much of it was dismantled after 1867, but enough survives to be walked. The old quarter and the fortifications are on the UNESCO list.

  2. 2. Larochette

    Larochette sits in a narrow, wooded valley in the middle of Luxembourg, under a huge sandstone crag with a ruined castle perched on top. The Luxembourgish name is Fiels, meaning rock, and the rock is the point. The castle was built in the Middle Ages and developed over the centuries by local lords, then abandoned in the sixteenth century and left to the ivy, and its walls still stand, with a few towers and halls open to visitors. The town below is small and pleasant, with a main square, old houses along the White Ernz stream, cafes and a long history of textile making.

  4. 4. Echternach

    Echternach claims to be the oldest town in Luxembourg, and its story begins in the year 698, when the Northumbrian missionary Willibrord received land here from a local noblewoman and founded an abbey beside the river Sure. He had come from England to convert the Frisians, and the abbey that grew up around his tomb became one of the great centres of learning of the early Middle Ages, famous for its illuminated manuscripts. The basilica, rebuilt after heavy damage in the Second World War, still holds his sarcophagus in the crypt, and pilgrims come to it every year.

  5. 5. Vianden

    Vianden is one of those towns that looks like a scene from a children's book, and the castle is the reason. It stands on a rocky spur above the Our River, in the north-east of Luxembourg, a pile of towers, halls and chapels that grew over several centuries from the eleventh to the fourteenth, and was once the seat of the counts of Vianden. It fell into ruin in the nineteenth century and was restored from the 1970s, and today you can walk through its great halls, kitchens and armouries with a very good sense of how a medieval noble household worked.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Paysage et ensemble miniers du secteur d’A

    Coal made this corner of northern France, and for a century and a half it made it black. The mining landscape listed by UNESCO runs for a hundred and twenty kilometres across the old Nord and Pas-de-Calais coalfield, and the sector at this stop lies in the Valenciennes end of it, close to the Belgian frontier and the river Escaut, which the Belgians call the Scheldt. The pits of Anzin, the company that started the whole business in the middle of the eighteenth century, were the first great mines in France.

  2. 2. Fosse Delloye

    The name on the label belongs to the most famous pit in the whole northern coalfield. Fosse Delloye, at Lewarde a short ride west of here, closed in 1971 and became the Centre Historique Minier, France's largest mining museum, where former miners once guided visitors through reconstructed galleries and the old lamp rooms, showers and machine halls still stand as they were left. The point itself, though, sits beside Denain on the Escaut, and Denain deserves a story of its own.

  3. 3. Cités de la Solitude

    On the edge of Waziers, near Douai, the cités de la Solitude are rows of brick houses built by a mining company for its workers, laid out in neat streets with small gardens. Such company towns, the cités minières, were the backbone of life in the coal basin of northern France, providing housing, schools, churches and shops for miners and their families, and they are among the most important elements of the Nord-Pas-de-Calais Mining Basin, listed by UNESCO in 2012. The name Solitude seems almost ironic for a place built to house so many people close together.

  4. 4. Paysage et ensemble miniers d’Estevelles e

    Estevelles is a small town where the mining basin meets the waterways of the Deûle, between Pont-à-Vendin and Carvin. The label points to one of the mining ensembles of the area, part of the UNESCO-listed basin: a pit, a cité, perhaps a spoil heap, all together telling the story of how coal shaped every aspect of local life. The mining companies built not only houses but churches, schools, health centres and gardens, controlling their workers' lives from cradle to grave. The cités around here, with their neat rows of brick houses and small vegetable gardens, still house many descendants of miners.

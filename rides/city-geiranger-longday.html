Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Geiranger

    The Geirangerfjord is a narrow cut, around fifteen kilometres long, between cliffs that rise over a thousand metres from the water, and it is among the best-known sights in Norway. The village at its head, Geiranger, is tiny, with only a few hundred residents, but in summer, cruise ships anchor in the fjord one after another and the pier fills with people. UNESCO listed the fjord in 2005, along with Naeroyfjord, and a short walk along the shore brings you to the base of the Seven Sisters waterfall, which pours straight into the fjord from a high ledge.

  2. 2. Urnes Stave Church

    The oldest stave church in Norway still standing stands on a ledge above a fjord, and you reach it by ferry. Urnes is on the north shore of Lustrafjord, the innermost arm of the great Sognefjord, and a small ferry crosses from Solvorn on the opposite shore in a few minutes, running regularly in the summer season. From the landing you walk up a path through farm fields to the church, which dates from around 1130 and is made of pine timber, black with tar, its shingled roofs layered up like a pagoda. Urnes was listed by UNESCO in 1979, among the first sites to be inscribed.

  4. 4. Nærøyfjord Area

    The Nærøyfjord is narrow in a way that photographs struggle to show. Branching from the Aurlandsfjord, itself an arm of the great Sognefjord, it squeezes to about 250 metres across at its tightest, with mountain walls that climb to nearly 1,800 metres on either side and cast the water in shadow for much of the day. UNESCO listed it together with Geirangerfjord in 2005, and the setting at Gudvangen at its head is dramatic even by Norwegian standards, with waterfalls dropping from hanging valleys and snow patches lingering into summer.

Why this ride

All-American Road. North Cascades, alpine lakes, high passes. 160 km from Sedro-Woolley to the finish, about 2 h 55 of riding. The highest point is about 1,650 m. Washington Pass is subject to seasonal closure - check it is open before you set off. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Sedro-Woolley

    Sedro-Woolley is two towns that stuck together. Sedro and Woolley were separate rivals on the Skagit River until they merged in 1898, and the double-barrelled name, with its hyphen, has stayed ever since. It is a working town of about eleven thousand people on the flat, green floodplain of the Skagit valley, in north-western Washington, with farms, sawmills, a timber heritage and a loggers' festival in July. For riders it is the start of the North Cascades Highway, and the last easy place to buy supplies.

  2. 2. North Cascades Scenic Highway point 2

    The first time you see Diablo Lake from the overlook on State Route 20, you will probably assume someone has tinted the photo. The water is an intense, milky turquoise, and it sits in a deep valley ringed by steep, forested slopes and jagged peaks streaked with snow and ice. The colour comes from glacial flour, fine rock dust ground by the glaciers above and carried down by streams. It reflects green and blue light, giving the lake its extraordinary hue.

  3. 3. North Cascades Scenic Highway point 3

    Washington Pass, at 1,669 metres, is the high point of the North Cascades Highway, and the view from the overlook is one of the finest in the Pacific Northwest. A short paved path leads to a viewpoint perched on a rocky outcrop, from where you look straight up at the sheer granite towers of Liberty Bell Mountain and the Early Winters Spires, rising more than a thousand metres above the road. Below, the highway makes a tight hairpin around the base of the cliffs and drops away into the valley of Early Winters Creek.

  4. 4. North Cascades Scenic Highway point 4

    Winthrop looks like a film set, and in a way it is. In the 1970s, when the North Cascades Highway was about to open and bring tourists through the Methow Valley, local business owners decided to give the town a Wild West makeover, with false-front wooden buildings, boardwalks and hitching posts. The idea worked, and today Winthrop is a lively little town full of cafés, saloons, outfitters and gift shops, with a real sense of fun. The town has a genuine frontier history, too, and the novelist Owen Wister is said to have drawn on stories from the valley when he wrote The Virginian, one of the first great Western novels.

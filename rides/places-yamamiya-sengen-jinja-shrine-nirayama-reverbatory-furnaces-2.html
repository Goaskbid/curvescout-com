Why this ride

From Fujinomiya it runs 211 km to Fujinomiya; allow about 3 h 38 of riding. It goes by way of “Oshi” Lodging House, Nirayama Reverbatory Furnaces and back through “Oshi” Lodging House. The highest point is about 900 m. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Yamamiya Sengen-jinja Shrine

    Fuji does not need an introduction, but the shrines around its foot deserve one. Yamamiya Sengen-jinja stands on the southwest side of the mountain at the edge of Fujinomiya, in Shizuoka Prefecture, and it is one of the old shrines dedicated to Konohanasakuya-hime, the blossoming princess who is the deity of Mount Fuji, and her cult spread to the whole country as the Sengen shrines. The Yamamiya precinct is a calm, cedar-shaded compound where the mountain presides from behind, and the main buildings stand in the old Edo-period style, with dark wood and copper roofing, and the mood of a place that has served pilgrims for centuries.

  2. 2. “Oshi” Lodging House

    Mount Fuji has been a place of pilgrimage for a thousand years, and the lodging houses in Fujiyoshida are what the pilgrims used. Believers in the Fuji cult, called the Fuji-kō, travelled in confraternities from Edo and farther away, and when they reached the foot of the mountain they stayed with an oshi, a guide and priest who arranged their prayers, their meals and their climb. The streets of old Kamiyoshida still have a few of these houses, with deep entrances, long rooms and a small shrine inside, designed for groups to eat, sleep and prepare. UNESCO lists one of them as part of the Fujisan cultural landscape.

  3. 3. Nirayama Reverbatory Furnaces

    Two pairs of tall brick chimneys stand in a quiet suburb at the base of the Izu Peninsula, looking strangely European against the green hills. They belong to the Nirayama reverberatory furnace, built in the 1850s to melt iron for casting cannons, at a time when Japan felt threatened by Western warships arriving in its waters. The project was led by Egawa Hidetatsu, the local magistrate, a forward-thinking official who studied Western military technology from Dutch books. He died before it was completed, and his son finished the work. It is the only surviving reverberatory furnace of its era in Japan that was actually used for casting, and since 2015 it has been part of the UNESCO site of the Sites of Japan's Meiji Industrial Revolution.

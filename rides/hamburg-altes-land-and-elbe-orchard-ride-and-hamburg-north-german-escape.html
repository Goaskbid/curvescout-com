Why this ride

A one-way ride of 609 km, Ottensen to Neu Wulmstorf - roughly 10 h 44 in the saddle. On the way it passes Jork orchard roads, Stade old town, Zollenspieker dike stop and Finkenwerder. Rinteln Weser bends is the high point. It keeps to quiet secondary roads.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Finkenwerder ferry feel

    Some rides begin in a car park, and some begin on a boat. The ferry across the Elbe from the Landungsbrücken to Finkenwerder is one of the better openings to a day on the road, because for the price of a public transport ticket you get a half-hour cruise through the biggest port in Germany. The passenger ferries run out past the great cranes, the dry docks and the container terminals, with huge ships sliding by at what feels like arm's length, and the smell of diesel, salt and wet rope. If you are starting from the other side, the feeling is the same in reverse.

  2. 2. Jork orchard roads

    The roads of the Altes Land are organised by water, and in Jork you can see how. The little river Este runs through the village and out toward the Elbe, and parallel dike roads follow it on both banks, with farms strung along them like beads on a thread. The orchards lie behind the farms in long, narrow strips, and a network of drainage ditches runs between them, each one marked by a footbridge to a field. It is the ordered result of eight centuries of drainage by Dutch settlers, and the pattern has barely changed.

  3. 3. Stade old town food

    Stade's old town is built around a harbour, and the harbour is built around a tidal creek, the Schwinge, which loops into the centre and ends in a basin lined with merchants' houses. Brick gables, a wooden treadwheel crane on the quay and cobbles polished smooth by centuries of cartwheels make the Hansehafen look like something from a Dutch painting. In the Middle Ages Stade was one of the main ports on the Elbe, a rival of Hamburg, and its prosperity shows in the old buildings. The place went quiet when the river silted up, and the quiet saved it.

  4. 4. Zollenspieker dike stop

    Zollenspieker is a place where a river crossing has been important for a very long time. The name comes from the old customs post, the Zoll, that stood here to tax traffic crossing the Elbe, and the little ferry that carries cars, bikes and walkers across to Hoopte on the south bank still runs, a short, unhurried journey of a few minutes. A half-timbered inn stands beside the landing stage, one of the oldest in the region, with a thatched roof, low beams and a terrace facing the river. It is a place where Hamburgers come for coffee and cake on fine Sundays.

  5. 5. Back to Finkenwerder

    Finkenwerder is an island that is not quite an island, a long, low spit of land in the Elbe that belongs to Hamburg and feels like a village. For centuries its people were fishermen, who sailed out into the North Sea in their sturdy boats and returned to a row of brick houses with thatched roofs, and the old quarter still has a sleepy, nautical atmosphere. The writer Johann Kinau, who published under the name Gorch Fock and wrote tales of the sea, was born here, and the famous German sail-training ship was named after him.

  6. 6. Hamburg south-west edge

    Hamburg does not end so much as thin out, and this is where it happens. Out here on the south-west edge, between the Elbe and the heath, the port's industrial yards give way to a landscape of commuter villages, motorway interchanges, fields and low wooded hills, and the town of Neu Wulmstorf, with about twenty thousand people, is the nearest place of any size. Behind you lies Harburg, once a separate town on the south bank of the river, and ahead is the flat green country of Lower Saxony.

  7. 7. Rotenburg fuel gate

    Rotenburg an der Wümme is a quiet district town of about twenty-two thousand people on the river Wümme, halfway between Hamburg and Bremen, and a convenient place to fill the tank on the way south. The landscape here is typical of northern Lower Saxony: flat or gently rolling, with heath and pine, wet meadows and the dark, boggy ground of the moors. Everything is green, and the sky is large.

  8. 8. Nienburg river road

    With some thirty thousand people, it is a proper town with fuel, supermarkets and cafes, and with a pleasant, slightly sleepy old centre of brick and half-timber. The river here is broad and slow, lined with willows and meadows, and it makes a good place for a stop and a flask of coffee.

  9. 9. Rinteln Weser bends

    Rinteln is a small Weser town of about twenty-eight thousand people, set where the river swings in a series of wide bends between the hills of the Weserbergland. The old town is full of half-timbered houses and handsome buildings in the Weser Renaissance style, a distinctive form of gables, bay windows and decorated stonework that spread along the river in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

  10. 10. Hameln food stop

    Everyone knows the story, and Hameln has not tried to hide it. According to the legend, in 1284 the town was overrun by rats, and a stranger in colourful clothes offered to rid it of them in return for payment. He played his pipe, the rats followed him into the Weser and drowned, and the town refused to pay. So he played again, and this time the town's children followed him out of the gate and were never seen again. The Brothers Grimm wrote it down, Robert Browning wrote a poem, and the Pied Piper became one of the best-known figures in European folklore. The historical record does record some tragedy in 1284, though what actually happened is still a mystery.

  11. 11. Celle return reset

    Celle is a town that looks like it has been preserved in a jar. More than four hundred half-timbered houses, with carved beams, painted inscriptions and crooked upper storeys, line the streets of the old centre, and the whole place has survived with very little damage because it escaped bombing in the Second World War. About seventy-one thousand people live here, on the banks of the Aller, at the southern edge of the Luneburg Heath.

  12. 12. Back to Hamburg

    Hamburg's high ground is not very high. The highest point in the whole city, a wooded hill called the Hasselbrack, reaches only about 116 metres, and it lies in the Harburg Hills, just a few kilometres from this point on the southern edge of the city. Still, in a country as flat as north Germany, those hills are a real change of scene, and the road that runs through them is one of the best for riders on the south bank of the Elbe.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hurez - Dealul Prislop 7

    To understand the little hills around Hurez, you have to go back to the winter of 101. The emperor Trajan marched into Dacia with legions, and the king, Decebalus, resisted fiercely from his mountain strongholds in the south, until the Romans broke through in 102 and again in 105 and 106. Trajan's column in Rome shows the soldiers building roads, bridges and forts. The new province brought gold and salt to the empire, and in the north, a line of watch posts and forts along the Meses hills defended its edge against the free Dacians and others beyond.

  2. 2. Trezenea - Gura Teghișului

    The Meseș mountains are a low, forested ridge in northwestern Transylvania, and for the Romans they formed a natural wall at the edge of the province of Dacia. Along the ridge, they built a chain of forts, watchtowers and earthworks, and the big fort of Porolissum, near Zalău, was the key to it all, guarding a pass through the mountains used by traders and travellers. In 2024, much of this frontier was listed by UNESCO as part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire – Dacia, and the site between Treznea and Gura Teghișului is one of its components. On the ground, there is little more than a line of ditches, banks and the foundations of towers, hidden in the forest.

  3. 3. Mirșid - La Strâmtură 2-Pârâul Lupilor

    Roman Dacia's north-western frontier ran along the crest of the Meseș mountains, and the hill country around Mirșid, east of Zalău, is laced with its traces: watchtowers, earthworks and small forts placed so that each could see the next. In 2024 these remains became part of the World Heritage listing for the Frontiers of the Roman Empire in Dacia. The name of the spot, La Strâmtură by the wolves' brook, gives a fair idea of its character: a narrow place in the hills, wooded and quiet, where the eye has to work to pick out a low mound or a ditch in the grass.

Why this ride

A 348 km round trip from Ōdachō-ōda - roughly 6 h in the saddle. On the way it passes Hiroshima Peace Memorial, Itsukushima Shinto Shrine, back through Hiroshima Peace Memorial and back through Itsukushima Shinto Shrine. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Iwami Ginzan Silver Mine and its Cultural

    Under the forest that covers Iwami Ginzan is one of the great silver mines of the world, and it is a kind of miracle that you can hardly see it. The silver was dug here from the 1520s to 1923, in narrow hand-cut tunnels, and the district grew into a complex of mine, refineries, a mining town, roads and harbours, all of which were listed by UNESCO in 2007 as a single cultural landscape. What the committee praised was the lightness of the touch. Unlike so many mining regions, this one was not ripped apart, and the forest simply grew back.

  2. 2. Hiroshima Peace Memorial

    The skeleton of a dome against the sky, the twisted steel frame of what was once an elegant exhibition hall, is one of the most recognisable images of the twentieth century. On 6 August 1945, the atomic bomb exploded almost directly above this building in central Hiroshima. Almost everything around it was destroyed, but part of its walls and dome remained, and after long debate the city chose to preserve the ruin exactly as it was. It was listed by UNESCO in 1996 as the Hiroshima Peace Memorial, also known as the Genbaku Dome.

  3. 3. Itsukushima Shinto Shrine

    The great orange torii gate of Itsukushima, standing in the sea off the island of Miyajima, is one of the most famous images of Japan. At high tide, it seems to float on the water; at low tide, you can walk out across the sand to its feet. The shrine behind it is built on piers over the bay, a series of halls and corridors painted vermilion and white, connected by boardwalks, so that it too seems to float when the tide is in. A shrine has stood here for well over a thousand years, and its present form owes much to Taira no Kiyomori, the powerful warlord who rebuilt it in the twelfth century. The island itself has long been considered sacred, so much so that in the past, births and deaths were not permitted on it. The shrine was listed by UNESCO in 1996, and the torii was restored a few years ago after a long period under scaffolding.

Peru Coastal Panamericana South

Why this ride

A one-way ride of 1832 km, Lima to Arequipa - roughly 24 h 48 in the saddle. On the way it passes Paracas, Huacachina, Nazca and Camana. The highest point is about 3,600 m. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Lima

    Lima sits on the edge of one of the driest deserts on earth, and yet it is a city of more than seven million people, held together by a grey sky that locals call la garúa, a damp, low cloud that hangs over the coast for much of the year and rarely produces rain. The city's coastline rises in cliffs above the Pacific, where the districts of Miraflores and Barranco look out to the surf, and parks, cafes and paragliders occupy the clifftops.

  2. 2. Paracas

    The Paracas peninsula juts into the Pacific from the Peruvian coast, a dry, red-and-ochre tongue of sand and rock where no rain falls for years, and it is as strange and beautiful a place as any on the Panamericana. The Paracas National Reserve covers a huge area of this coast and sea, with dramatic cliffs, empty beaches, and a Red Beach whose sand takes its colour from volcanic rock.

  3. 3. Huacachina

    Huacachina is the kind of place that looks like a joke the Peruvian desert is playing on you. A small lagoon, ringed with palms and old-fashioned lamp posts, sits in a bowl of enormous sand dunes five kilometres from the city of Ica. Local legend says it formed when a princess, bathing, saw a hunter, dropped her mirror and fled; the mirror became the lake, and her robe the dunes. The truth is rather drier, a natural spring that has been dwindling for decades as water is pumped out for farms nearby.

  4. 4. Nazca

    Nazca sits in the middle of one of the driest places on earth, a small desert town of about twenty-four thousand people on the Pan-American Highway, famous for lines drawn in the ground that nobody quite agrees on. The Nazca Lines, a collection of enormous figures and straight lines scratched into the stony plain, were made between about two thousand and fifteen hundred years ago by removing dark surface pebbles to expose the pale ground beneath. Birds, a monkey, a spider, a hummingbird: they were drawn on a scale that can only be appreciated from the air, and in 1994 they were listed as a World Heritage site.

  5. 5. Camana

    Camaná is a fishing and farming town at the mouth of a river that actually reaches the sea, which in this part of Peru is a minor miracle. South of Lima, the Pacific coast is one of the driest deserts in the world, a belt of sand and bare rock that goes on for a thousand kilometres, and only a handful of rivers carry enough water down from the Andes to make a green valley. The Camaná is one of them, and the ribbon of rice fields, orchards and reeds around the town is startling after hours of grey and yellow. The town has about sixteen thousand people, with beaches nearby, and it is a favourite summer holiday spot for families from Arequipa, who drive down the long road from the mountains.

  6. 6. Arequipa

    Arequipa announces itself from a distance with a volcano. El Misti, a near-perfect cone of about 5,800 metres, stands behind the city, flanked by the broader mass of Chachani and the older, eroded Pichu Pichu, and on a clear morning the three seem to hang above the rooftops. Arequipa is Peru's second city, home to well over eight hundred thousand people, sitting in a dry valley at roughly 2,300 metres, so you arrive here after a long climb from the coast with your lungs and your engine both a little short of breath.

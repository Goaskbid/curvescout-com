Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cyamudongo Natural Forest

    In the far southwest corner of Rwanda, the land drops toward Lake Kivu and the Ruzizi river, and the green hills here are mostly tea, banana and small farms. Cyamudongo is the exception: a patch of natural forest, a few hundred hectares at most, standing as an island of tall trees in the middle of cultivation. It is separated from the great Nyungwe forest by a belt of farmland, yet it still shelters a small population of chimpanzees, along with other primates and a remarkable range of birds and butterflies.

  2. 2. Nyungwe National Park

    Nyungwe is one of the oldest rainforests in Africa, a mountain forest spread over the ridges of south-western Rwanda between Lake Kivu and the Burundi border. It is high, wet and green in a way that makes the rest of the world feel dry: tree ferns taller than a house, orchids on the branches, mist pouring through the valleys in the morning, and the sound of water everywhere. The ridges here form part of the great divide between the Congo and Nile basins, and Rwanda proudly claims one of the remotest sources of the Nile in these hills.

  3. 3. Gisakura Natural Forest

    In the far southwest of Rwanda, a small patch of forest surrounded by tea plantations is home to one of the most charming wildlife encounters in Africa. Gisakura forest lies on the western edge of Nyungwe National Park, near the tea estate that shares its name, and a large troop of Ruwenzori colobus monkeys lives there, black and white with long tails and flowing capes of fur. Watching them leap through the canopy, sometimes in groups of dozens, is a joy. The forest is a remnant of the great montane rainforest that once covered these mountains, isolated now by the tea fields.

  4. 4. Nyungwe National Forest

    Nyungwe is one of the oldest rainforests in Africa and one of the wettest, a block of mountain forest in the south-west of Rwanda that survived the ice ages when so much of the continent dried out. It holds around a thousand square kilometres of dense growth on steep ridges between about 1,600 and 3,000 metres, and it shelters thirteen species of primate, including chimpanzees, L'Hoest's monkeys and large troops of black-and-white Angolan colobus that move through the canopy in loud, tumbling groups. Birdlife is exceptional, with over three hundred species, many found only in the Albertine Rift. Some of the headwaters of the Nile are said to start in its streams.

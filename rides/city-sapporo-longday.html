Why this ride

387 km from Sapporo to Sapporo, about 6 h 40 of riding. It goes by way of Takasago Burial Site, Irie Site, Kitakogane Site and Ofune Site. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Sapporo

    Sapporo is a young city by Japanese standards. Until 1869 it was an empty plain at the foot of the mountains, and then the Meiji government, wanting to settle and develop Hokkaido, laid out a grid of streets in the American manner and called the project the Kaitakushi, the Development Commission. Today it is a metropolis of nearly two million, the fifth largest in Japan, and the only one with wide boulevards, a broad central park and a cheerfully northern attitude.

  2. 2. Takasago Burial Site

    The Takasago burial site lies in the Date area on the shore of Funka Bay, in southwestern Hokkaido, and it belongs to the Jomon period, when people in these northern lands buried their dead with care and ceremony. Excavations turned up graves with ornaments of shell and other goods, which suggests a settled community with trading links. The site is part of the northern Jomon group that earned UNESCO listing in 2021. There is not much to see above ground, and a rider who expects dramatic ruins will be disappointed, but the information boards and the surroundings give a good sense of why people lived here: the bay was rich in fish and shellfish, and the hills behind held game and forest foods.

  3. 3. Irie Site

    Hokkaido's southern coast sits on the edge of Funka Bay, a large, nearly circular inlet of the Pacific that is well sheltered, and about ten kilometres from the town of Date there is a shell mound that records how people lived here some four thousand years ago. Irie is one of the sites that make up the Jōmon Prehistoric Sites of Northern Japan, which UNESCO listed in 2021, a group of settlements and ritual places left by hunter-gatherer-fishers who lived without agriculture but built permanent villages, and who made the remarkable coiled-cord pottery that gives the era its name. The mound consists of discarded shells, fish bones and other debris, along with graves, and it is a rich archive of daily life.

  4. 4. Kitakogane Site

    Hokkaido does not usually come to mind when people talk about ancient Japan, yet the shell heaps around Uchiura Bay are some of the oldest signs of settled life in the archipelago. Kitakogane sits on the edge of the bay near Date, roughly halfway between Muroran and the volcanic country around Lake Toya. The Jomon people who lived here gathered shellfish, hunted deer and fished, and they threw the shells onto the same pile for centuries, which is why a modern visitor can read a kitchen midden like a diary. It belongs to the group of northern Jomon sites that UNESCO listed in 2021.

  5. 5. Ofune Site

    Ofune is a Jomon settlement on the southern tip of Hokkaido, in the hills above Hakodate, and it is a lot bigger than the signboards make it look. The Jomon were hunter-gatherers who lived in the northern forests of Japan for something like ten thousand years, long before rice farming, and they built a settled life around chestnuts, fish, deer and the odd well-travelled trade good. Ofune was inhabited for centuries and is now part of the Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan, which UNESCO recognised in 2021. Reconstructed pit houses hold the grass in place, and the scale of the pits tells you someone intended to stay.

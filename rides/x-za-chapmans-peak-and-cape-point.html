Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cape Town

    Cape Town sits at the foot of Table Mountain, a flat-topped sandstone wall about a kilometre high, and on a day when the tablecloth of cloud spills over the edge it is one of the great sights of the world. The city lies between the mountain and the Atlantic, with a harbour that was founded in 1652 by the Dutch East India Company as a supply station for ships on the route to the East. It grew into a port of many cultures, and the mix of Dutch, Malay, African and British influences is still visible in its food, language and architecture.

  2. 2. Hout Bay

    Hout Bay is a fishing harbour wedged into a bowl of mountains on the Atlantic side of the Cape Peninsula, and it keeps a stubborn, salty independence from the city just over the ridge. The name is Dutch for wood: early settlers cut the forests that once stood in the valley for timber, long before the suburbs, and today the slopes of the Sentinel, a rock peak that guards the mouth of the bay, are covered in fynbos and houses with fine views. Constantia, about seven kilometres away over the pass, is the nearest big suburb.

  3. 3. Chapmans Peak

    Nine kilometres of road hang on the cliffs between Hout Bay and Noordhoek, and the Cape Peninsula has nothing better. Chapman's Peak Drive was cut into the sheer face of the mountain in the early twentieth century, opening in 1922 after years of work, and it clings to the rock a hundred metres or more above the Atlantic with 114 corners, so people say, in a short, spectacular ride. Between bends there are galleries and shelters built over the road to protect it from falling rock, which is a fact of life here.

  4. 4. Cape Point

    Cape Point is where the Cape Peninsula runs out of land: a ridge of sandstone cliffs falling about two hundred metres into the Atlantic, with a lighthouse on top and a wind that can blow your visor shut. People often say it is where two oceans meet and that it is the southern tip of Africa. Neither is quite right. The tip of the continent is Cape Agulhas, a long way east, and the meeting of warm and cold currents is a broad, shifting zone rather than a line, but none of that matters when you stand on the viewing platform and watch the swell come in from Antarctica.

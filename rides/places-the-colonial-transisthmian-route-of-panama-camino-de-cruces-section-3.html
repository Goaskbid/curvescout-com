Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. The Colonial Transisthmian Route of Panamá

    The Camino Real, the royal road that Spain built across the Isthmus of Panama in the sixteenth century, is the reason this country matters in world history. Gold and silver from Peru were carried by ship to Panama City, loaded on mules and carried over the continental divide to the Caribbean coast, then shipped to Spain. The route had two arms: a paved mule road, the Camino Real, to Portobelo, and a mixed river-and-trail route, the Camino de Cruces, which used the Chagres River for part of the way. Together with the forts that guarded them, the Caribbean forts at Portobelo and San Lorenzo are on the UNESCO list, listed in 1980.

  2. 2. Fortifications on the Caribbean Side of Pa

    Columbus named it Puerto Bello, the beautiful harbour, in 1502, and for once a sailor's first impression was right. Portobelo sits on a deep, sheltered bay on Panama's Caribbean coast, backed by steep green hills, and for two centuries it was the point where the wealth of Peru left the New World. Gold and silver came by sea up the Pacific coast to Panama City, crossed the isthmus on mule trails like the Camino de Cruces, and were loaded here onto the Spanish treasure fleets. Every year, a great fair was held, with merchants and soldiers crowding the little port, and prices and diseases both soared.

  3. 3. Near Curundú

    This point lies on the western side of Panama City, in the belt of land that was once the American-run Canal Zone, between the old neighbourhoods of Curundú and Ancón and the green slopes leading towards the canal. Ancón Hill, the forested hump that dominates this part of the city, was for decades the administrative heart of the zone, and the area still has the wide lawns, tidy wooden houses and tropical trees of that era.

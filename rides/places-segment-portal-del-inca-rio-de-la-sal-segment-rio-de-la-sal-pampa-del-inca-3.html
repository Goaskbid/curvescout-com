Why this ride

It goes by way of Segment Río de La Sal - Pampa del Inca and back through Segment Río de La Sal - Pampa del Inca. The highest point is about 3,150 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Segment Portal del Inca - Río de La Sal

    A long way up in the Atacama, where the desert climbs into the high Andes of northern Chile, a worn path crosses the landscape between a place called the Portal del Inca and the Río de la Sal, the salt river. It is a segment of the Qhapaq Ñan, the great network of Inca roads, which UNESCO listed in 2014 across six countries. In Chile the road went south from the Inca heartland, crossing passes of over four thousand metres to reach the valleys of Copiapó and beyond, and traces of it survive in the dry air with striking clarity: stone markers, small shelters and the line of the trail itself.

  2. 2. Segment Río de La Sal - Pampa del Inca

    Think of this as the same journey seen from the other end of the segment. The label here is Portal del Inca to Río de la Sal, and the first half of that name is the interesting one. Portal suggests a gateway, a place where the trail passes through a narrowing or a pass and the ground begins to change. Without a signed monument underfoot, the useful thing to say is what the surrounding country does: the Atacama's interior basins rise gradually toward the Andean crest, and the Inca engineers used each step in that rise as a staging post.

  3. 3. Segment Río de La Sal - Pampa del Inca

    Out here the Incas walked a road through country that most of us would call empty, and they did it without wheels, horses or a single petrol station. The stretch between the Río de la Sal and the Pampa del Inca is one of the Chilean pieces of the Qhapaq Ñan, the great Andean road network that tied Cusco to the far corners of the empire and was listed by UNESCO across six countries in 2014. In the Atacama region it shows up as a faint, dead-straight trace across gravel plains, sometimes edged with stones, sometimes marked by the low ruins of a tambo where runners and caravans once rested.

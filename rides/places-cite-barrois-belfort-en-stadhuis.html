Why this ride

From Pecquencourt it runs 149 km to Pecquencourt; allow about 2 h 34 of riding. The stops in between: Ancienne Fosse n°2 et mine-image, Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Lille, Belfort en Stadhuis and back through Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Lille. It crosses from France into Belgium - carry ID and check the toll rules.

The stops

  1. 1. Cité Barrois

    Start here.

    Rows of red-brick cottages, each with its garden, set along curving streets with trees: the Cité Barrois in Pecquencourt looks almost English, and that is no accident. In the later years of the coal era, the mining companies of northern France built garden cities for their workers, influenced by ideas from across the Channel about healthy housing and green space. This one is part of the Nord-Pas-de-Calais Mining Basin, listed by UNESCO in 2012 as an entire landscape of pits, slag heaps, workers' housing, schools, churches and railways spread over more than a hundred kilometres.

    Read the complete Cité Barrois write-up.

  2. 2. Ancienne Fosse n°2 et mine-image

    About 25 km in.

    Oignies holds a special place in the history of northern France: it is where coal mining in the Pas-de-Calais began and where it ended. In 1842, coal was found here during a search for water on the estate of a local landowner, Henriette de Clercq, starting a rush that transformed the region. And on 21 December 1990, the last tonne of coal in the Nord-Pas de Calais basin was brought up from the 9-9bis pit in Oignies, ending more than two and a half centuries of mining in the region. The old Fosse n°2 and its mine-image, a training gallery where young miners learned their trade above ground, are part of the World Heritage site.

    Read the complete Ancienne Fosse n°2 et mine-image write-up.

  3. 3. Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Lille

    About 50 km in.

    Lille's town hall belfry is a child of the twentieth century. After the old town hall was lost, the architect Émile Dubuisson built a new one in the 1920s and early 1930s in a working-class district south of the centre, and its belfry, rising about 104 metres, is the tallest civic belfry in Europe. Built of reinforced concrete and brick in an Art Deco reinterpretation of the Flemish tradition, it is part of the UNESCO-listed Belfries of Belgium and France, a group that celebrates the towers built by towns to symbolise their freedom and civic pride.

    Read the complete Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Lille write-up.

  4. 4. Belfort en Stadhuis

    About 75 km in.

    British soldiers marching to the front in the First World War left Ypres by the road that led east towards Menen, and the gate they passed through became the Menin Gate, now a memorial bearing the names of more than fifty thousand soldiers with no known grave. Menen itself, about twenty kilometres from Ypres, sits right on the French border, on the river Leie, which the French call the Lys, and its town hall and belfry, standing on the market square, are part of the UNESCO listing of the belfries of Belgium and France. The belfry, like much of the town, was damaged in the war and rebuilt, and it remains the proud symbol of the town's civic history.

    Read the complete Belfort en Stadhuis write-up.

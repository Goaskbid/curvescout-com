Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Albuquerque

    Albuquerque lies in the Rio Grande valley at about 1,500 metres, with the long wall of the Sandia Mountains standing to the east, and it is the biggest city in New Mexico. On Route 66 it holds a special place, because in 1937 the highway was straightened here, abandoning a long loop through Santa Fe and sending the road directly across the city along Central Avenue. The change shortened the journey by about a hundred miles and made Albuquerque a boomtown for motels, diners and neon, and a good deal of that survives.

  2. 2. Madrid New Mexico

    Madrid, pronounced the American way with the stress on the first syllable, is one of the more unlikely success stories in New Mexico. It began as a coal-mining town, with company housing strung along a narrow canyon in the Ortiz Mountains, and for a while it was known for its extravagant Christmas lights, which the coal company strung up each year and which could be seen from miles away. When the mines closed, the town was nearly abandoned. Artists began moving in during the 1970s, buying cheap cottages and turning the old storefronts into galleries and workshops, and the place found a second life.

  3. 3. Cerrillos

    Cerrillos is the sleepy older sibling of Madrid, a dusty, unhurried little place a short ride away that feels like a film set because it has been used as one. Its wide main street of adobe and wooden storefronts, its church, and its dry hills all suggest the nineteenth-century frontier, and the village makes little effort to dress it up. Chickens, dogs, and cats are more in evidence than crowds, and that is part of the pleasure.

  4. 4. Santa Fe

    Route 66 used to go to Santa Fe, and then in 1937 it did not. The original alignment of the Mother Road ran over Glorieta Pass into the old Spanish capital of New Mexico, before dropping south to Albuquerque, and then engineers cut a straight line across the desert from Santa Rosa to Albuquerque, shaving about a hundred miles off the journey and leaving Santa Fe on a bypassed loop. The ride from Amarillo to Santa Fe follows that early route, and this is the end of it, in a city at about seven thousand feet in the foothills of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains.

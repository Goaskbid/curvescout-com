Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Bassari Salémata

    The Bassari people live in the hills of southeastern Senegal and northern Guinea, in a country of rocky outcrops, savannah and deep, lush valleys that run down from the Fouta Djallon plateau. Salemata is one of their main centres, a small town in the Kedougou region of Senegal, and the Bassari, with their neighbours the Bedik and the Fula, make up the cultural landscape of Bassari Country, listed by UNESCO in 2012. The Bassari have kept their traditions with great determination. Young men go through initiation rites in the forest, lasting weeks and ending with masked dances and ceremonies in which the whole village takes part. Dancers wear costumes of leaves and fibres, and the Bedik have their own, different, masks.

  2. 2. Bédik Bandafassi

    In the hills of south-eastern Senegal, near the border with Guinea, the Bedik people live in villages perched on rocky hilltops, reached by steep paths through the bush. Their round huts with tall conical thatched roofs, clustered closely together, and their traditions of masks, initiation and seasonal festivals have survived because of their isolation. The Bedik country around Bandafassi is part of the Bassari Country: Bassari, Fula and Bedik Cultural Landscapes, listed by UNESCO in 2012, along with the lands of their Bassari and Fula neighbours.

  3. 3. Peul Dindéfello

    Dindéfello is where the Sahel finally gives up and the Fouta Djallon begins. The village sits in southeastern Senegal, tucked against the border with Guinea, and the plateau behind it drops away in a red cliff that feeds a waterfall of about a hundred metres. After the rains the fall is a proper white rope hanging off the rock. In the dry season it thins to a veil, but the pool beneath is still deep enough for a cool plunge and the walk through the gorge in the shade is worth the heat.

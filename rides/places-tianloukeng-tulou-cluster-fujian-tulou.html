On the way it passes Hokgkeng Tulou Cluster, Chuxi Tulou Cluster, Gaobei Tulou Cluster and Fujian Tulou. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Tianloukeng Tulou Cluster

    The tulou of southwestern Fujian look like something dropped from the sky, huge round or square earthen fortresses sitting among tea terraces and bamboo, with roofs that curve like turned-up eaves. Tianloukeng is a cluster of them in Nanjing County, in the hills inland from Xiamen, and the group is probably the most photographed in the region because it can be seen as a whole: a square tulou at the centre, surrounded by round ones, set on a slope so the roofs stagger like a bowl of dumplings.

  2. 2. Hokgkeng Tulou Cluster

    This is the Hakka country of southwestern Fujian, the hills of Nanjing and Pinghe counties, where villagers built vast earthen fortress-homes called tulou. The ones near Hukeng and Tianloukeng are among the finest, and they are set in valleys of terraced tea, bamboo groves and rice paddies in a green so deep it looks artificial after rain. Each tulou is a ring, square or sometimes oval of rammed earth walls up to a metre or more thick, three or four storeys tall, with one big gate and a single courtyard in the middle where families kept a well, a shrine and animals.

  3. 3. Chuxi Tulou Cluster

    In the mountains of western Fujian the villages are shaped like fortresses, and Chuxi is one of the classic examples. The tulou, or earthen buildings, here are huge rammed-earth rings and squares up to several storeys high, built by Hakka families over the centuries so that a whole clan could live, store grain and defend itself behind one thick wall. The Chuxi cluster, in the Yongding district, is part of the Fujian Tulou World Heritage group, and the buildings sit among terraced fields, bamboo and tea bushes in a valley that is green nearly all year.

  4. 4. Gaobei Tulou Cluster

    In the green hills of south-western Fujian, in a corner of China that few foreign riders ever see, the Hakka people built round and square fortress houses of rammed earth, called tulou, and the cluster at Gaobei is the one usually shown first. The Hakka were migrants from the north who arrived in waves over centuries, and they were often unwelcome in settled areas, so they built for defence and for family unity, with each clan living inside a single enormous building. The outer walls, made of clay, sand and lime tamped hard between wooden boards, are often more than a metre thick, with few windows on the lower floors, and they keep the interior cool in summer and warm in winter.

  5. 5. Fujian Tulou

    Picture a giant ring of rammed earth, three or four storeys high, with almost no windows on the outside and a single heavy gate. Inside, galleries of wooden rooms face a courtyard with an ancestral hall at its heart, and up to several hundred members of one extended family once lived within those walls. That is a tulou, and the hills of south-western Fujian are dotted with them, round and square, some centuries old, many still inhabited.

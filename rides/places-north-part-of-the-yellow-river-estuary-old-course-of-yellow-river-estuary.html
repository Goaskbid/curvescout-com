Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. North Part of the Yellow River Estuary

    The Yellow River has never stayed in one place. In 1855 it burst its banks in Henan and abandoned the course it had followed for centuries to reach the sea south of the Shandong peninsula, and instead found a new route north-east into the Bohai Gulf. The mouth of that new course is here, in the northern part of the Yellow River Delta, and it is a place of geological theatre: a young landscape of mudflats and reed beds, growing seaward by some metres a year in good times, a map being redrawn as you watch.

  2. 2. Dawenliu

    Where the Yellow River meets the sea, the land is made of mud. The river carries an immense burden of silt, scoured from the loess plateau thousands of kilometres upstream, and at its mouth on the Bohai Gulf it drops it, building new land year by year. The Dawenliu area, in the Yellow River Delta, lies in the southern part of the national nature reserve and is one of the best places to see how fresh the ground is: reed beds, tamarisk, salt marsh and shallow channels, all flat and glittering under a huge sky.

  3. 3. South Part of the Yellow River Estuary

    Few rivers move so much earth as the Yellow, and here, at its mouth in Shandong, you can watch the land being made. The Huang He carries more silt than any other major river, a load picked up from the loess plateau, and where it slows at the sea, it drops it, building a delta that grows by many square kilometres in some years. The river has often changed course here. It has flooded, shifted and found new channels, and its mouth has moved hundreds of kilometres over the centuries, which is why the Chinese call it China's Sorrow.

  4. 4. Old Course of Yellow River Estuary

    The Yellow River is the most restless big river on earth, and this is one of the places it used to leave the land. Over the centuries it has carried so much silt out of the loess plateau that it keeps building its own floor, rising above the plain, bursting its banks and changing course. In 1855 it broke out in Henan, gave up its long southern route to the Yellow Sea and swung north to the Bohai Gulf instead. The channel that ended here, on the low coast of Shandong, is one of the old mouths of that restless history, and the land around it is still being made by mud.

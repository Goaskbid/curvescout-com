Uglich and the Upper Volga

Along the Volga where it is still narrow: a kremlin at Uglich, wooden houses at Myshkin and the river always on one side.

Why this ride

Roadbook

  1. Uglich start

    A boy died in Uglich in 1591, and the history of Russia changed. Tsarevich Dmitry, the youngest son of Ivan the Terrible, was found with his throat cut in the courtyard of the town's kremlin, and whether it was murder or an accident has been argued ever since. His death left the throne without an heir, set off the Time of Troubles, and gave rise to a string of pretenders who claimed to be the lost prince. The Church of St Dmitry on the Blood, a red-and-white building with blue domes on the spot, is one of the town's best-known sights.

    Uglich is a small town on the upper Volga with a lot of charm. The river was dammed here in 1940, so the water is now a broad reservoir, and the old centre sits above it with churches, merchants' houses and a monastery on the bank. The town's bell is a local legend: it rang to signal the death of the tsarevich, and as a punishment it was whipped, had its tongue removed, and was exiled to Siberia, only returning in 1892. Uglich also makes cheese, which is sold in the market, and a plate of it with local honey is a good lunch.

    The riding around is gentle and green. Roads to Myshkin, about thirty kilometres away, wind through villages and meadows, and Myshkin itself is a small town known for its mouse museum and its quiet streets. To the west, the huge Rybinsk Reservoir spreads out like a sea, with flooded forests and migrating birds. Surfaces range from fair to rough, and traffic is light, though trucks use the main roads. Summer is the best season, with long evenings and clear skies.

    Uglich has about thirty-eight thousand people, so fuel, food and rooms are easy to find. Myshkin is smaller, so top up here first. Walk along the embankment in the evening, when the light on the water is lovely. Foreign riders should check current conditions and rules before planning a trip.

  2. Myshkin scenic

    Myshkin is a small town on the Volga, about two hundred kilometres north of Moscow, and it is one of those places that has made a virtue of its smallness. About six thousand people live here in a cluster of old merchant houses, churches and wooden cottages on the river bank, at a point where the great river, broad and brown, rolls slowly past. The town grew rich from river trade in the nineteenth century and still has a tidy, provincial look, with a long street of two-storey brick houses and a cathedral with a tall bell tower.

    The name is said to come from the Russian word for mouse, and the town has embraced the idea with enthusiasm. A local legend tells how a mouse saved the town's founder, a prince, from a snake, and the mouse is now the town's symbol. A museum devoted entirely to mice displays thousands of mouse figures, toys, and paintings, and is surprisingly entertaining, with an interactive exhibition that has delighted children and adults for decades. There are other small museums too, some of them gloriously quirky, and a number of craft shops sell souvenirs.

    The surroundings are more serious. The Volga here is part of a chain of huge reservoirs built in the Soviet era, and the Rybinsk Reservoir, a short distance north, was flooded in the early 1940s, drowning the old town of Mologa and many villages. On the other side of the river, Uglich, with its brightly painted churches and the famous Church of St Dmitry on the Blood, is within easy reach. The riverbanks are lined with willow and birch, and in summer the water is busy with pleasure boats and cruise ships.

    Riding to Myshkin is a gentle journey on flat roads through farmland and forest, and the surface is mostly good, though patched in places. Fuel is best found in Uglich or Rybinsk, the nearest towns with larger populations, since Myshkin itself is small, and the distances between services can be long. Summer is warm and the evenings are light until late, while winter is long, dark and cold, with snow and ice. A walk along the embankment at sunset, watching the river turn pink and the boats slide past, is the best way to end a day here.

  3. Rybinsk finish

    Rybinsk is a Volga river port in Yaroslavl Oblast, where the city's history is written in grain, barges and water. It started as a fishing village, and its name comes from the Russian word for fish. By the nineteenth century it had become the main grain port of the upper Volga, where cargo from the south was transferred from large river boats to smaller vessels that could travel north through the canals to St. Petersburg. Thousands of men worked on the quays, and the city's warehouses and exchange building, a handsome brick structure that stands on the embankment, recall that boom.

    The workers included the burlaks, the barge haulers who dragged vessels upstream along the riverbank with ropes, a job of famous misery. The painter Ilya Repin made them an icon of Russian suffering in his picture Barge Haulers on the Volga, and a monument to the burlaks stands on the Rybinsk waterfront. Another landmark is the great reservoir that spreads north of the city. The Rybinsk Reservoir, created in the early 1940s, was for a time the largest man-made lake in the world, and it drowned the old town of Mologa and many villages, which now lie under the water. In dry years, the old foundations sometimes emerge, a haunting sight.

    The city is large, with over two hundred thousand people, a mix of industry and old buildings. It builds aircraft engines, and the Transfiguration Cathedral and the museum of local history are worth visiting. The embankment is pleasant for a walk in the evening. The surrounding country is gentle, with farmland and wood on either side of the Volga, and the roads are fairly flat and quiet, with long straight sections and occasional curves. The summer is warm, with a good chance of rain, and the autumn can be brilliant.

    Fuel stations, supermarkets and cafes are everywhere, and Rybinsk is a reliable stop on the way to Uglich or Myshkin. A Volga fish, smoked or fried, is the natural thing to eat, and local places often serve it with potatoes and pickles.

    Look at the river from the embankment in the late afternoon. The water is broad and slow, and the old warehouses glow in the light.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Subterranean city of Derinkuyu

    Derinkuyu means deep well, and the town above the underground city is named for it. Beneath the plain of Cappadocia, south of Nevşehir, people have carved a maze of tunnels, rooms, stables, kitchens, chapels, wine presses and storerooms to a depth of some sixty metres, over many levels, though only the upper eight or so are open to visitors. At its busiest the city could shelter thousands of people along with their animals and food stores. Large round stone doors, shaped like millstones, could be rolled across the passages from the inside to block intruders, and narrow shafts supplied air and water, so that a community could live below ground for months.

  2. 2. Yesilöz site

    Cappadocia was made by volcanoes and carved by people. Millions of years ago, the volcanoes of central Anatolia, Erciyes and Hasan, covered the plateau in thick layers of ash that hardened into a soft rock called tuff, and wind and water have since eroded it into the cones, pinnacles and ridges that are known as fairy chimneys. People found the rock easy to carve, and for thousands of years they have dug homes, churches, storerooms and whole underground cities into it. The area is a World Heritage site, and it is hard to find a corner without a cave or a dovecote cut into a cliff.

  3. 3. Soganli site

    Soğanlı lies in a side valley of Cappadocia that most tour buses skip, south-east of Ürgüp and Yeşilhisar, on the slopes of the volcanic uplands. The name means with onions, which tells you something about the humble character of the place. The valley sides are made of soft volcanic tuff, which the local Christians carved out in the Byzantine centuries into churches, chapels, monasteries, and houses. Many of the rock churches date from the ninth to the thirteenth century and keep fragments of frescoes: Karabaş, the Yılanlı or Snake church, the Barbara church and others, whose names reflect old stories more than documented history.

  4. 4. Karlik site

    The Karlık church, one of the rock-cut sanctuaries of the district, is usually described as a dark church, with a simple cross-in-square plan cut into the stone and painted decoration that has faded over the centuries. Many rock churches here were cut by monks in the tenth and eleventh centuries, and their interiors imitate built architecture, with carved domes, pillars and arches that look like masonry but are all hollowed from a single mass of rock.

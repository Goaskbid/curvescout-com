Why this ride

  1. 1. Fuel stop near Kremmen

    In 1412, on a causeway through the marshes near Kremmen, the forces of the new Hohenzollern rulers of Brandenburg fought the Dukes of Pomerania in a battle that marked the beginning of the Hohenzollern era, which would eventually lead to Prussia and the German Empire. The causeway, the Kremmer Damm, still crosses the wetland, and a memorial cross marks the site of the battle. Fontane described it in his travels, and the story is part of Brandenburg folklore.

  2. 2. Near Konradshöhe

    On the north-western edge of Berlin, the city dissolves into water and woods. Around Konradshöhe and Heiligensee, villas and gardens run down to the shores of the Havel and the Tegeler See, Berlin's second-largest lake, with islands, sailing boats and a long promenade at Alt-Tegel. Across the water, the Tegel forest stretches away, a dense area of pine and oak that hides walking paths and small lakes.

  3. 3. Photo stop near Słubice

    Until 1945, Słubice was the Dammvorstadt, the suburb of Frankfurt an der Oder on the east bank of the river. When the border was redrawn along the Oder after the war, the town became Polish, its German population was expelled, and it was resettled by Poles, many of them themselves displaced from lands further east. Today, a bridge links the two towns, and the border is open, with people crossing freely to shop, work and study. The Collegium Polonicum, a joint institution of the European University Viadrina in Frankfurt and a Polish university, stands near the bridge as a symbol of reconciliation.

  4. 4. Scenic stretch near Wendisch Rietz

    Brandenburg likes to call the Scharmützelsee its sea, the Märkisches Meer, and from the southern shore at Wendisch Rietz you can see why. The lake stretches away for about ten kilometres towards Bad Saarow, wide enough that on a windy day it throws up proper waves. Wendisch Rietz sits at the southern tip, with a harbour, a beach, holiday homes and a channel linking the lake to the Glubigsee and the waterways beyond. The name hints at the Slavic Wends who lived here before German settlement.

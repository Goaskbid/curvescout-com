Why this ride

A 360 km round trip from Ágios Pávlos - roughly 6 h 12 in the saddle. It goes by way of Bronze Age settlement and Cemetery of Tumu, Archaeological site of Dion, Archaeological Site of Aigai and back through Archaeological site of Dion. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Blatades Monastery

    Vlatadon, or Blatades, is the monastery to see if you want to understand why Thessaloniki's upper town is the city's loveliest part. It sits against the old Byzantine walls, near the Eptapyrgion fortress, and it was founded in the 1360s by two brothers named Vlatas, with a church that mixes the brick and stone of the Palaiologan style. The monastery has been a centre of church learning for centuries and still houses the Patriarchal Foundation for Patristic Studies, so you may find priests and students crossing the courtyard with books. It is a place where life continues.

  2. 2. Bronze Age settlement and Cemetery of Tumu

    Vergina is a small village at the foot of the Pierian mountains in northern Greece, and for most of history nobody outside the region knew its name. That changed in 1977, when the archaeologist Manolis Andronikos opened a great burial mound here and found royal tombs with astonishing grave goods. The site is ancient Aigai, the first capital of the Macedonian kingdom, and one of the tombs is widely attributed to Philip II, father of Alexander the Great. The prehistoric settlement and the large cemetery of burial mounds nearby show that people had been living and burying their dead here for a very long time before the Macedonian kings.

  3. 3. Archaeological site of Dion

    Mount Olympus rises straight out of the plain behind Dion, and on a clear morning its snow-streaked summit seems close enough to touch. The ancient Macedonians built their most sacred city here, at the foot of the home of the gods, and the kings came to sacrifice to Zeus before going to war. Philip II celebrated victories here, and Alexander the Great is said to have held a great festival at Dion before setting out to conquer Persia. Walking among the ruins, you can see why they chose the place: water bubbles up everywhere, the plain is fertile, and the mountain looms like a living presence.

  4. 4. Archaeological Site of Aigai

    In 1977 the Greek archaeologist Manolis Andronikos lifted the stone slab of a vaulted tomb under a huge mound near the village of Vergina and found a gold casket holding the cremated bones of a man in his forties, with a golden wreath beside them. Most scholars now accept that it was Philip II of Macedon, the father of Alexander the Great, murdered in the theatre of the old capital in 336 BC. The mound, the Great Tumulus, is the centrepiece of Aigai, the first capital of Macedon, and today the royal tombs are displayed underground in a museum built inside the tumulus itself, in a half-light that makes the gold glow.

Roadbook

  1. Abashiri start

    Abashiri is a port town on the Okhotsk Sea coast of north-eastern Hokkaido, and its fame rests on two things: ice and a prison. From about late January to March, drift ice from the Amur river region far to the north arrives on the current and fills the sea, and boats take visitors out through the floes in a ship with a reinforced bow. The sea goes quiet, white and creaking, a landscape that feels like the edge of the world.

    The prison belongs to the late nineteenth century. When Hokkaido was being opened up by the Meiji government, convicts were used as labour to build roads and railways, and Abashiri became one of the harshest places they were sent. The old wooden buildings now serve as a museum, and a modern prison still operates nearby. It adds a sombre note to a town otherwise known for seafood, with crab, scallops and salmon roe on every menu, and for the lakes on the doorstep, Lake Abashiri and the wide, shallow Lake Notoro.

    About forty-two thousand people live here, so fuel, shops and a good choice of places to eat and sleep are close, which is why it works so well as the beginning of a ride east to the Shiretoko Peninsula. The roads leading out of town run across broad plains with potato and beet fields, long straights and gentle bends, and the pace is relaxed. Distances here are large, and the sea breeze can be strong, so gusts and cool temperatures should be expected even in summer.

    The best season is summer, when wildflowers run along the coast, especially in the coastal meadows of the Okhotsk, and the light is long. September offers clear air and fewer riders, while winter belongs to those prepared for ice and snow. Before heading east, take a few minutes at the harbour, check the weather for the peninsula, and eat something substantial, because the next stretch of the route runs into wilder country.

  2. Utoro stop

    Utoro is a small fishing village on the northern coast of the Shiretoko Peninsula, facing the Okhotsk Sea, and it works as the gateway to one of the wildest places in Japan. Shiretoko was inscribed as a UNESCO natural World Heritage Site in 2005, and the reason is the relationship between sea and land: drift ice brings nutrients that feed plankton, which feed fish, which feed everything from salmon to sea eagles to brown bears. It is a food chain you can see from the shore.

    The name Shiretoko comes from the Ainu language and is usually translated as the end of the earth, which is a fair description once you are here. Beyond the village the road continues to the end of the peninsula, but the real wilderness is a no-road zone, reachable only on foot or by boat. Cruises from the harbour run along the coast, and the chances of spotting brown bears on the shore are better than almost anywhere else in the country. Near the village, the dark rock outcrop of Oronko Iwa serves as a viewpoint, and the sunsets over the sea are famously good.

    For motorcyclists, this is a destination reached by long, easy roads, with sea on one side and forested hills on the other. The surface is good, the bends are open, and traffic is light outside the summer peak. Weather matters: sea fog can drift in and make visibility poor, and the temperature drops quickly in the evening. Because bears and Ezo deer live here, take care at dawn and dusk, keep to the speed limit and never feed or approach any animal.

    Utoro is small, with guesthouses, a few restaurants and shops for basic needs, so plan the bigger purchases in advance. Abashiri, behind you on the coast, is the bigger town for supplies, and it makes sense to arrive with a full tank and a booked bed in the busy season. The fish here, from the cold northern waters, is memorable, especially grilled salmon, and the evening air smells of sea and woodsmoke.

  3. Shiretoko Pass stop · 738 m

    Shiretoko Pass crosses the spine of the peninsula at 738 metres, and it is the high point of the road between Utoro and Rausu. The climb from the Utoro side runs through forest, then the trees thin and the road emerges onto an open shoulder of mountain, with the sea dropping away on the Okhotsk side and, on the other, the water of the Nemuro Strait. The view from the lookout is one of the finest in Hokkaido, taking in the volcanic peak of Mount Rausu, a little over 1,600 metres, rising behind.

    On clear days you can see across the strait to Kunashir, one of the southern Kuril Islands, whose volcanic outline appears remarkably close. The island is administered by Russia, but Japan claims it as part of the Northern Territories, and the sight of land so near that is out of reach gives the lookout a poignant charge that no sign can explain. People tend to fall quiet there, then take a lot of photographs.

    The pass road is a seasonal one: heavy snow closes it through winter, usually until spring, so check the status before you plan a crossing. In the open season the surface is good and the bends are smooth, with gradients that are steady rather than severe, and plenty of places to pull over. Mist and fog are frequent, particularly in summer when cool, moist air rises off the sea, and the temperature at the top can be many degrees below the coast, so a warm layer is wise.

    Wildlife is part of the experience. Brown bears are present in Shiretoko in significant numbers, though sightings from the road are usually brief, and Ezo deer and foxes are common. Do not stop on the carriageway for animals, and never leave food visible. There is no village at the summit, only the lookout and parking, so carry water and a snack and arrive with fuel from Utoro or Rausu, the two small towns at either end. On a clear evening, the light on Kunashir is enough to justify a long wait.

  4. Rausu stop

    Rausu is the main settlement on the eastern side of the Shiretoko Peninsula, a fishing port facing the Nemuro Strait, and it feels like a place where the weather and the sea decide everything. The harbour is busy with boats, the air smells of kelp and diesel, and the houses are low and sturdy against the wind. The town's best-known product is Rausu kelp, a prized variety of konbu dried on the shore in summer, used for the stock that underlies much of Japanese cooking.

    Rausu is also a base for seeing wildlife. Boat tours run out from the harbour to watch seabirds and whales, and in winter the drift ice that pushes into the strait brings Steller's sea eagles and white-tailed eagles, which gather in large numbers to fish. Behind the town the land rises into the volcanic mountains of the peninsula, and the hot springs that come from them are part of local life. One open-air bath beside a river is free to enter, a simple stone pool where bathers, birds and the odd bear-warning sign share the same landscape.

    The road from the pass descends toward Rausu in smooth bends, with forest giving way to open slopes and then the coast, and on a clear day you look across at Kunashir the whole way. The surface is good, but fog can settle on the coast in summer and reduce visibility to a few metres, so expect to slow down. Winter makes the road impassable over the pass, and the east coast road beyond town can be cold and windy even in late summer.

    Rausu is small, with a handful of guesthouses and restaurants, and it is wise to book ahead in high season. Fuel and food are available in a town of this kind, but hours can be short, so do not leave it late in the evening. Order a seafood bowl or grilled fish, taste the kelp, and take your time over the harbour views. For a riders' stop on the far side of Hokkaido, it is oddly comforting.

  5. Nemuro Strait finish

    The Nemuro Strait is the narrow stretch of sea between the eastern end of Hokkaido and Kunashir, the southernmost large island of the Kuril chain, and the coast road beside it is a good place to end a ride. South of Rausu, the road follows the shore with the water on one side and low, grassy, windswept country on the other, and the view across the strait is of a long volcanic island that is plainly visible and, for most visitors, entirely out of reach. Kunashir is administered by Russia, while Japan still claims it, so the sea here has a quiet political weight.

    It is also a rich piece of water. The strait is cold and nutrient-heavy, with kelp beds, salmon runs, and fishing fleets that work out of small harbours. Sea fog is common in summer, rolling in from the cold current and wrapping the coast in white, then lifting by midday to reveal a bright, hard blue. In winter drift ice can reach the shore, and a few weeks later the sea breaks free again. Birds are everywhere, from gulls and cormorants to the great eagles of the region.

    Riding along the strait is simple and open. The road is wide, the surface is good, and the bends are mild, with long views and few trees. It is a place to relax after the mountain sections, with a sea breeze and occasional farm vehicles as the only company. Wind can be stiff, so watch for gusts on exposed sections, and the temperature can drop abruptly when fog arrives, which is a reason to carry an extra layer even in July.

    The nearest town of any size is Shibetsu, about thirty-four kilometres south, with around twenty thousand people and the usual choice of fuel, food and beds, and it is the sensible place to plan the end of the day. Further south, the long sand spit of the Notsuke Peninsula reaches out into the water, and its shallow lagoon draws birds in large numbers. The local fish and dairy products are excellent, so look for fresh salmon, scallops and ice cream before you turn for home.

Why this ride

A bigger sea-air day for stable weather and daylight. The best part sits around Hohwacht bay view, Fehmarn bridge view, Eutin return lake, while Heiligenhafen harbour keeps the day practical instead of turning it into a fuel or food hunt. Check the OSM map, Google preview and GPX checkpoint order before departure; they use the same waypoint sequence. Ride it with legal pull-offs, patience through villages and enough daylight to stop without rushing.

Roadbook

  1. Lütjenburg fuel gate fuel · 35 m

    Lütjenburg is a small, tidy market town on the Baltic side of Schleswig-Holstein, a few kilometres from the sea in a rolling country of fields, lakes and low hills. People here call the region Holstein Switzerland, a joke that is half serious, because by the flat standards of northern Germany the landscape counts as dramatic, with hills of a hundred metres or so and lakes in the hollows. About five and a half thousand people live in the town, and it has the feel of a place that has been a local centre for a long time.

    The market square, with its church and its stepped gables, is the heart of town, and on market days it fills with stalls selling vegetables, fish, flowers and cheese. A hill above the town, the Hohe Lütjenburg, offers a view over the roofs and the bay, and a short drive takes you to the beaches at Hohwacht, where the Baltic is shallow, grey-blue and quiet. The coast here is gentle, with dunes, reed beds and long beaches, and in summer it fills with holidaymakers from Hamburg and Kiel.

    The surrounding country is manor-house land, with big estates, brick farm buildings and long avenues of beech and lime trees, a legacy of the Danish and German nobility who owned most of the land until the twentieth century. Several of these estates, with their moats and parks, can be seen from the road.

    The riding is easy and relaxed. Narrow lanes follow ridges and valleys, bend through villages and cross low rises, with wide views over the fields, and traffic is light outside the summer weekends. The wind from the sea is the main hazard, and on exposed stretches, it can push a bike around. Fuel is available in town, along with bakeries, cafes and fish shops, where you can get a smoked fish roll, a local favourite. At about thirty-five metres above sea level, the town is not high, but the surroundings roll gently. Spring and early summer are lovely, with yellow rapeseed fields, and autumn brings misty mornings and golden light.

  2. Hohwacht bay view swim · 10 m

    Hohwacht Bay is a wide, shallow curve of the Baltic between the Bay of Kiel and the Fehmarn Belt, and the view from the shore on a clear day opens a long, gentle arc of pale sand and low green land. The beach at Hohwacht is a broad one, backed by low dunes and a wooded cliff, and the water in front of it is so shallow that you can wade out a long way before it reaches your waist. That makes it good for families, and for anyone who wants a cool dip without the effort of swimming.

    The place began as a small fishing village, and the fishermen's cottages and the little harbour are still there, now surrounded by holiday flats and hotels. The harbour is a pleasant place to wander, with fishing cutters and yachts lined along the quay and a smokehouse nearby. The sea changes by the hour, from a flat blue to a choppy grey, and the sky is nearly always large and full of moving cloud. Gulls and terns wheel over the water, and in autumn flocks of geese pass overhead in long lines.

    Behind the beach lies a lagoon, the Sehlendorfer Binnensee, a protected area where many birds breed and rest, and it is a good place for a short walk. The land between the sea and the lagoon is low and open, with reeds, meadows and grazing cattle, and the road along the shore passes through it. Riding here is a matter of flat, open tarmac with the wind off the sea, and the main hazards are tourist cars, cyclists and the occasional drift of sand.

    If you want to swim, the main beach is lifeguarded in summer, and the water warms up quickly in a hot spell. The Baltic is nearly tideless and brackish, so it has no strong currents, but wind can raise a chop and you should watch the flags. Services are not on the beach itself beyond kiosks and cafés. For fuel and shops, Lütjenburg, about six kilometres inland, is the nearest town of any size, with petrol stations and supermarkets. Take sun cream, a towel and a layer for the evening. Sunset over the bay on a clear evening, with the water the colour of a peach, is a very good reason to linger.

  3. Heiligenhafen harbour food · 4 m

    The first thing you notice in Heiligenhafen is the smell, a mix of diesel, salt and smoked fish that tells you the harbour is still working. A small fleet of cutters ties up along the quay, and in the morning you may see men sorting the catch of herring, cod and flatfish before it goes to the fish shops and the stalls. The harbour sits at the end of a peninsula that pushes out into the Baltic, and the town spreads around it, with a seafront promenade, a long pier and a marina full of sailing boats.

    Fish is what to eat here, and it is simple. A smoked mackerel or herring from a stand, a Fischbrötchen with pickled herring or fried fish, or a plate of fresh plaice with fried potatoes in a harbourside restaurant are all fine choices, and the prices are fair. In the right season, the local speciality is smoked eel, though it divides opinion. Ice cream is available on every corner, and a coffee on a terrace by the water is a pleasure, though gulls will do their best to steal your lunch.

    Just outside town is a nature reserve of real value, the Graswarder, a long, low spit of sand, shingle and grass that curves out into the sea. It is a breeding and resting place for terns, avocets and other seabirds, and the lagoon behind it holds waders in migration. Access is limited during the breeding season in spring and summer, and it is worth keeping to the signed paths. In the distance, to the east, the great arch of the Fehmarn Sound Bridge rises over the water, linking the mainland to the island of Fehmarn.

    The roads in are flat and relaxed, crossing farmland with wide skies and the sea glinting at the edge of view. In summer the town is very busy, and traffic along the coast road can be slow, so a weekday or an early start makes the day easier. Heiligenhafen has about nine thousand people, so fuel stations, supermarkets and plenty of cafés and restaurants are all on hand. Parking a bike near the harbour is usually possible, though the quay cobbles are slippery with spray. Wind can make a cool day cold, so a windproof layer is wise.

  4. Fehmarn bridge view sight · 8 m

    Fehmarn is joined to the mainland by one of the most recognisable bridges in Germany, a steel arch about a kilometre long that spans the Fehmarnsund and carries both road and rail. Opened in 1963, it has an elegant, slightly improbable shape, and locals call it the coat hanger. The bridge was built as part of the so-called Bird Flight Line, a transport route linking Hamburg with Copenhagen via the island and a ferry to Denmark, and it has been the main way on and off Fehmarn ever since.

    A far bigger project is now taking shape just to the east. A road and rail tunnel is being built under the Fehmarn Belt to link Germany and Denmark directly, a project that has been discussed for decades and has already changed the look of the island's northern shore with construction sites and new roads. When it opens, the ferries that have crossed here for generations will lose most of their traffic.

    Riding across the bridge is a small thrill, though a windy one, since the crossing is exposed and gusts can be strong. Once on the island, you ride through a flat, open landscape of big fields, wind turbines, sheep grazing on the dikes and small brick villages, with the sea showing in glimpses on every side. The roads are straight and gently curved, and traffic is light except on summer weekends, when holidaymakers fill the beaches and the roads to them.

    Fehmarn is a cyclists' paradise, and you will share the road with many of them, so give them room. The island's main town, Burg, has fuel, supermarkets and cafés, and the whole island is just over a dozen thousand people. Wind is the dominant feature, so check the forecast and pick your direction carefully, as riding into a strong headwind on a flat road can be tiring. Keep your fuel stops simple: Burg is the best option, and the island is small enough that you will never be far from it.

  5. Eutin return lake scenic · 28 m

    The ride back toward Eutin at the end of the day has its own light. The sun is low, the colours deepen, and the lakes of Holstein Switzerland turn from blue to a deep pewter or a burnished gold, depending on the sky. The road comes in from the north through beech and farmland, past Kasseedorf and the small lakes, and the town appears with its church tower and palace roofs above the trees. On a still evening the water is like glass, and the only sound is the tick of the engine cooling.

    The lakes here were made by ice. The glaciers of the last ice age scoured out deep basins and left behind a jumble of hills, and the meltwater filled the hollows, creating the chain that now stretches from Eutin to Plön. The Great Eutin Lake is the biggest in the immediate neighbourhood, and the smaller lakes around it are linked by streams and channels. Reed beds line the banks and hold warblers and rails, while on the open water swans, coots and great crested grebes are easy to see. Late in the day, fishermen in small boats sit motionless, and rowers practise off the shore.

    For the riding, the return road is one of the gentlest in the state. The bends are open and smooth, the surface mostly good, and the gradients are no more than a long slope up from a lake or a short rise through a wood. The lanes around Eutin are popular with cyclists in the evening, so keep to a measured pace and give plenty of room. Deer come out of the woods at dusk, and a collision with one is a real risk, so scan the verges and ease off when the light fails.

    For services the town offers everything a tired rider needs: fuel stations at the edge, supermarkets, bakeries, and a variety of cafés and restaurants in the centre, with outdoor tables around the market in summer. With about seventeen thousand people, it is easy to find a bed if you decide to stay. A short walk down to the lake gives a view of the palace across the water, with its reflection in the evening light. Allow time for a drink at the water's edge before the last leg of the day.

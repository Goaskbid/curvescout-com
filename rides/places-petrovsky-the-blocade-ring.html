Why this ride

155 km from Lakhtinskiy to Lakhtinskiy, about 2 h 51 of riding. It goes by way of Vyborgskaya Road, Koltushskoe Highway, The Blocade Ring and back through Koltushskoe Highway. The line favours views over speed.

The stops

  1. 1. Petrovsky

    Start here.

    The label is a puzzle: Petrovsky could refer to any of a dozen places in a city named after Peter the Great, and it is safest to treat it as a viewpoint on his great project, the opening of Russia to the sea. Whatever the exact spot, the landscape is unmistakable: flat shore, reeds, wide grey water, and on the horizon the dome of the Kronstadt cathedral and the line of the flood barrier.

    Read the complete Petrovsky write-up.

  2. 2. Vyborgskaya Road

    23 km in.

    Every road out of Saint Petersburg to the north-west ends up in Finland or in the memory of it, and the Vyborg highway is the old way. It leaves the city through the northern suburbs of Shuvalovo, Pargolovo and Parnas, passing parks, lakes and rows of tower blocks, and then continues over the Karelian Isthmus to Vyborg, a town that belonged to Finland until 1940.

    Read the complete Vyborgskaya Road write-up.

  3. 3. Koltushskoe Highway

    53 km in.

    East of Saint Petersburg, the Koltushskoe highway runs through suburban sprawl and countryside toward the village of Koltushi, and it belongs to a memorial landscape that ties the whole region together: the Green Belt of Glory, a chain of monuments marking the defensive line around Leningrad during the siege of 1941 to 1944, when nearly nine hundred days of blockade cost the lives of hundreds of thousands of civilians. Monuments, steles and memorials stand along the roads where the front lines and supply routes ran, including the Road of Life across Lake Ladoga.

    Read the complete Koltushskoe Highway write-up.

  4. 4. The Blocade Ring

    79 km in.

    The Blockade Ring stands on the south bank of the Neva, east of Saint Petersburg, close to where the Road of Life began its journey toward Lake Ladoga. It commemorates the siege of Leningrad, which lasted from September 1941 to January 1944, roughly 872 days, and the corridor that kept the city alive. When Shlisselburg fell in September 1941, the city was cut off by land, and the only lifeline ran across Ladoga, by boat in summer and over the ice in winter.

    Read the complete The Blocade Ring write-up.

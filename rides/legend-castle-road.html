official tourism route. castles, historic towns, river valleys. From Mannheim it runs 390 km to Karlovy Vary; allow about 7 h 05 of riding. Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Mannheim

    Mannheim is a city laid out like a chessboard, and that tells you most of what you need to know about it. The old centre was planned in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries by the Electors Palatine, who built a vast baroque palace on the flat ground where the Neckar meets the Rhine and then arranged the streets in squares with letters and numbers instead of names. Locals call it the Quadratestadt. You will find that nobody needs a satnav to cross the middle of town, which is a rare luxury for a rider starting a long day.

  2. 2. Heidelberg

    Heidelberg is the place that German Romantics made famous and Mark Twain made funny. The Neckar comes out of its wooded valley here and slips past a steep old town, a stone bridge with a gate, and above everything the red sandstone ruins of the castle. Visitors have been climbing up to look at it since the early nineteenth century, when poets and painters arrived and decided it was the most beautiful ruin in Germany. The damage was done by French troops in the 1680s and 1690s, in a war of succession, and a lightning strike finished the job later, so it was never a romantic wreck by design.

  3. 3. Rothenburg ob der Tauber

    Rothenburg ob der Tauber looks like a film set because, for a long time, it had no choice. The medieval town stands on a ridge above the Tauber valley in Franconia, enclosed by an almost complete ring of walls with towers and gates, and its timber houses, steep roofs and cobbled lanes have survived because the town lost its wealth after the Thirty Years War and could not afford to rebuild. It was too poor to modernise, and so it stayed old. Today it draws visitors in large numbers, and the main streets can be packed with tour groups by mid-morning.

  5. 5. Bamberg

    Bamberg is one of those German cities that came through the twentieth century almost undamaged, so its old centre feels more like a seventeenth-century drawing than a place that has been repaired. It was listed by UNESCO in 1993, and it is easy to see why: it stretches across seven hills, like Rome, with the cathedral and the old bishop's residence on the highest, the Regnitz river looping through the middle, and rows of half-timbered houses and fishermen's cottages along the water. The little town hall sits on an artificial island in the river, reached by bridges, and has frescoes that seem to spill out over the walls.

  6. 6. Bayreuth

    Bayreuth is the town where one man's operas became a pilgrimage. Richard Wagner moved here in the 1870s and built a festival theatre on a hill outside the town, designed to his own ideas, with a hidden orchestra pit and a plain brick exterior. The first festival was held in 1876, and every summer since, thousands of people have made the trip to hear his operas. The tickets are so hard to obtain that the waiting list is said to be measured in years.

  7. 7. Karlovy Vary

    Legend has it that Emperor Charles IV was out hunting in the forests of western Bohemia in the fourteenth century when a stag he was chasing, or in some versions one of his hounds, fell into a pool of scalding water, and that is how the hot springs of Karlovy Vary were found. Whatever the truth, the town that grew up around them bears his name, Karlsbad in German, and by the nineteenth century it had become one of the most fashionable spas in Europe, visited by tsars, composers, poets and the cream of European society. In 2021, it became part of the UNESCO listing of the Great Spa Towns of Europe.

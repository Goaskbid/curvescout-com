Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Church of Santa Maria dell’Ammiraglio

    Santa Maria dell'Ammiraglio, known to everyone in Palermo as La Martorana, is a Norman church of the twelfth century that was founded in 1143 by George of Antioch, the admiral who served King Roger II. George was a Greek Christian from Syria, who had risen through the Arab administration of North Africa before moving to Sicily, and the church shows his taste, with Byzantine mosaics, Arabic inscriptions and a Norman bell tower. It was one of the first monuments of the Arab-Norman style, and is on the UNESCO list.

  2. 2. Cefalù Cathedral

    Cefalù is where Sicily squeezes a medieval town between a rock and a beach. The Rocca, a limestone crag, rises a steep couple of hundred metres straight behind the old centre, and the cathedral sits at its foot, a heavy, golden-grey Norman building with two square towers that you can see from the coastal highway long before you reach the town. According to tradition King Roger II promised to build it after surviving a storm at sea, and work began in 1131. Whatever the truth of the vow, the church is a statement of power by a Norman king who ruled Sicily and wanted it to look Mediterranean, not merely northern.

  3. 3. Villa Romana del Casale

    Somewhere in the fourth century AD, a very rich Roman, perhaps a senator or an imperial official, built a country villa in the hills of central Sicily and covered its floors with mosaics. The Villa Romana del Casale, just outside Piazza Armerina, has some of the finest and most extensive Roman floor mosaics anywhere, around 3,500 square metres, inscribed on the UNESCO list in 1997. Buried by a landslide in the Middle Ages, the villa was rediscovered and excavated mainly in the twentieth century, and the mosaics were preserved remarkably well.

Why this ride

Out of Neu Wulmstorf, one way: 336 km, roughly 6 h 40 in the saddle. The stops in between: Rotenburg and Nienburg river road. The climb of the day is Rinteln Weser bends. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hamburg south-west edge

    Hamburg does not end so much as thin out, and this is where it happens. Out here on the south-west edge, between the Elbe and the heath, the port's industrial yards give way to a landscape of commuter villages, motorway interchanges, fields and low wooded hills, and the town of Neu Wulmstorf, with about twenty thousand people, is the nearest place of any size. Behind you lies Harburg, once a separate town on the south bank of the river, and ahead is the flat green country of Lower Saxony.

  2. 2. Rotenburg fuel gate

    Rotenburg an der Wümme is a quiet district town of about twenty-two thousand people on the river Wümme, halfway between Hamburg and Bremen, and a convenient place to fill the tank on the way south. The landscape here is typical of northern Lower Saxony: flat or gently rolling, with heath and pine, wet meadows and the dark, boggy ground of the moors. Everything is green, and the sky is large.

  3. 3. Nienburg river road

    With some thirty thousand people, it is a proper town with fuel, supermarkets and cafes, and with a pleasant, slightly sleepy old centre of brick and half-timber. The river here is broad and slow, lined with willows and meadows, and it makes a good place for a stop and a flask of coffee.

  4. 4. Rinteln Weser bends

    Rinteln is a small Weser town of about twenty-eight thousand people, set where the river swings in a series of wide bends between the hills of the Weserbergland. The old town is full of half-timbered houses and handsome buildings in the Weser Renaissance style, a distinctive form of gables, bay windows and decorated stonework that spread along the river in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

  5. 5. Hameln food stop

    Everyone knows the story, and Hameln has not tried to hide it. According to the legend, in 1284 the town was overrun by rats, and a stranger in colourful clothes offered to rid it of them in return for payment. He played his pipe, the rats followed him into the Weser and drowned, and the town refused to pay. So he played again, and this time the town's children followed him out of the gate and were never seen again. The Brothers Grimm wrote it down, Robert Browning wrote a poem, and the Pied Piper became one of the best-known figures in European folklore. The historical record does record some tragedy in 1284, though what actually happened is still a mystery.

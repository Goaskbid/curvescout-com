Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Gia Long Tomb

    Emperor Gia Long, who founded the Nguyen dynasty and reunified Vietnam in 1802, chose a spot for his tomb about eleven kilometres from Hué, and he chose it far from the city on purpose. The Thien Tho hills rise south-west of the capital, and his burial ground lies tucked among them, a long, quiet complex of terraces, stone courtyards and guarding statues of mandarins and elephants. He died in 1820. His queen is buried beside him, which was unusual enough that guides still mention it.

  2. 2. Complex of Hué Monuments

    The Perfume River bends around the old capital of Vietnam like a hand around a cup, and the Citadel of Hué sits on its north bank behind moats and thick brick walls. This is the heart of the Complex of Hué Monuments, the seat of the Nguyen emperors, who ruled from here from the early nineteenth century until the last of them abdicated in 1945. Inside the walls is the Imperial City, and inside that the Forbidden Purple City, where only the court was allowed. The Ngo Mon gate, with its upper pavilion where proclamations were made, is the grand entrance, and it is worth walking through on foot rather than just photographing from the saddle.

  3. 3. Tran Hai Fortress

    Trấn Hải Thành sits at the sea mouth of the Perfume River's lagoon system, a low old fort looking out over the beach at Thuận An, about twelve kilometres from the centre of Huế. It was built in the early nineteenth century under Emperor Gia Long, the founder of the Nguyễn dynasty, and rebuilt and strengthened under his son Minh Mạng, to guard the one waterway by which a hostile fleet could reach the imperial capital. In the middle of the century French ships showed what that fear was worth, and the Nguyễn defences fell in 1883 when the French attacked the estuary forts.

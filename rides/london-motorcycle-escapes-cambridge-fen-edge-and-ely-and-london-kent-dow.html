Why this ride

A one-way ride of 832 km, Culverstone Green to Westerham - roughly 17 h 49 in the saddle. On the way it passes near Burnham-on-Crouch and Kidlington. The climbs: near Great Dunmow, Scenic stretch near Luton and near Hook. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Scenic road chapters

Dover White Cliffs → Folkestone coast

Slow enough to look. The surface follows the shoreline, so the corners arrive where the water decided, not where an engineer did. Water arrives beside the road and stays for 22 km. The line follows the shore because there is nowhere else for it to go.

  6. 6. Photo stop near Hook

    Hook is a busy Hampshire village built around a crossroads and a railway station, and it exists for most riders as a convenient refuelling stop just off junction 5 of the M3. It is not a place of great charm, but the countryside around it is pleasant, with woods, commons and old villages, and there are a few surprises close by.

  7. 7. Near Enfield Town

    The world's first cash machine was installed at a Barclays branch in Enfield in June 1967, and a blue plaque marks the spot. Enfield has another claim to fame for riders: the Royal Small Arms Factory at Enfield Lock made the Lee-Enfield rifle, and the town's name passed to the Royal Enfield motorcycle company, whose bikes are still built today, though now in India. The factory site has since been redeveloped into housing around the old buildings.

  9. 9. Westerham lanes

    Westerham is a small Kent town of about three thousand people, tucked into the Darent valley under the escarpment of the North Downs, and it has a bigger history than its size suggests. General James Wolfe, who captured Quebec in 1759, was born here, and his statue stands on the green opposite a statue of Winston Churchill, who lived at Chartwell, a few kilometres to the south. Squerryes Court, a seventeenth-century manor house with gardens, stands on the edge of town and has been home to the same family for centuries.

  10. 10. Canterbury food

    Canterbury asks for a pause even from people in a hurry. The cathedral towers over the roofs, the little River Stour slips under footbridges and old timber houses lean over the lanes, and the whole centre is wrapped in a medieval wall that still survives in long stretches. It was already a Roman town, and in 597 Augustine arrived from Rome to begin the English church here, so this is, in a sense, where English Christianity starts.

  11. 11. Dover White Cliffs

    Dover's cliffs are not just white, they are enormous: chalk faces about a hundred metres high, bright against the sea on a sunny day, the first and last piece of England for about a thousand years of travellers. This stop sits on the clifftop on the eastern side of town, where the road gives way to open grass, a National Trust area and a long view across the Strait of Dover. On a clear day you can see the French coast, about twenty miles away, as a thin pale line.

  12. 12. Folkestone coast

    Folkestone has quietly rearranged itself. A seaside resort that went through lean years has become a town of galleries, painted streets and a revived harbour, and it still has the long clifftop promenade, the Leas, laid out by the Victorians when the railway made this a place for the well-to-do. Today it is easy to spend an hour strolling there, watching the ships in the Channel, and it makes a sound pause before or after the road to Dover.

  13. 13. Royal Tunbridge Wells return

    Tunbridge Wells owes its existence to a rusty spring. In 1606 a young nobleman, Lord North, was resting in the woods here and noticed a pool with an orange scum; he tried the water, felt better and told the world. Within a few decades the court was coming down from London to drink it, and the Pantiles, a raised, colonnaded walk with trees along it, became the place to be seen taking the waters. The town was granted its Royal prefix in 1909 by Edward VII, and has been the butt of letters to the editor ever since, usually signed by someone disgusted.

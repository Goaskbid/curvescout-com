Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Alesund

    Ålesund spreads across a string of islands at the mouth of the fjords on Norway's north-west coast, and it looks unlike any other town in the country because a single night in January 1904 burned it down. A wind-whipped fire destroyed most of the wooden town, leaving thousands homeless, and the rebuilding took only a few years. The architects who did the work were young and fresh from study abroad, so the centre came out in stone and Art Nouveau, with towers, turrets and curling ornaments in every street. German Emperor Wilhelm, who had holidayed in the area, is remembered here for helping with relief supplies.

  2. 2. Geirangerfjord Area

    Geirangerfjord is a narrow arm of the sea that cuts into the mountains for about fifteen kilometres, and its walls climb straight up out of the water. Waterfalls drop down the cliffs, the best known being the Seven Sisters, and abandoned mountain farms cling to ledges that seem impossible to reach. The whole area is on the World Heritage list, and the reason is easy to grasp the first time you come around a bend and see it. The village at the head is small and in summer is crowded by cruise ships, which dwarf the houses and give the place a strange scale.

  3. 3. West Norwegian Fjords - Geirangerfjord and

    Here is the other side of Geirangerfjord, the one the tour brochures skip. High above the water, on the mountain called Åkneset, a huge slab of rock is slowly sliding towards the fjord. Tens of millions of cubic metres of it are monitored day and night by sensors, because if it ever fell in one go it could send a wave through the narrow fjord and the village at its head. The danger is real, the monitoring is serious, and the local authorities have plans for evacuation. It is the sort of fact that puts the postcard in a different light.

  4. 4. Urnes Stave Church

    The oldest stave church in Norway still standing stands on a ledge above a fjord, and you reach it by ferry. Urnes is on the north shore of Lustrafjord, the innermost arm of the great Sognefjord, and a small ferry crosses from Solvorn on the opposite shore in a few minutes, running regularly in the summer season. From the landing you walk up a path through farm fields to the church, which dates from around 1130 and is made of pine timber, black with tar, its shingled roofs layered up like a pagoda. Urnes was listed by UNESCO in 1979, among the first sites to be inscribed.

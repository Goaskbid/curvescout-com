Why this ride

A 102 km round trip from Kashihara - roughly 1 h 46 in the saddle. On the way it passes Richu-tenno-ryo Kofun, Hokki-ji Area and back through Richu-tenno-ryo Kofun. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Mozu-Furuichi Kofun Group: Mounded Tombs o

    Konda Gobyoyama is the name of the great tomb that dominates the Furuichi end of this group, and it is the burial mound attributed to the emperor Ojin, a ruler of semi-legendary standing whom the chronicles place around the end of the fourth century. At over four hundred metres long, it is the second largest tomb in Japan, bigger by volume than many pyramids, and beside it stands the shrine of Konda Hachimangu, whose forest and approach give the place a gentle atmosphere. The pairing is typical of Japan. A shrine to the war god Hachiman, who is identified with Ojin, sits beside an earthen tomb that nobody is permitted to enter.

  2. 2. Richu-tenno-ryo Kofun

    You cannot really see a kofun. That is the first lesson of Richu-tenno-ryo, one of the great keyhole-shaped tombs of the Mozu group in Sakai, south of Osaka. These are burial mounds built in the fifth century for the early rulers of Japan, shaped like a keyhole with a round back and a wide front, and surrounded by moats. The Richu mound is about three hundred and sixty metres long, among the largest in the country, and you walk around the water on a path, catching glimpses through the trees of a green hill that looks like a wooded island. From the ground it simply looks like a park.

  3. 3. Hokki-ji Area

    Ikaruga, a quiet town on the western edge of the Nara basin, is where Japanese Buddhist architecture begins. The temple of Hōryū-ji, founded by Prince Shōtoku in the early seventh century, has wooden buildings counted among the oldest surviving in the world, and in 1993 the Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area became, along with Himeji Castle, the first Japanese sites on the UNESCO list. A short distance north, among rice fields and houses, Hokki-ji is a smaller temple with a single treasure: a three-storey pagoda completed in the early eighth century, the oldest of its type in Japan. Seen across the fields with the hills behind, it is a lovely, peaceful sight.

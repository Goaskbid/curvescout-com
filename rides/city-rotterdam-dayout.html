Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Rotterdam

    Rotterdam is a young city by Dutch standards, and that is because the old one was destroyed. German bombers flattened the centre in May 1940, and rather than rebuild a historic town the Dutch built a modern one, bold and experimental, with towers, bridges and cubic houses that tilt on their corners. Nothing here is quiet or pretty in the postcard sense, and that is its appeal. The Erasmus Bridge, a white swan-necked span over the Maas, is the city's symbol, and the Markthal, a vast arched market hall with a ceiling painted in giant fruit and flowers, is the best place to eat. Cheap, quick, varied food is sold from stalls there, and Dutch herring, eaten raw with onion, is available at every street corner.

  2. 2. Woerden-Centrum

    Woerden is a small Dutch town on the Oude Rijn, the old Rhine, halfway between Utrecht and Leiden, in the middle of the Green Heart of Holland, a landscape of polders, meadows, ditches and cows. Its centre is a compact old town ringed by canals, with a castle, a big church and a windmill on the old ramparts. The Romans were here too: Woerden lies on the line of the Lower German Limes, and the remains of a Roman fort lie beneath the town centre.

  3. 3. Utrecht-Limes road | Zandweg

    Two thousand years ago the Rhine marked the northern edge of the Roman Empire here, and along its southern bank the army built a chain of forts, watchtowers and a road to link them. In the western suburbs of Utrecht, in the new district of Leidsche Rijn and the old village of De Meern, that road has been found again under the fields, a gravel and clay embankment with ditches either side, and part of it can be traced along the street called Zandweg. Nearby, at Castellum Hoge Woerd, the outline of a Roman fort has been rebuilt in a modern form, with a museum, a theatre and an observatory, and a flat-bottomed Roman river barge, found here in 1997 almost intact and complete with the cargo and personal belongings of its skipper, is on display. The Lower German Limes became a World Heritage site in 2021.

  4. 4. Utrecht-Limes road | Veldhuizen

    This is a ride along an old frontier that is now a residential suburb. When the Romans held the lower Rhine, the river itself was the border of the empire, and a chain of forts, watchtowers and roads ran along its south bank. One of the forts stood where Utrecht is now, under the name Traiectum, and another, with a fine reconstruction, lies in the modern district of Leidsche Rijn. The whole frontier, the Lower German Limes, was added to the World Heritage list in 2021. Today it is hard to see anything of it, because the Rhine silted up and shifted, and the land is mostly polder, pasture and new housing estate.

  5. 5. Utrecht-Limes road | De Balije

    The Romans built their northern frontier along the Rhine, and in what is now the Netherlands they built a road beside it, running on the natural levee of the river, which was the highest and driest ground. Today the Kromme Rijn, the Crooked Rhine, flows past Utrecht as a small, quiet river, but in the first century it was the main channel, and the road ran alongside it, linking forts and watchtowers from the sea to the Roman towns upstream. At Utrecht there was a fort, Traiectum, from which the city takes its name, and nearby at Vechten stood the larger fort of Fectio, one of the most important along the Dutch stretch of the frontier.

Why this ride

From Siem Reap it runs 306 km to Siem Reap; allow about 5 h 17 of riding. On the way it passes Roluos, Banteay Srei, Koh Ker: Archaeological Site of Ancient Li and back through Banteay Srei. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Angkor

    Angkor is bigger than most visitors expect, and its scale only starts to make sense once you realise that it was a city, not a temple. The great complex north of Siem Reap, in north-western Cambodia, was the capital of the Khmer Empire from the ninth century to the fifteenth, and at its height it covered hundreds of square kilometres, with reservoirs, canals, roads and houses of wood that have long since vanished. What survives is the stone: Angkor Wat, built in the early twelfth century by King Suryavarman II as a temple to Vishnu, with its five towers, long galleries of carved relief and a moat that mirrors the sunrise, and Angkor Thom, the walled city built by Jayavarman VII, with the Bayon and its dozens of towers carved with huge, smiling faces.

  2. 2. Roluos

    Roluos is where the story of Angkor begins. About thirteen kilometres south-east of Siem Reap, along the road toward Phnom Penh, a cluster of brick and sandstone temples marks Hariharalaya, the capital of the Khmer kings in the late ninth century. Before the great monuments of Angkor Wat and the Bayon were built, the rulers lived and built here, and the three main temples show the first mature forms of Khmer architecture. Preah Ko was dedicated in 879 by Indravarman I, and Bakong, a few years later, was the first great temple mountain, a stepped pyramid with elephants in stone at the corners, representing Mount Meru, the home of the gods.

  3. 3. Banteay Srei

    Most people come to Angkor for the giants, Angkor Wat and the Bayon, but the temple that many remember best is small, pink and astonishingly intricate. Banteay Srei, the "citadel of the women", stands about twenty-five kilometres northeast of the main Angkor group, and it was consecrated in 967, earlier than the great royal temples. It was built not by a king but by a high-ranking courtier, the Brahmin Yajnavaraha, and dedicated to Shiva. Its reddish sandstone is fine-grained and easy to carve, and the result is decoration so delicate it looks almost like woodwork: lintels with scenes from Hindu epics, female guardian deities in niches, and foliage spiralling across every surface.

  4. 4. Koh Ker: Archaeological Site of Ancient Li

    For a few short decades in the tenth century, the capital of the Khmer empire was not at Angkor but here, deep in the forest of northern Cambodia. King Jayavarman IV established his court at Koh Ker, then called Lingapura, and in less than twenty years his builders raised dozens of temples across a wide area, before the capital returned to Angkor. The jungle took the city back. Today Koh Ker is a scatter of brick and sandstone sanctuaries among the trees, and in 2023 it was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list.

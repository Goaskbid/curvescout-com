Why this ride

From Montealegre del Castillo it runs 189 km to Montealegre del Castillo; allow about 3 h 16 of riding. It goes by way of Cueva del Peliciego, Abrigo de los Cortijos, Abrigo del Paso and back through Abrigo de los Cortijos. The highest point is about 800 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cantos de la Visera II

    Monte Arabí rises out of the plains of the Altiplano like an upturned boat, a long ridge of pale rock above a country of vines and cereal fields, and the Cantos de la Visera are among the shelters that gather round its flanks. Cantos de la Visera II is one of two shelters bearing that name, and it holds Levantine paintings that are part of the UNESCO list of Mediterranean rock art. The shelters sit in the rock of the Visera, an overhanging outcrop, giving shelter from the rain and sun that has kept the paint alive.

  2. 2. Cueva del Peliciego

    Jumilla is wine. This town in the north of Murcia sits in a high, dry valley surrounded by bare mountains, and its vineyards, planted with the Monastrell grape, produce powerful red wines under the Jumilla designation of origin, one of the oldest in Spain. The Cueva del Peliciego, a rock shelter in the hills near the town, holds prehistoric paintings in the Levantine style, and it has been known since the early twentieth century, when it was one of the first rock art sites studied in the region.

  3. 3. Abrigo de los Cortijos

    Hellín is a town in the south-east of Albacete province, on the edge of the dry plains of La Mancha, where the landscape breaks into hills and ravines on its way down to the Segura river. Its old town climbs a hill crowned by a castle, and its streets are lined with houses from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The Abrigo de los Cortijos lies in the hills about nine kilometres from the town, toward Albatana and Tobarra.

  4. 4. Abrigo del Paso

    Calasparra is a rice town, which is not what most people expect of inland Murcia. The rice grown here, in terraced paddies along the Segura and Mundo rivers, was the first in Spain to receive a protected designation of origin, and it is prized for absorbing flavour without turning to mush, so paella cooks prefer it. The paddies shimmer green in summer and gold in autumn, with herons and egrets feeding in the flooded fields, and behind them rise bare limestone hills, so the effect is of a patch of Asia dropped onto a dry Spanish landscape.

  1. 1. Würzburg Residence with the Court Gardens

    Balthasar Neumann was a military engineer before he was an architect, and it shows in the Würzburg Residence, a palace so confidently engineered that its great staircase vault, with no central support, was said to be impossible. It was built for the prince-bishops of Würzburg in the early eighteenth century, with the main construction running from about 1720 to the mid-1740s. The ceiling above the stairs carries a fresco by Giovanni Battista Tiepolo, which is among the largest in the world and depicts the four continents. UNESCO listed the whole ensemble in 1981, court gardens included.

  2. 2. Watchtower WP 7/37

    The Romans liked straight lines, and nowhere more than here. On the edge of Walldürn, in the hilly country between the Odenwald and the Tauber valley, runs the course of the Upper Germanic-Raetian Limes, the frontier wall that marked the edge of the Roman Empire across what is now southern Germany. Watchtower WP 7/37 is one of hundreds of small posts that lined it, each within sight of the next, so that signals could be passed along the frontier. The Limes here famously runs almost dead straight for many kilometres, regardless of hills and valleys, a statement of Roman confidence and surveying skill.

  3. 3. Watchtowers WP 8/31 to 8/41

    Somewhere between Walldürn and the hills north of Stuttgart, the Roman surveyors drew a line on the map and simply refused to bend it. For about eighty kilometres, the Upper Germanic Limes runs almost perfectly straight across hill and valley, one of the most remarkable feats of ancient surveying in Europe. The watchtowers WP 8/31 to 8/41 near Osterburken and Adelsheim lie on that straight stretch, and like the rest of the frontier they are part of the UNESCO Frontiers of the Roman Empire. Most are now low mounds in fields and woods, but here and there you will find a reconstructed tower or a stretch of wall to give you an idea of what it looked like.

  4. 4. Watchtowers WP 9/14 to 9/32 including the

    For about eighty kilometres through northern Württemberg, the Roman frontier runs in a nearly perfectly straight line, ignoring hills, valleys and rivers as if someone had drawn it with a ruler on a map. Surveyors still argue about how the Romans managed it. The stretch with watchtowers 9/14 to 9/32, near Öhringen in the Hohenlohe region, lies along this remarkable line, and Öhringen itself grew from Roman forts, two of them, which guarded the frontier here. The line is part of the UNESCO Frontiers of the Roman Empire, and the Limes trail follows it through fields and forests.

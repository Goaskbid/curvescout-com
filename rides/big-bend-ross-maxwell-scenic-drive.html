Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Panther Junction

    Panther Junction is the headquarters of Big Bend National Park, a modest cluster of buildings, a visitor centre, a post office and a fuel station in the middle of the Chihuahuan Desert, on the road that connects the park's entrance with the Chisos Mountains. It sits at a little under four thousand feet, in a broad basin of creosote and yucca, with the jagged Chisos range to the south and a vast sky overhead. The park is one of the most remote in the lower forty-eight, and the nearest sizeable towns are a long way off.

  2. 2. Sotol Vista

    Sotol Vista is where Big Bend National Park shows you how much of it there is. The overlook sits on a rise beside the Ross Maxwell Scenic Drive, named for the park's first superintendent, and the view runs south and west across a basin of pale desert with the Chisos Mountains standing behind you and the far-off notch of Santa Elena Canyon on the horizon. The land is open Chihuahuan Desert, and in every direction you see more of it. There is nothing between you and the Rio Grande but space, heat and the occasional hawk.

  3. 3. Mule Ears

    Two rock horns stand up from the desert floor beside the road, close together and slightly tilted, and anybody who has ever seen a mule will recognise them instantly. The Mule Ears are the remains of volcanic dikes, sheets of molten rock that forced their way into cracks in the earth millions of years ago, hardened, and were then exposed as the softer rock around them wore away. They rise roughly a thousand feet above the surrounding plain and dominate this section of Big Bend National Park's Ross Maxwell Scenic Drive.

  4. 4. Santa Elena Canyon

    At the far south-western end of the Ross Maxwell Scenic Drive, the Rio Grande runs straight into a wall of limestone and cuts through it. Santa Elena Canyon is a gap in the Mesa de Anguila, a long cliff of rock, with sheer walls about fifteen hundred feet high on either side, and the river, wide and brown in the open desert, turns narrow and green and slow inside it. Mexico is on one bank and Texas on the other. A short trail climbs a few steps from the parking area, crosses the mouth of Terlingua Creek, which may be dry or may be a knee-deep wade after rain, and then follows the river into the shade between the cliffs.

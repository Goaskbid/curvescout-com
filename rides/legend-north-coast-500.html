Why this ride

A loop of the Scottish Highlands from Inverness. A one-way ride of 487 km, Inverness to Inverness - roughly 8 h 51 in the saddle. On the way it passes North Coast 500, Ullapool and Golspie. The line favours views over speed.

Scenic road chapters

North Coast 500

Slow enough to look. The surface follows the shoreline, so the corners arrive where the water decided, not where an engineer did. Water arrives beside the road and stays for 4 km. The line follows the shore because there is nowhere else for it to go.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Inverness

    Inverness is where the North Coast 500 starts and ends, the circuit that has turned the top of Scotland into a bucket-list road, and the sensible traveller treats the city as a place to arrive, wash, shop and sleep before heading out. It sits where the River Ness meets the Moray Firth, and for centuries it was the capital of the Highlands in practice, a market town at the junction of roads running north, west and south. The population of about forty-seven thousand makes it by far the largest settlement for a hundred miles in any direction, and that means all the fuel, tyres, spares and supermarkets you could want.

  2. 2. North Coast 500 point 2

    Applecross sits at the end of a road that most of Scotland would rather not drive. The village faces west across the Inner Sound to the Isle of Raasay and the Cuillin ridge of Skye, a row of whitewashed houses along a sheltered bay, and its Gaelic name, A' Chomraich, means the sanctuary. In 673 an Irish monk, Maelrubha, founded a monastery here, and the sanctuary around it was a place of refuge for centuries. The hills of Skye fill the view across the water, though Portree is a very long way off by road.

  3. 3. North Coast 500 point 3

    The Bealach na Bà is the nearest thing Britain has to an Alpine pass. Its name, the Pass of the Cattle, comes from the drovers who once took their herds over it, and the road that now follows the old route climbs from sea level at Tornapress to about 626 metres in just a few kilometres, with gradients of up to one in five. The road was opened in 1822. Applecross, down the far side, is only a handful of kilometres from the summit.

  4. 4. Ullapool

    Ullapool is a white-painted fishing village laid out on a grid, which in the Highlands is as unusual as a roundabout. The British Fisheries Society planned it in 1788 as a base for herring boats on the shore of Loch Broom, and the long straight streets facing the water reflect that planning. Today about fifteen hundred people live here, the harbour is busy with creel boats and the ferry to Stornoway on Lewis, and in summer the quay fills with visitors eating chips from paper and looking out to the hills.

  5. 5. North Coast 500 point 5

    Durness is the last village on the north-west corner of mainland Scotland, a scatter of crofts on a limestone headland, and it is an oasis of green in a landscape of peat and gneiss. The limestone is the reason: it breaks down into sweet, fertile soil, and the grass here grows lush where the surrounding moor is bare. Smoo Cave, a short way east, is a huge sea cave whose entrance is among the biggest in Britain, with a waterfall dropping into the inner chamber. There is no large town for miles, only the villages of the far north coast.

  6. 6. North Coast 500 point 6

    John o' Groats is the place people talk about, and it is, in truth, a modest collection of buildings at the north-east corner of Scotland. The name is said to come from Jan de Groot, a Dutchman who is said to have run a ferry to Orkney centuries ago, and the village has long marked the end of the long journey from Land's End, 874 miles away, a pilgrimage for cyclists and walkers. The famous signpost by the harbour, with its distances, is the usual photograph. Wick and Thurso, the nearest towns, are some twenty-five kilometres off.

  7. 7. Near Golspie

    Just north of Golspie, on the east coast of Sutherland, a fairytale castle rises above the sea. Dunrobin Castle, seat of the Earls and later Dukes of Sutherland, looks like something from the Loire, all conical turrets and white walls, because in the nineteenth century the architect Charles Barry, who also designed the Houses of Parliament, remodelled it in a French-Scottish style. Its formal gardens step down toward the shore. It is one of the grandest houses in the Highlands, and it is open to visitors in the warmer months.

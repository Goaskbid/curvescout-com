Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Istanbul

    Istanbul sits across two continents, split by the Bosporus, and the point here is in the old peninsula on the European side, where the Golden Horn meets the strait. The city was Byzantium, then Constantinople, capital of the Roman Empire in the east for over a thousand years, and from 1453 the seat of the Ottoman sultans. The layers show everywhere. Hagia Sophia, finished in 537, still has the most astonishing dome in the world, hung as if from nothing over a vast interior, and nearby the Blue Mosque, with its six minarets and cascade of domes, stands across a garden, built in the early seventeenth century as an Ottoman answer.

  2. 2. Hüdavendigar

    Bursa was the first capital of the Ottomans, and the Hüdavendigar Mosque in the hot-spring suburb of Çekirge is one of the earliest great buildings of the dynasty. Murad I, who conquered large parts of Thrace and the Balkans in the fourteenth century, took the title Hüdavendigâr, meaning sovereign, and the mosque complex that carries the name was built in the second half of that century. It has an unusual plan, with an upper floor that served as a school, and the shapes already show a style that would grow into the classical Ottoman mosque.

  4. 4. Muradiye

    Bursa was the first great capital of the Ottomans, taken in 1326 and held for over a century before the court moved to Edirne, and the Muradiye district is where the sultans of that period are buried. The complex was begun in 1426 by Sultan Murad II, a mosque with a medrese and a garden, and around it are a dozen or so tombs of sultans and their families, set among cypress trees and roses. The tombs are plain from outside and surprisingly lovely inside, with painted ceilings, tiled walls and a row of simple sarcophagi. One of them holds the sons of Mehmed the Conqueror, and the stories of the princes buried here, some of them victims of palace power struggles, have their own sad drama.

  1. 1. Halifax

    Halifax is a harbour first and a city second, and every part of its history follows from that. The British founded it in 1749 as a naval counterweight to the French fortress at Louisbourg, and the big grassy hill in the middle, Citadel Hill, still carries a star-shaped fort with kilted guards and a noon gun. The harbour below is one of the largest natural ice-free ports in the world, which made the city a base for convoys in both world wars.

  2. 2. Chester

    Chester looks like the kind of place where sailors have retired to tinker with boats for a century, and that is more or less true. It sits on a protected corner of Mahone Bay, with hundreds of small islands scattered across the water in front of it, and wealthy New Englanders began building summer houses here in the nineteenth century after settlers from the American colonies founded the town around 1760. The result is a handsome village of white clapboard and tall trees, with a few shops, a playhouse and a yacht club that organises Chester Race Week every August.

  3. 3. Lunenburg

    Lunenburg is the most colourful working fishing town you are likely to ride into. The old centre, laid out on a grid in 1753 by British authorities settling Protestants from Germany, Switzerland and the French-speaking Montbéliard region, climbs a steep hill above the harbour, and every wooden house seems to have been painted a different bright colour. UNESCO recognised it as a World Heritage Site in 1995 as the best surviving planned British colonial settlement in North America. Many houses have a small projecting dormer above the front door, known locally as the Lunenburg bump.

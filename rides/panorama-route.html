Why this ride

It goes by way of Gods Window, Bourkes Luck Potholes and Three Rondavels. The highest point is about 1,500 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Graskop

    Graskop perches right on the lip of the Drakensberg escarpment in Mpumalanga, where the highveld plateau drops about a thousand metres to the lowveld below, and nearly everything worth seeing in the area is a view. It is a small tourist town with craft stalls, cafes and guesthouses, and its position at the top of the Panorama Route makes it the natural base. Locals call the edge here the Berg, and when the mist comes up from the lowveld in the morning it fills the gaps between the forested spurs like milk.

  2. 2. Gods Window

    God's Window is a viewpoint at the edge of the Drakensberg escarpment in Mpumalanga, and the name tells you what to expect. The cliff falls away almost a kilometre to the Lowveld below, a flat, hazy plain of bush and farmland that stretches east toward Kruger National Park and the Mozambique border. On a clear morning you can see an astonishing distance, and on a misty one the whole plain vanishes under cloud and you are left standing on the roof of a white sea. Weather is capricious here, and even in summer a fog can swallow the view within minutes, so give yourself a margin.

  3. 3. Bourkes Luck Potholes

    Where the Treur River meets the Blyde, the water has carved a set of smooth, round potholes into the rock, some of them deep enough to swallow a car, and the effect is a little like looking into the inside of a giant's pocket. The potholes are cylindrical bowls of yellow, orange and grey dolomite, created over thousands of years by whirlpools spinning sand and pebbles against the riverbed. A series of footbridges and paths lets visitors look down into them from above, with the river rushing through the channels below.

  4. 4. Three Rondavels

    Three huge, round-topped pillars of rock stand along the rim of the Blyde River Canyon, and local people liken them to the rondavels, the round thatched huts of traditional African homesteads, which is how they got their name. Each is a dome of dark rock, capped with grass and bush, rising hundreds of metres above the water. Local tradition links them to the wives of a chief, and the place is treated with some respect.

  5. 5. Blyde River Canyon

    Few canyons carry such cheerful names. When Voortrekkers found the river in the 1840s, they had left the Delagoa Bay fever lowlands in sorrow, fearing their leader was dead; they called a stream Treur, meaning mourning, and when they met the party returning safely, the next river became Blyde, meaning joy. The Blyde River Canyon, in Mpumalanga, is the result: a gorge about twenty-five kilometres long and up to seven hundred metres deep, cut into the northern end of the Drakensberg escarpment and clothed in green subtropical vegetation, making it one of the largest green canyons on Earth.

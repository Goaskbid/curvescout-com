Why this ride

A 183 km loop that starts and ends near Lake Charles, about 2 h 57 of riding. It goes by way of Sulphur, Holly Beach, Cameron and Creole. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Creole Nature Trail

Water arrives beside the road and stays for 16 km. The line follows the shore because there is nowhere else for it to go — slow enough to look. The surface follows the shoreline, so the corners arrive where the water decided, not where an engineer did.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Lake Charles

    Lake Charles is the gateway to the Creole Nature Trail, an All-American Road that loops south from the city across the marshes of southwest Louisiana to the Gulf. It is a flat, low, watery place where the horizon is mostly sky and the road often runs on a raised bank between bayou and rice field. This is Cajun country, and you notice it first in the language on the radio and the boudin sold at gas stations, a rice-and-pork sausage that locals eat on the way to work and consider a proper breakfast.

  2. 2. Sulphur

    Sulphur is the working front door of Louisiana's Cajun coast, a refinery and chemical town in Calcasieu Parish a few minutes from Lake Charles. It may not look like the start of a nature trail, and that is part of the point. The industrial flares and stacks give way, a short ride south, to marsh, bayou, and open Gulf, and the contrast is the story of this corner of Louisiana.

  3. 3. Holly Beach

    Holly Beach has the odd distinction of being a beach town without much of a town. It is a long ribbon of sand along the Louisiana Gulf coast, backed by a scattering of raised beach houses and camps on stilts, and it has been called the Cajun Riviera by people with an affectionate sense of humour. The sand is brown, the water is warm and cloudy, and the horizon is full of oil platforms. It is not a postcard, and it is all the more honest for that.

  4. 4. Cameron

    Cameron is a small town with a very large story. It sits on the Louisiana coast where the Calcasieu Ship Channel reaches the Gulf, and the first thing most visitors do is cross the water on the free ferry, a short, slightly comic ride that carries cars, trucks, and motorcycles over to the east bank. On a clear day you can see the shipping lanes and the long, low shape of the marsh, and a pelican usually appears as if on cue.

  5. 5. Creole

    Creole is a village you ride through almost without noticing, and yet it belongs to one of the most unusual landscapes in the United States. East of the Texas border, along the Louisiana coast, the marsh is broken by low ridges of oak and live oak that rise a metre or two above the flat. These are cheniers, the old beach ridges left behind as the Mississippi's sediment pushed the coast outward. Highway 27 follows some of them, and the live oaks bent by Gulf winds are one of the real sights of the Creole Nature Trail.

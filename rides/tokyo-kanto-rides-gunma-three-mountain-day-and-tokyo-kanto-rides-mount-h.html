Why this ride

680 km round from Machida - plan on about 12 h 22 of riding. On the way it passes near Ōiso, Kimitsu and Ishige. The climbs: near Ichihara and near Sagamihara.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Fuel stop near Machida

    The northern part of Machida climbs into the Tama Hills, where the old ridge roads once linked villages scattered through woods and valleys. Today much of the area is covered by Tama New Town, one of the largest housing developments in Japan, built from the 1960s onwards, but the ridges still offer views, and pockets of woodland, temples and farms survive between the estates.

  2. 2. Food stop near Ōiso

    Inland from the coast at Ōiso, the land rises into a line of low, green hills between Hiratsuka and Ninomiya, and from their tops you look out over Sagami Bay, the Shonan coast and, on clear days, Mount Fuji and the Hakone mountains. The Shonan-daira hilltop park, above Hiratsuka, has an observation tower with a panoramic view, and in Ninomiya, Azumayama Park is famous for its early-spring display of rapeseed flowers with Fuji in the background.

  3. 3. Kimitsu

    Kimitsu runs from the industrial shore of Tokyo Bay deep into the forested hills of the inner Boso peninsula, and the contrast is striking. On the coast, the Nippon Steel works stretches for kilometres, a tangle of furnaces, chimneys and cranes. Inland, within half an hour, you are among steep green hills, narrow valleys and reservoirs, with hardly a building in sight.

  4. 4. High point near Ichihara

    A layer of rock in a riverbank near Ichihara has given its name to a slice of Earth's history. In 2020, the international body that decides such things named a geological age the Chibanian, after a cliff on the Yōrō river where the last reversal of the Earth's magnetic field is recorded with unusual clarity. The site is small and muddy, reached by a short path, but standing in front of it is a strange experience, a few hundred thousand years of time in a single bank of clay.

  5. 5. Near Ishige, on the way back

    The Kinugawa river looks placid enough as it slides through the rice fields of south-western Ibaraki, but in September 2015, swollen by days of torrential rain, it breached its levee at Jōsō and flooded a vast area of the plain, forcing thousands to flee and leaving towns under water for days. Driving the levee road today, high above the fields, you can see how flat and vulnerable the land is.

  6. 6. Photo stop in Kukichūō

    Kuki is the kind of place that rarely appears in travel guides, a pleasant, low-rise city in the flat farmland of north-eastern Saitama, crossed by railways, rivers and expressways. Kukichūō is its central district, with shops, homes and offices, and it makes a practical stop on the long run north from Tokyo towards the mountains of Gunma and Tochigi.

  7. 7. Near Tokyo

    When the poet Bashō set out in 1689 on the journey that became The Narrow Road to the Deep North, he left from Senju, on the Sumida river, where friends saw him off. This finish lies in that same old northern edge of Edo, in Arakawa ward, a neighbourhood of narrow streets, small workshops and wooden houses that still feels more like shitamachi, the old downtown, than the glassy Tokyo of Shinjuku and Roppongi.

    Machida is a big commuter city on the south-western edge of Tokyo, poking into Kanagawa prefecture like a thumb, so that locals joke about whether it really belongs to Tokyo at all. It is busy, built-up and practical, with department stores, train stations and endless convenience stores, and it makes a straightforward starting point for rides into the mountains to the west.

    Sagamihara is a big city on the western edge of the Tokyo sprawl, but it stretches surprisingly far into the mountains. Its western part takes in the Tanzawa foothills, Lake Sagami and Lake Tsukui, two reservoirs on the Sagami river surrounded by forested hills, and the change from suburb to mountain happens quickly.

Springs emerge at its foot, feeding small streams like the Nogawa, and some of the paths along them feel almost rural, with bamboo groves, old trees and the sound of running water.

  12. 12. Scenic stretch in Ageoshimo

    Ageoshimo is a district of Ageo, a commuter city in Saitama prefecture, north of Tokyo, on the flat Omiya plateau between the Arakawa and Ayase rivers. In the Edo period, Ageo was a post town on the Nakasendō, one of the five great highways that radiated from Edo, linking the capital with Kyoto through the mountains of central Japan. Travellers, merchants and feudal lords on their way to and from the capital stayed in its inns.

  13. 13. Photo stop near Satte

    Every spring, along an embankment in Satte, about a thousand cherry trees bloom at the same time as fields of yellow rapeseed beneath them, and the combination of pink and yellow draws huge crowds. Gongendō Park, on the levee of an old river, is the place, and if you happen to ride through in late March or early April, it is a sight to remember. The rest of the year, Satte is a quiet town in the flat farmland of north-eastern Saitama.

  14. 14. Near Ōami, on the way back

    It is farming country: rice paddies, vegetable fields, greenhouses and patches of woodland, with low hills to the south and a scattering of small towns. Chiba is famous for its peanuts, and in autumn the fields are dotted with drying stacks, called bocchi, a sight unique to this part of Japan.

  15. 15. Food stop near Kisarazu

    Kisarazu is the Chiba end of the Tokyo Bay Aqua-Line, and for riders that alone makes it special. The Aqua-Line crosses the bay from Kawasaki in two halves: a long tunnel under the water, then a low bridge that skims across the surface for several kilometres into Kisarazu. In the middle sits Umihotaru, an artificial island with a parking area, restaurants and a deck with views over the bay, where on a clear day you can see Mount Fuji to the west and the towers of Tokyo and Yokohama to the north. It is tolled, and crosswinds on the bridge section can be fierce.

Why this ride

A 420 km round trip from Athens - roughly 7 h 14 in the saddle. It goes by way of Sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus, Archaeological Site of Mystras and back through Sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Acropolis

    The rock itself is a surprise. The Acropolis rises about a hundred and fifty metres above Athens, a limestone outcrop with steep sides and a flat top, and for most of ancient history it was the city's fortress and shrine. The buildings you see today, the Parthenon, the Propylaia gateway, the little temple of Athena Nike and the Erechtheion with its stone maidens, were mostly built in the second half of the fifth century BC under Pericles, with the sculptor Phidias overseeing the work. The Parthenon was finished in 432 BC.

  2. 2. Sanctuary of Asklepios at Epidaurus

    Drop a coin in the centre of the orchestra at Epidaurus and people sitting in the top row, fifty-odd rows above, will hear it. The theatre of the sanctuary of Asklepios, built in the fourth century BC, is famous for its acoustics and its almost perfect state of preservation, and it still hosts performances of ancient drama during the summer festival. But the theatre was only part of the sanctuary. Epidaurus was the most famous healing centre of the ancient Greek world, where the sick came to sleep in the sacred buildings and hope that the god Asklepios would visit them in a dream with a cure.

  3. 3. Archaeological Site of Mystras

    Mystras is a whole Byzantine city draped down a steep hillside, a few kilometres west of modern Sparta, at the foot of the Taygetos mountains. It began in 1249, when William II of Villehardouin, a Frankish prince ruling the Peloponnese after the Fourth Crusade, built a castle on the summit. Within a few years the Byzantines had taken it back, and Mystras grew into the capital of the Despotate of the Morea, a centre of learning and art in the last centuries of the empire. The philosopher Gemistos Plethon taught here, and in 1449 Constantine XI Palaiologos, the last Byzantine emperor, was crowned here before leaving for Constantinople, where he died when the city fell in 1453.

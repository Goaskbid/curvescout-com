Why this ride

official wine route. vineyards, cellars, rolling hills. A one-way ride of 77 km, Reims to Château-Thierry - roughly 1 h 24 in the saddle. The stops in between: Verzenay and Épernay. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Reims

    For over a thousand years the kings of France came to Reims to be crowned, and the cathedral that still dominates the city shows why. The tradition goes back to Clovis, baptised here around the year 500 by Bishop Remigius, and some thirty-odd monarchs followed, ending with Charles X in 1825. The present Gothic building dates from the thirteenth century, its west front crowded with statues, among them the Smiling Angel, and it was damaged badly by German shelling in the First World War before being painstakingly restored. Inside, stained glass by Marc Chagall glows blue behind the apse, installed in the 1970s.

  2. 2. Verzenay

    Verzenay is a Champagne village that decided to build a lighthouse in the middle of a sea of vines. The Phare de Verzenay, put up in 1909 by a local Champagne merchant as an advertisement, stands among the vineyards on the northern slope of the Montagne de Reims, a long way from any actual coast. It is a delightful piece of folly, and you can climb it for a view across the rows of Pinot Noir to the plain of the Marne and the spire of Reims cathedral on a clear day. A small museum inside tells the story of the vine.

  3. 3. Épernay

    Épernay is the sort of town that makes a bubble feel like an institution. It sits on the Marne river, in the heart of Champagne, and its main street, the Avenue de Champagne, is lined with the grand houses of famous producers: Moët and Chandon, Perrier-Jouët, Pol Roger, Mercier and others, in mansions of stone, brick and wrought iron, set behind gardens and gates. The avenue is sometimes called the most expensive street in the world, a joke based on the value of the bottles stored beneath it.

  5. 5. Château-Thierry

    Château-Thierry lies along the Marne at a point where the river valley narrows and the hills close in, and it is the town where the poet Jean de La Fontaine was born in 1621. His house is now a museum, and the fables he spun about foxes, crows and cunning grasshoppers are still learned by schoolchildren across France. Above the river, the ruins of a medieval castle crown the hillside, with walls and towers that once guarded the road to Paris, and they give a good view over the roofs and the water.

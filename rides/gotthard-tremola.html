Why this ride

From Airolo it runs 27 km to Andermatt; allow about 28 min of riding. The stops in between: Tremola lower hairpins, Tremola upper hairpins and Gotthard Pass. The highest point is about 2,300 m. Gotthard Pass is subject to seasonal closure - check it is open before you set off. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Furka Pass

The last of the trees go and the road runs out onto open ground. The summit sits at 2106 m, and what surrounds it is rock, weather and distance. Cold comes early up here and stays late. Ride it with something in reserve: the summit is not the end of the work, only the middle of it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Airolo

    Airolo sits at the southern mouth of the Gotthard, the point where northern and southern Europe seem to hand over to each other in a single valley. At 1,175 metres it is a small town of about fifteen hundred people, and the first sign of Italian Switzerland if you come from Andermatt: the roofs turn to stone, the stucco gets warmer, and the language changes to Italian. It has fuel, cafes, a couple of bakeries and a railway station, and it is a natural place to start or finish a day among the passes.

  2. 2. Tremola lower hairpins

    The Tremola is the old road up the south side of the Gotthard, and its lower hairpins are where the cobbles begin. Built between 1827 and 1832, when the Gotthard became a proper carriage route, it climbs out of the Ticino valley above Airolo in a staircase of twenty-four tight bends, laid with granite setts and held up by dry-stone walls. The setts are rounded and worn, and in the morning dew or after rain they are slippery in a way that tarmac riders forget until the front wheel reminds them.

  3. 3. Tremola upper hairpins

    Higher up the Tremola, the hairpins lose their trees. The old Gotthard road climbs out of the last of the stunted spruce and into bare granite and tussock grass, and the walls that hold up the bends rise in neat steps, one above another, with the tiny shapes of other riders and walkers moving along them. This is the part of the road that appears on postcards, and it deserves it. From a bend near the top you can look straight down on three or four hairpins at once, laid out like the folds of a ribbon.

  4. 4. Gotthard Pass

    The Gotthard Pass is the pass that Switzerland is built around. At 2,106 metres, it lies in the middle of the country on the great watershed between the Alps' northern and southern slopes, and four of Europe's major rivers rise within a few kilometres: the Rhine, the Rhône, the Reuss and the Ticino. Armies, merchants, pilgrims and, today, motorcyclists have been crossing it for eight centuries, since the Schöllenen gorge was opened by a daring bridge in the thirteenth century, and the pass became the shortest link between Germany and Italy.

  5. 5. Hospental

    Hospental is a small village with a big job: it sits in the Urseren valley, a high, treeless basin between the Furka, the Oberalp and the Gotthard, where the Reuss river begins its journey north. Its name comes from the old hospice, a resting place for travellers on the road over the Gotthard, and the village is still very much a gateway. Above the houses stands a stone tower, often called the Lombard tower, a medieval watchtower from which the lords of the valley could see who was coming. Andermatt, a couple of kilometres away, has more shops and fuel, and the nearest sizeable town is Altdorf, thirty kilometres down the valley.

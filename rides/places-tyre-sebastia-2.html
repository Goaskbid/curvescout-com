  1. 1. Tyre

    Tyre was already ancient when Alexander the Great arrived in 332 before Christ. Then it was an island city just off the coast of what is now southern Lebanon, famously rich and famously stubborn, and when its citizens refused to open their gates, Alexander built a causeway out to the island and besieged it for months. Sand piled up against that causeway over the centuries, and the island became the peninsula you see today, with the modern city of Tyre spreading across it and the old harbour still busy with fishing boats.

  2. 2. Bahá’i Holy Places in Haifa and the Wester

    Mount Carmel rises straight from the sea at Haifa, and on its northern slope a staircase of nineteen terraces of gardens, lawns and fountains climbs from the German Colony to the ridge, with the golden-domed Shrine of the Báb in the middle of it all. The Báb, a young merchant from Shiraz, proclaimed in 1844 that a new messenger of God would come, and he was executed in Tabriz in 1850. His remains were hidden and finally brought to Haifa and placed in a tomb on the slope, with the first building completed in 1909 and the present octagon with its dome of gold tiles finished in 1953. The terraces were completed in 2001. The gardens and the shrine, together with the sites at Bahji near Akko, make up the Baha'i Holy Places in Haifa and the Western Galilee, listed by UNESCO in 2008.

  3. 3. Sebastia

    Sebastia, Sabastiya in Arabic, is a Palestinian village on a hill north-west of Nablus, in the northern West Bank, surrounded by olive groves and terraced hillsides. It stands on the site of Samaria, the capital of the northern Kingdom of Israel in the ninth and eighth centuries BC, later rebuilt by Herod the Great, who named it Sebaste in honour of the Emperor Augustus. The ruins on the hilltop above the village span a thousand years of history.

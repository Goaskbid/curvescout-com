Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cardiff

    Cardiff Castle tells you a lot about the city in one place. There is a Roman fort wall, a Norman keep on a mound, and, behind it, a fantastical Victorian palace designed by William Burges for the third Marquess of Bute, one of the richest men in Britain, whose money came from the coal that was shipped through the docks. In the nineteenth century, Cardiff was the world's biggest coal-exporting port, and it grew from a small town into a capital in a few decades. Wales formally recognised it as capital in 1955.

  2. 2. City of Bath

    Bath is one of the few whole cities on the UNESCO list, and it earns it twice over. Underneath lie the Roman baths of Aquae Sulis, built around the only natural hot springs in Britain, where water still comes up at around forty-six degrees and the great pool, with its later colonnade above, steams in the cold. On top sits the Georgian city that eighteenth-century society built when Bath became the place to see and be seen, in honey-coloured local limestone: the Circus, the sweep of the Royal Crescent, the shops on Pulteney Bridge over the Avon and the long elegant terraces climbing the hills.

  5. 5. Dorset and East Devon Coast

    The cliffs around Lyme Regis are made of dark, crumbling clay and shale, and every winter storm brings some of them down. That is why fossil hunters love them. In the early nineteenth century a local girl named Mary Anning, the daughter of a carpenter, found the skeleton of an ichthyosaur on the beach and went on to unearth some of the first plesiosaurs, earning a grudging place in a scientific world that did not welcome women. The ammonites, fossil crinoids and sea-reptile bones that still turn up here are nearly two hundred million years old, and the whole coast from Exmouth to Studland, the Jurassic Coast, was listed by UNESCO in 2001 for its record of Earth history.

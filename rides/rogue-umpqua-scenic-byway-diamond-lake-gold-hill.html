Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Diamond Lake

    Mount Thielsen is the first thing you see, and it is hard to look at anything else. The mountain rises behind Diamond Lake in the Oregon Cascades, a needle of dark volcanic rock about 2,800 metres high, so sharp and so often struck by lightning that it is known as the lightning rod of the Cascades. It is an old volcano, eroded by glaciers until only the plug of hardened lava in the core remains. The lake sits at around 1,600 metres in a forested basin, with the broader bulk of Mount Bailey on its other side, and the water reflects both peaks on a calm morning.

  2. 2. Crater Lake

    Crater Lake does not look real the first time you see it. The water is a deep, saturated blue, almost unnaturally pure, and it fills a bowl nearly ten kilometres across, with steep walls rising some six hundred metres above the surface. It formed about 7,700 years ago, when the volcano Mount Mazama erupted so violently that it emptied its magma chamber and collapsed in on itself. Rain and snow slowly filled the caldera, and with no rivers flowing in or out, the water stayed exceptionally clear. At nearly six hundred metres, it is the deepest lake in the United States.

  3. 3. Gold Hill

    Gold Hill lives up to its name in a modest way. A gold strike in the early 1850s brought miners into this stretch of the Rogue River valley in southern Oregon, and the town grew where the river cuts through a narrow stretch of rock, with a small downtown of brick storefronts and a bridge across the water. Today it is a quiet place of about twelve hundred people, with a diner or two, a hardware store and the feel of a place that lets the interstate pass on the other side of the hill. Locals fish for salmon and steelhead in the Rogue, and rafting companies run the stretches of river below.

Why this ride

From Hachiōji it runs 410 km to Ōme; allow about 7 h 26 of riding. It goes by way of Okutama lake road, Chichibu forest bend core, Nagatoro river stop and Ome exit roads. Daibosatsu high line is the high point. It tops out at about 1,150 m.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hachioji fuel gate

    Mount Takao stands at the edge of Hachiōji like a patient dog, a green hill of about six hundred metres that Tokyoites climb in their hundreds of thousands. For a rider, it is a start and finish point, a convenient place for fuel, parking and a good bowl of ramen before the mountains begin. The city sits at the foot of the Tama hills, and the climate here is a few degrees cooler than central Tokyo.

  2. 2. Okutama lake road

    The Ome Kaido, Route 411, is the road that leads from the western suburbs of Tokyo up into the Okutama mountains, and it follows the Tama river most of the way, with the valley narrowing and the slopes steepening mile by mile. This stop falls on the lake road, with Itsukaichi and Ome some seventeen kilometres behind, and the air at this point is noticeably cooler and cleaner than in the city. The ride out from the suburbs takes an hour or more on a busy day, and the reward is a different world.

  3. 3. Chichibu forest bend core

    Chichibu sits in a high basin ringed by mountains, a town of fifty-eight thousand people that feels more like a market town in the Alps than a place two hours from Tokyo. The ground here is limestone, and to the south the bulk of Mount Buko has been quarried for cement for a century, a vast scar on the hillside that is visible from miles away. The industry made the town prosperous, and it also sits beside a river, the Arakawa, that begins in the mountains above and flows down toward the capital.

  4. 4. Nagatoro river stop

    Nagatoro is a small town on the Arakawa River, at the point where the water leaves the Chichibu mountains and cuts through a long stretch of flat, banded rock. The riverbed here is made of crystalline schist, a pale green stone laid down in broad stepped terraces, known as Iwadatami, or rock tatami, because it looks like a floor of giant mats. Geologists have studied the formation for well over a century, and it is among the most celebrated rock exposures in Japan.

  6. 6. Ome exit roads

    At the end of a day in the mountains, the Tama valley delivers you back to Ōme like a conveyor belt. For a rider coming down from Okutama, it is the place where the road widens, the traffic thickens and the first petrol stations appear, a signal that the mountains are done for the day. Fuel here is no problem, with stations on every main road, and Fussa and Hannō are close.

  7. 7. Okutama Lake

    Few people riding beside Lake Okutama realise that they are looking at the roof of a drowned valley. The lake exists because of the Ogouchi dam, built across the Tama river in the 1950s to give Tokyo more drinking water, and when the gates closed, the rising water covered farms, a shrine and a handful of villages. Several hundred households were moved to higher ground, and old photographs show the fields and houses that vanished. It is a quiet piece of postwar history, and it explains the unusual stillness of the place.

  8. 8. Daibosatsu high line

    Daibosatsu, the Great Bodhisattva, gives its name to a pass, a lake and a ridge on the Yamanashi side of the Okutama and Chichibu mountains, and the high line here follows the ridge for much of the way, with broad views to the south and the great dark shapes of the Southern Alps on the western horizon.

  9. 9. Chichibu valley food

    If Chichibu has a signature dish, it is waraji katsudon, a pair of large, flat pork cutlets laid across a bowl of rice and glazed in a sweet soy sauce, named for the straw sandals, waraji, that they resemble in shape. It is a big meal, enough for any hungry rider after a morning in the mountains, and restaurants in the town centre serve it with miso soup and pickles. The portion is generous, and a small amount of sauce and a lot of appetite are all you need.

  10. 10. Nagatoro river bends

    Nagatoro is a small town of seven thousand people in Saitama Prefecture, at the point where the Arakawa River squeezes through a gorge of layered rock, and it has been a day-trip destination for Tokyoites for more than a century. The name means something like long rock bed, and it describes the scene accurately: the river runs over broad shelves of crystalline schist, worn into smooth steps and pools, with cliffs of dark green stone along the bank. In the nineteenth century, geologists came to study the rock; now people come to float on it.

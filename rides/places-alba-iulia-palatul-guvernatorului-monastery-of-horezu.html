Why this ride

From Alba Iulia it runs 331 km to Alba Iulia; allow about 5 h 42 of riding. It goes by way of Capâlna, Comărnicel I, Voineasa - Crac-Găuri and Monastery of Horezu. The highest point is about 1,600 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

  1. 1. Alba Iulia - Palatul Guvernatorului

    The Habsburgs rebuilt Alba Iulia in the early eighteenth century as a fortress, and the buildings inside the walls still bear the mark of imperial administration. After the Habsburgs took control of Transylvania at the end of the seventeenth century, they chose this old princely seat as a military and administrative base, and a succession of governors and garrison commanders lived and worked in the barracks, arsenals and offices that fill the citadel. The Governor's Palace takes its name from that period. Oarda, a village now almost a suburb, lies just to the south, along the Mureș river.

  2. 2. Capâlna

    The Dacians liked their fortresses high, hidden and hard to reach, which is why the Roman legions of Trajan took two bloody wars at the start of the second century to conquer them. Căpâlna stands on a steep wooded hill above the Sebeș valley, a Dacian citadel with stone walls built in the distinctive murus dacicus technique, blocks of cut stone tied together with timber beams, and a tower that served as the last refuge. It is one of the six Dacian fortresses of the Orăștie Mountains listed by UNESCO in 1999, along with the capital Sarmizegetusa Regia, and it is one of the easier ones to reach, with a path climbing through the forest from the valley road.

  3. 3. Comărnicel I

    Comărnicel is the name on the map, and the Dacian fortresses of the Orăștie mountains, listed by UNESCO in 1999, are the reason this country matters. Here, in the first century BC and the first century AD, the Dacian kings built stone citadels on mountain tops, with terraces, walls and sacred precincts, the grandest of them Sarmizegetusa Regia, not far to the north-west.

  4. 4. Voineasa - Crac-Găuri

    North of the Jiu Valley, the land rises steeply into the Șureanu and Parâng mountains, a wild region of high ridges, deep valleys, spruce forests and alpine meadows. Crac-Găuri lies up in this high country, linked by name to the old routes that crossed the mountains in Roman times, when Dacia was a province and soldiers watched the passes between the Transylvanian basin and the south. Little is visible today, and the remoteness is half the attraction: this is mountain land, where shepherds bring their flocks to high pastures in summer and the forest stretches for miles.

  5. 5. Monastery of Horezu

    Constantin Brâncoveanu, prince of Wallachia at the end of the seventeenth century, was a wealthy, cultured ruler who built churches, palaces and monasteries across his principality, and Horezu was his masterpiece. Founded in 1690, the monastery sits in a quiet valley in the foothills of the Căpățânii mountains, surrounded by walls, with a church at the centre of a courtyard lined with arcaded cells. It is the finest example of the Brâncovenesc style, a blend of Byzantine, Renaissance and Baroque, with carved stone, painted interiors and elegant porches, and it was inscribed on the UNESCO list in 1993. In 1714, Brâncoveanu and his four sons were executed by the Ottomans in Istanbul; the Romanian Orthodox Church later canonised them as martyrs.

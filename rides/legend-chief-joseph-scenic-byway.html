  1. 1. Cody

    Cody was founded by a man who understood the value of a story. William F. Cody, Buffalo Bill, the scout and showman whose Wild West show toured America and Europe in the late nineteenth century, helped establish the town in 1896 on the Shoshone River in north-west Wyoming, and he built a hotel here, the Irma, named after his daughter. The cherry-wood bar in the Irma was a gift from Queen Victoria, so the story goes. Today Cody has about ten thousand people and calls itself the Rodeo Capital of the World, with a rodeo every night in summer.

  2. 2. Chief Joseph Scenic Byway point 2

    The ground is a patchwork of sagebrush, juniper, dry gullies and eroded bluffs in shades of red, yellow and grey, laid down by volcanic activity long before the first people arrived. The Absarokas are volcanic in origin, made of old lava and ash flows, and so they have a very different look to the granite peaks of nearby ranges, with dark, crumbling ridges and jagged tops.

  3. 3. Chief Joseph Scenic Byway point 3

    The road climbs here into real mountains. The name is old and its origins are disputed, so treat any single story with care, but the place itself is easy to understand: a high, windy shoulder of the Absaroka Range with dark forest, bare grassy slopes and a view that stretches back toward the Bighorn Basin.

  4. 4. Chief Joseph Scenic Byway point 4

    Cooke City is a one-street mountain town in the far corner of Montana, and nobody drives to it by accident. It sits at around 7,600 feet beside Soda Butte Creek, just outside the northeast entrance to Yellowstone, and its twin, Silver Gate, lies a mile to the east. Together they are home to a few hundred people in summer and fewer than a hundred in the long winters, when the snow can be deep enough to bury the ground floor of the buildings.

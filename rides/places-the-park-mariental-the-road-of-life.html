Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. The Park «Mariental»

    Pavlovsk's great park spreads over several hundred hectares along the Slavyanka river, a valley of meadows, groves and winding water that was designed as a landscape garden for the family of Tsar Paul I, with the palace by the Scottish architect Charles Cameron as its centrepiece. The name Mariental, meaning Maria's valley, recalls the empress Maria Feodorovna, who spent decades shaping the grounds after her husband's death. Little of it was left in 1944, when the retreating German army burned the palace and felled thousands of trees, and its restoration after the war took years of patient work.

  2. 2. Historical Centre of the Town of Lomonosov

    Lomonosov was called Oranienbaum until 1948, and its history is a story of ambition. Prince Alexander Menshikov, Peter the Great's close friend and favourite, began a grand palace here in the early eighteenth century, on the shore of the Gulf of Finland, and the name, meaning orange tree, came from its greenhouses. Later, the estate passed to the imperial family, and in the 1760s Catherine the Great had the Chinese Palace built in the park, a small, richly decorated pavilion with painted ceilings and exotic interiors. The ensemble is part of the UNESCO listing of Saint Petersburg and related monuments.

  3. 3. The Road of Life

    On the south-eastern shore of Lake Ladoga, east of Saint Petersburg, a road ends at the water. This is the start of the Road of Life, the supply route across the ice that kept Leningrad alive during the siege of 1941 to 1944. The siege lasted 872 days, from September 1941 to January 1944, and it cut the city off from nearly everything except the lake. In the first winter, when Ladoga froze, trucks and horse-drawn sledges began to cross the ice with food and fuel, and on the return journey they carried out women, children and the sick. Lorries broke through the ice, and bombing and artillery attacked the route, but it kept going.

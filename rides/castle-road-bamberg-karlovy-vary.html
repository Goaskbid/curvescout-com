Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Bamberg

    Bamberg is one of those German cities that came through the twentieth century almost undamaged, so its old centre feels more like a seventeenth-century drawing than a place that has been repaired. It was listed by UNESCO in 1993, and it is easy to see why: it stretches across seven hills, like Rome, with the cathedral and the old bishop's residence on the highest, the Regnitz river looping through the middle, and rows of half-timbered houses and fishermen's cottages along the water. The little town hall sits on an artificial island in the river, reached by bridges, and has frescoes that seem to spill out over the walls.

  2. 2. Bayreuth

    Bayreuth is the town where one man's operas became a pilgrimage. Richard Wagner moved here in the 1870s and built a festival theatre on a hill outside the town, designed to his own ideas, with a hidden orchestra pit and a plain brick exterior. The first festival was held in 1876, and every summer since, thousands of people have made the trip to hear his operas. The tickets are so hard to obtain that the waiting list is said to be measured in years.

  3. 3. Karlovy Vary

    Legend has it that Emperor Charles IV was out hunting in the forests of western Bohemia in the fourteenth century when a stag he was chasing, or in some versions one of his hounds, fell into a pool of scalding water, and that is how the hot springs of Karlovy Vary were found. Whatever the truth, the town that grew up around them bears his name, Karlsbad in German, and by the nineteenth century it had become one of the most fashionable spas in Europe, visited by tsars, composers, poets and the cream of European society. In 2021, it became part of the UNESCO listing of the Great Spa Towns of Europe.

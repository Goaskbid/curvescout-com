Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Tangier

    Tangier sits where Africa nearly touches Europe, on the Strait of Gibraltar, and on a clear day you can see Spain across the water. About fourteen kilometres separate the continents here, and for thousands of years this has been a crossroads and a place where the Atlantic and the Mediterranean meet. Phoenicians, Romans, Portuguese, English and Spanish all held it, and from 1923 until 1956 it was an international zone run by several powers, which gave it a reputation for spies, smugglers and writers.

  2. 2. Medina of Tétouan

    Tétouan wears its Andalusian heritage in whitewash. After the Christians conquered Granada in 1492, thousands of Muslim and Jewish refugees crossed the strait to North Africa, and a good number settled in this city beneath the Rif mountains, which had been rebuilt a few years earlier by a former Granadan commander, Sidi Ali al-Mandri. They brought music, cooking and architecture, and the medina they created is a maze of narrow lanes, tiled fountains, courtyards and carved wooden doors that still carries the memory of Spain. UNESCO inscribed it in 1997, and it is among the best preserved of Morocco's old cities, and one of the least touristed.

  4. 4. Gorham's Cave Complex

    Gorham's Cave is a hollow in the east face of the Rock of Gibraltar, opening toward the sea on the far side from the town, and it is one of the last known refuges of the Neanderthals. They lived in these caves for many thousands of years, hunting ibex and rabbit and gathering shellfish on the shore, and excavations have found hearths, tools, and even bird bones that show cut marks, along with a cross-hatched engraving in the rock that some researchers think was made deliberately. Dates suggest Neanderthals may have survived here later than anywhere else in Europe, perhaps until around 40,000 years ago or later, though the exact timing is debated. The cave complex is protected as a UNESCO site, and the cave itself is closed to casual visitors.

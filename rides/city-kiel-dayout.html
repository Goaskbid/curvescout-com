Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Kiel

    Kiel is a city that faces the water in every direction. It sits at the head of the Kiel Fjord, a long inlet of the Baltic, and at the eastern end of the Kiel Canal, which cuts across the base of the Jutland peninsula to the North Sea and is the busiest artificial waterway in the world by ship traffic. Cargo ships, tankers and ferries pass in a line, and from the high bridges you can watch giant vessels slide past apparently at roof level. The canal was opened in 1895, and for its size and importance it deserves more visitors than it gets.

  2. 2. East Wall Area 1A to 1C

    When the Vikings built their trading town of Hedeby at the head of the Schlei, a long narrow inlet of the Baltic, they chose a spot with a short portage to the North Sea rivers, making it one of the great crossroads of Northern Europe. To protect the isthmus between the two seas, the Danes built a long earthwork called the Danevirke, begun in the eighth century and strengthened for centuries after, with a rampart, a ditch and in places a brick wall added in the twelfth century by King Valdemar. Hedeby and the Danevirke are together a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The East Wall, the Ostwall, is the eastern arm of the system, running through the countryside near Kosel above the Schlei.

  3. 3. Offshore Work

    The word on the sign suggests a ship, but the offshore work here is a piece of earthwork, an outlying defence belonging to the old Danevirke system. The ground is south of the Schlei, the long narrow fjord that cuts inland from the Baltic to Schleswig, between the villages of Fahrdorf, Schaalby and Fleckeby. In practice, for a rider, the label is a pointer to a landscape rather than a single monument: low farmland, hedged fields, small woods and quiet lanes that run down to the water.

  4. 4. Kovirke Area 8

    Kovirke is the name of one of the oldest sections of the Danevirke, the great earthwork that guarded the southern border of Denmark for the better part of a thousand years. From the early eighth century, the Danes built ramparts, ditches and later brick walls across the narrow neck of land between the Baltic and the North Sea, and the system grew in stages. Neighbouring sections have names of their own, the Crooked Wall among them, and the Kovirke is among the earliest. The line runs through fields and woodland, a long bank that you can see best in the low light of morning or evening.

  5. 5. Kovirke Area 7

    South of Schleswig, in the narrow neck of land where the Jutland peninsula pinches itself between the Baltic and the North Sea marshes, lies one of the longest ancient monuments in Europe. This is the Danevirke, a system of earth ramparts, ditches and walls that runs for about thirty kilometres across the country, and the label here, Kovirke Area 7, refers to one of its sections, the Kovirke, whose name is usually translated as the Cow Work. Today the walls are grassy banks in fields, easy to miss if you are riding fast, but they once barred the way into Denmark.

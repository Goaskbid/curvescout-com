Why this ride

48 km from Osaka to Osaka, about 50 min of riding. On the way it passes Zenizuka Kofun, Gobyoyama Kofun, Richu-tenno-ryo Kofun and Itasuke Kofun.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Osaka

    Osaka runs on appetite. It is Japan's third-largest city, with some two and a half million people, and for centuries it was the nation's kitchen, the place where rice from the whole country was traded and where merchants made fortunes and lived well. The local motto kuidaore, eat until you drop, is not a boast so much as a way of life, and you will see it in the neon crabs and puffer fish along Dotonbori.

  2. 2. Zenizuka Kofun

    Mozu is an old word for the shrike, a small predatory bird that impales its prey on thorns, and the name belongs to the stretch of Sakai where the biggest tombs lie. A chronicle tells how, when workers were building the tomb of Emperor Nintoku in the fifth century, a deer suddenly fell dead on the site, and a shrike flew out of its ear. The story is a legend, of course, but it gave the district its name, and it hints at how strange and mysterious these monuments seemed even to later Japanese.

  3. 3. Gobyoyama Kofun

    The Gobyoyama tomb is the resting place attributed to Emperor Richu, the seventeenth emperor in the traditional count, and it is a giant: a keyhole-shaped mound in Sakai more than three hundred metres long, one of the biggest in the whole country. It was built in the late fourth or early fifth century, and its builders moved an enormous amount of earth. Surfaces were covered with fitted stones, and rows of haniwa figures stood on the terraces, so that in its day the whole mound would have gleamed like a stepped pyramid.

  4. 4. Richu-tenno-ryo Kofun

    You cannot really see a kofun. That is the first lesson of Richu-tenno-ryo, one of the great keyhole-shaped tombs of the Mozu group in Sakai, south of Osaka. These are burial mounds built in the fifth century for the early rulers of Japan, shaped like a keyhole with a round back and a wide front, and surrounded by moats. The Richu mound is about three hundred and sixty metres long, among the largest in the country, and you walk around the water on a path, catching glimpses through the trees of a green hill that looks like a wooded island. From the ground it simply looks like a park.

  5. 5. Itasuke Kofun

    Itasuke is a modest keyhole-shaped mound from the same age of burial monuments that gave the city its World Heritage listing. It sits amid ordinary streets, one more piece of green surrounded by tiled roofs, with its moat and trees telling you that something older than the neighbourhood is under there. It is a reason to arrive early, on a quiet weekday morning, and see what a city looks like before the engines warm up.

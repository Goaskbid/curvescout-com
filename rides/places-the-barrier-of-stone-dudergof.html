Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. The Barrier of Stone

    The Barrier of Stone is a name that sounds like a medieval wall, though what is actually out here is a creature of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries: the great flood barrier that stretches across the Gulf of Finland to protect St Petersburg. The city has been flooded again and again since its foundation, because it sits at the mouth of the Neva on land barely above sea level, and when westerly storms push water up the gulf the river backs up and spills into the streets. The worst floods are remembered in literature, most famously in Pushkin's poem about the bronze horseman.

  2. 2. The Water-bringing system of Peterhof

    The fountains of Peterhof run without a single pump, and that is the cleverest thing about them. When Peter the Great planned his gardens in the 1710s and 1720s, engineers led by Vasily Tuvolkov worked out that the water of the Ropsha hills, about twenty kilometres to the south, stood high enough above the Gulf of Finland to feed the fountains by gravity alone. They dug canals, built ponds and laid pipes, so that the water flows from the heights to the Upper Garden and down to the Great Cascade, where it shoots high into the air. The system, still essentially the same, is part of the UNESCO listing for Saint Petersburg.

  3. 3. The Palace and Park Ensemble of the Villag

    Gostilitsy is a village on the Izhora Upland, west of Ropsha and south of Peterhof, and it is one of those places near St Petersburg where the imperial past hides behind fences and overgrown trees. The estate here is associated with Alexei Razumovsky, the Ukrainian Cossack singer who rose to become the favourite and, by many accounts, the secret husband of Empress Elizabeth in the eighteenth century. The palace and park ensemble that grew up around the manor included a house, a church, ponds and landscaped grounds fed by springs, and it is listed among the monuments of the historic centre of St Petersburg and related groups, a UNESCO site that includes many of the palaces and estates around the city.

  4. 4. Dudergof

    South-west of St Petersburg, the flat land of the Neva lowland rises unexpectedly into a pair of wooded hills, the Duderhof heights, the highest ground for many kilometres around. Their name comes from Finnish, and they have long been a landmark, used for military observation since the eighteenth century and as a summer retreat for the people of the capital. In the nineteenth century, the imperial army held its summer manoeuvres around nearby Krasnoye Selo, and the officers' villas and parade grounds became part of the social life of St Petersburg.

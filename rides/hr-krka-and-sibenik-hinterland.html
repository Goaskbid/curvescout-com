  1. 1. Šibenik

    Šibenik is one of the few Croatian Adriatic towns not founded by the Greeks or Romans but by Croats, first mentioned in the eleventh century, and it still has the feel of a place that grew from the hillside rather than from a plan. It climbs steeply from a natural harbour, protected by the long channel of St Anthony, with stone houses, steps and lanes arranged like a staircase up to the fortress of St Michael. At the foot of the slope stands the Cathedral of St James, a UNESCO-listed building constructed entirely of stone, without any binding material, between the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Its dome is a vaulted shell of carefully fitted slabs, and a frieze of seventy-one carved faces runs around the apses, said to be portraits of ordinary townspeople.

  2. 2. Knin

    Knin sits in a bowl of karst where the Dalmatian hinterland gives way to the mountains, with the long grey wall of the Dinara standing over it to the north-east. Above the old town a huge fortress occupies a limestone ridge, and the scale of it surprises people who have only ever driven past on the way to the coast. It is a place that mattered because of where it sits: the gap between the coastal plain and the interior has always been a gateway, and whoever held Knin watched over the traffic between the two.

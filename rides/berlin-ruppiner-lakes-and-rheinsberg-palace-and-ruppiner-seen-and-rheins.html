Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Oranienburg fuel gate

    Oranienburg is a town on the Havel river, a short way north of Berlin, and it carries two very different histories side by side. On one side there is the palace, built in the seventeenth century for Louise Henriette of Orange, the Dutch-born wife of the Elector of Brandenburg, who gave the town its name. It was the first baroque palace in Brandenburg, and its Dutch influence is seen in the proportions and brick. The town became known as Oranienburg, the castle of Orange, and the palace now houses a museum and gardens.

  2. 2. Neuruppin lakefront

    Neuruppin is where Theodor Fontane was born, in 1819, in the pharmacy his father ran on the main square, and he began his famous walking tours of Brandenburg with the county around the town. The architect Karl Friedrich Schinkel, who would shape so much of Berlin, was born here too, in 1781. For a town of thirty-two thousand people, that is quite a pair of local boys.

  3. 3. Rheinsberg palace stop

    Rheinsberg is where a prince became a king, or at least learned to think like one. Frederick, the Prussian crown prince later called the Great, spent four happy years here, from 1736 to 1740, in a palace on the shore of the Grienericksee, away from his violent father, with books, flute music, friends and the philosophers he corresponded with. He had the architect Knobelsdorff remodel the building, and the result is a pale, graceful Baroque and early rococo palace, with a double-winged courtyard facing the water. Later he spoke of this period as the happiest of his life.

  4. 4. Stechlinsee water stop

    Few lakes in Germany have as strong a hold on the imagination as the Stechlinsee. It is deep, nearly seventy metres, and so clear that you can see far down through the water, with a bed of white sand and a ring of beech forest around the shore. Fontane made it the setting for his last novel, Der Stechlin, in which the lake is said to stir whenever something great happens in the world, a kind of seismograph of history. It is a beautiful idea, and standing on the shore you can almost believe it.

  7. 7. Gransee backroads

    Gransee is a small town with a big memory. In 1810 the young Queen Luise of Prussia died suddenly at her family's summer residence, and her coffin was carried back to Berlin in a slow procession that rested overnight in Gransee. The town later raised a monument on the spot, designed by Karl Friedrich Schinkel, a delicate cast-iron canopy that still stands in the market square, a rare piece of early German neo-Gothic metalwork. It is lovely and a little sad.

  8. 8. Rheinsberg castle and lake

    Rheinsberg belongs to the water. The town sits on the Grienericksee, with the long Rheinsberger Rhin running out toward the Ruppin lakes, and it is surrounded by a chain of lakes and forests that forms one of the quietest corners of Brandenburg. The palace is the headline attraction, but the lakes explain why the prince Frederick, later the Great, found the place so restful. On a still summer evening, with swans on the water and the pale walls of the castle reflected in the lake, it is easy to understand.

  9. 9. Neuruppin lakefront food

    In 1787 a great fire destroyed most of Neuruppin, and the town was rebuilt on a grid, in a clean neoclassical style, with wide streets and a large parade ground. The plan, drawn up by royal officials, gives the place an unusual openness, and walking through it you can feel the order of the Enlightenment. The lake side of the town, however, kept its old character, and the lakefront is where you want to eat.

  10. 10. Kremmen moor roads

    Kremmen sits at the edge of a marsh, and the roads around it are a lesson in how the Mark Brandenburg was made. The ground here is the Rhinluch, a broad, low-lying fen of peat and sand that was drained, diked and farmed from the eighteenth century onward, and the word Luch means exactly that, a wet meadow.

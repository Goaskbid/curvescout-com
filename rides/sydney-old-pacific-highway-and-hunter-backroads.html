Why this ride

Classic biker bends north of the city. 293 km round from Berowra Heights - plan on about 5 h 03 of riding. The stops in between: Wisemans Ferry, Wollombi and Hunter Valley roads. It keeps to quiet secondary roads.

Roadbook

  1. Berowra old road scenic

    Berowra sits on a ridge of Hawkesbury sandstone about forty kilometres north of central Sydney, and the old road that threads through it is what is left of the original route north before the freeway took the traffic away. That is exactly why it is nice. The big roar of trucks went to the motorway, and the old Pacific Highway alignment now carries local cars, cyclists and a steady trickle of riders who know it.

    The country here is Ku-ring-gai Chase on one side and the Hawkesbury River on the other, and you feel both. The road runs along the crest, then drops in tight bends toward the estuary through rock cuttings, with eucalyptus and angophora leaning over and glimpses of the water between the trunks. The surface is mostly good, but sandstone country means occasional rough patches, leaf litter in the shade and damp in the shadier hollows. On a weekend morning there are plenty of other bikes, so keep a little in reserve for blind corners.

    The town gives you what a finishing point should. Berowra has around four and a half thousand people and Berowra Heights, next door, a little more, so fuel, bakeries, cafes and a station are close at hand. The railway line runs through here, which tells you something: this was always a commuter-and-weekender sort of place, with bushwalkers heading down into Berowra Creek, the long tidal arm that feeds into the Hawkesbury.

    If the day has been long, the pleasure of this spot is how quickly it takes you back out of the bush. Sydney's northern suburbs start without much warning, but for those first few kilometres it feels like a different country. In summer the early evening smell of eucalypt and warm stone is the thing I remember best, and in winter the road stays clear and dry more often than you would expect. Take care in the first rain after a dry spell, when the oil and dust make the surface slippery, and you will enjoy it.

  2. Wisemans Ferry photo

    Wisemans Ferry sits where the Hawkesbury River turns through a deep sandstone gorge about an hour north-west of Sydney, and the crossing has been working since the 1820s. Solomon Wiseman, a convict who became a successful businessman, ran the ferry and gave the place its name. Today, a pair of free vehicle ferries, cable-guided and run by the state, carry cars and bikes back and forth across the river around the clock, with a short queue at busy times. It is a pleasing piece of old-fashioned travel, and on a bike there is something neat about rolling onto the steel deck with the river water slapping underneath.

    The land around is rugged. Dense eucalypt bush, steep cliffs and the green river fill the view, with Dharug National Park on one side and Yengo National Park beyond. The Hawkesbury is broad and quiet here, a drowned river valley, and the cliffs reflect in the water on still mornings. Kookaburras laugh from the trees, lyrebirds scratch in the undergrowth, and in spring the bush is full of wildflowers and wattle.

    The ride takes in the Old Great North Road, a remarkable piece of engineering built by convicts in the 1820s and 1830s to link Sydney with the Hunter Valley. Sections of the stone-walled road survive in the bush, with retaining walls, drains and culverts that are still intact. Some are sealed, some are gravel, and some are for walkers only, so do not assume that every bit is open to bikes. The roads on either side of the river, such as Settlers Road and the road to St Albans, wind in long curves through the valley, with good surfaces and little traffic outside weekends. On weekends, riders come from all over Sydney, so this is a busy place on a sunny Sunday.

    Services here are limited. The village has a pub, a store and a caravan park, but fuel is something to organise in advance, because the main towns are some distance away. Windsor and Berowra Heights are the sensible places to fill up. Summer can be hot and bushfire-prone, with road closures in dry spells. The cooler months are the best time, with clear air and soft light on the sandstone.

  3. Wollombi food food

    Wollombi lies in the folds of the hills between the Hunter Valley and the Hawkesbury, a small village that seems to have been overlooked by the twenty-first century. It sits where the Wollombi Brook meets Congewai Creek, with sandstone buildings, a historic pub and a church, all at the crossroads of the old convict road. The Wollombi Tavern, dating from the nineteenth century, is the centre of village life, and a good place to eat and drink, with a verandah where riders and locals sit side by side.

    The Great North Road runs through the area, and the history of the village is bound up with it. Convicts built the road in the 1820s and 1830s to link Sydney to the Hunter, working in chains with hand tools, and the quality of the surviving stonework still impresses. Wollombi was once a thriving staging post, with coaches changing horses and travellers staying overnight. The Aboriginal name is thought to mean meeting of the waters, and the Wonnarua people lived in the district for thousands of years before settlement.

    The road to the village is a rider's treat. The Wollombi Road winds through bush, dry creek beds and farmland, with steady curves, a few crests and quiet traffic during the week. At weekends, the roads fill with motorcyclists, cyclists and wine tourists on their way to Pokolbin, and you should be wary of cars cutting corners. The surface is mostly good, though the verges are soft and there is gravel where side roads join. Wildlife is a hazard, particularly at dawn and dusk, when kangaroos and wallabies come out to feed.

    The countryside is a mix of eucalyptus forest, grazing and vineyards, and in spring there are wildflowers. In summer, it is hot and dry, with a risk of bushfires, and in winter, nights can be cold. The best seasons are autumn and spring.

    Food in the village includes the tavern, plus a few cafes and bakeries that cater to travellers. Opening times can be limited on weekdays, so it is worth planning your lunch. Cessnock, about twenty-three kilometres away, is the nearest large town, and has stations, supermarkets and the services you need. From there, the vineyards of the Hunter are only a short run away.

  4. Hunter Valley roads sight

    The Hunter Valley is the oldest wine region in Australia, with vines planted in the early nineteenth century, and the country around Pokolbin is where most of the cellar doors, restaurants and weekend cottages cluster. It lies about two hours north of Sydney, in a broad valley of gentle hills below the long blue line of the Brokenback Range, and the landscape of vineyards, paddocks and eucalypts is as much of an escape for city people as it was for their grandparents. Semillon, which here matures into a lemony, toasty white with age, is the local speciality, and Shiraz does well too.

    The valley is also coal country. Open-cut mines and power stations lie a short distance up the road, and the town of Cessnock grew as a mining centre, so wine, heavy industry and horse studs sit side by side. Wollombi, a village in the hills to the south, has an old inn, a sandstone church and a stretch of the Great North Road, a convict-built route from the 1830s, with retaining walls and culverts in stone that have survived nearly two centuries.

    For riders, the backroads around the valley are the draw. Lanes wind between vineyards, with gentle rises and bends, and the main roads through the hills toward Wollombi and Broke carry less traffic. The sealed surface is mostly good, though some minor roads have gravel patches and potholes after heavy rain. Kangaroos and wallabies are common at dawn and dusk, and a collision with one can be serious, so slow down in the evening. Weekends, especially long weekends, bring crowds of visitors and a lot of slow traffic, so a weekday ride is more relaxing.

    Cessnock, eight kilometres away, is the main town, with fuel stations, supermarkets and cafes, and it is the safest place to fill up. Several of the wineries serve lunch, though riders will be limiting themselves to a taste at most. Summers can be very hot, with temperatures above forty degrees, and bushfire smoke occasionally affects the region. Autumn and spring are the best seasons, with pleasant temperatures and the vines changing colour in April and May.

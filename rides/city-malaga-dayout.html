Why this ride

A 116 km round trip from Málaga - roughly 2 h in the saddle. It goes by way of Antequera Dolmens Site, The Menga Dolmen and The Viera Dolmen, Tholos of El Romeral and La Peña de los Enamorados.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Malaga

    Málaga has been a port for nearly three thousand years, founded by the Phoenicians and later ruled by Romans, Moors and Castilians, and the layers show if you look. At the foot of the hill, a Roman theatre lies right next to the Alcazaba, an eleventh-century Moorish fortress-palace whose gardens and fountains climb the slope. Above it a ruined castle called Gibralfaro crowns the summit, linked by a walled path, and the view from its ramparts over the harbour, the bullring and the blue Mediterranean is the best in the city. The painter Pablo Picasso was born here in 1881, and a museum in a Renaissance palace in the old town holds a large collection of his work.

  2. 2. Antequera Dolmens Site

    A visitor's centre, some car parks, a long low mound and a view across a plain of wheat: the Antequera dolmens site does not look like much from the approach road, and then you step inside Menga and your sense of proportion rearranges itself. This stop is the formal gateway to the complex, a mile or so from the old town, and from here you can see the shape of the landscape that the prehistoric builders chose, with the strange rocky mountain called the Peña de los Enamorados rising to the northeast.

  3. 3. The Menga Dolmen and The Viera Dolmen

    Antequera sits in the middle of Andalusia like a coin in a pocket, and the two great dolmens stand on its northern edge, barely more than a kilometre from the centre. Menga and Viera were built more than five thousand years ago by farming people who dragged enormous blocks of stone into place, and Menga is one of the biggest megalithic chambers in Europe, with a roof of slabs that weigh many tonnes each. You walk into a long corridor of stone, with a mound of earth above, and the scale of it hits you before any explanation does.

  4. 4. Tholos of El Romeral

    Just outside Antequera, a low grassy mound hides one of Europe's more unusual prehistoric chambers. The Tholos of El Romeral is a Bronze Age burial, roughly four thousand years old, built from carefully fitted stone slabs that rise in a corbelled dome above a long entrance corridor. Unlike its neighbours, the huge Neolithic dolmens of Menga and Viera, it uses a dry-stone technique closer to what you find in the eastern Mediterranean, so archaeologists argue about where the builders learned it. All three belong to the Antequera Dolmens Site, listed by UNESCO in 2016 partly because the landscape itself seems to be part of the design.

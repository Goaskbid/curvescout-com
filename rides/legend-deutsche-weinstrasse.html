Why this ride

Bockenheim to Schweigen, vines the whole way. A one-way ride of 66 km, Wissembourg to Heidesheim - roughly 1 h 12 in the saddle. The stops in between: Bad Bergzabern, Landau in der Pfalz, Neustadt and Deidesheim. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Deutsche Weinstrasse

Expect company on a summer weekend. Early is better than fast. For 6 km this is Deutsche Weinstrasse, a road with a name of its own and a reason for it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Wissembourg

    The German Wine Road begins, or ends, depending on which way you are pointed, at a gate. In Schweigen-Rechtenbach, a wine village pressed right up against the French border, stands the Deutsches Weintor, a heavy stone arch built in the 1930s to mark the southern terminus of the Weinstrasse. It is a slightly pompous thing, very much of its time, and today it houses a wine bar and a viewing gallery from which you look out over vineyards rolling north toward the Haardt hills. A couple of kilometres south, across a border you will barely notice, lies Wissembourg in Alsace.

  2. 2. Bad Bergzabern

    Bad Bergzabern lies close to the French border, at the point where the German Wine Route runs out of road and the hills of the southern Palatinate fold into the Vosges across the frontier. It is a spa town of about seven and a half thousand people, with a castle that once belonged to the Dukes of Zweibrücken, a Renaissance inn called the Gasthaus zum Engel, with an ornate carved facade dating from the sixteenth century, and a calm, tidy centre built around a market square. The Palatinate Forest, a large area of woodland, begins just behind it.

  3. 3. Landau in der Pfalz

    Landau is the town the Weinstrasse orbits without ever quite touching. With some forty thousand people it is the largest place in the southern Palatinate, and the wine villages that string along the foot of the hills a few kilometres to the west, Birkweiler, Siebeldingen, Frankweiler, all look to it for shopping, schools and trains. It sits on the flat plain of the Rhine rift, so there is nothing dramatic about the ride in, but it is the obvious place in this stretch to refuel, eat properly and sort out anything the bike needs.

  4. 4. Neustadt

    Neustadt an der Weinstraße sits where the wooded hills of the Palatinate Forest drop down to the wide, sunny plain of the Rhine, and it is the unofficial capital of the German Wine Route, which runs for about eighty-five kilometres along the edge of the hills from the French border to the north. The town has an old centre of half-timbered houses, narrow lanes and wine taverns, and around it vineyards stretch in every direction, with small wine villages like Gimmeldingen and Haardt just above the town.

  5. 5. Deidesheim

    Deidesheim is what a wine town looks like when it has been rich for five hundred years and has kept the receipts. The cobbled centre is a short walk around a Gothic church, a Renaissance town hall with an outside staircase, and half-timbered houses painted in warm colours, with a few of the big estates, Bassermann-Jordan, Bürklin-Wolf and others, tucked into old courtyards. These families made Riesling famous well before Germany had a wine law, and their cellars have grown a fine reputation.

  6. 6. Bad Dürkheim

    The Palatinate has a way of making you hungry and thirsty at the same time, and Bad Dürkheim is where the effect is strongest. The town lies at the foot of the Haardt, the eastern edge of the Palatinate Forest, with vineyards climbing the slope behind it and the Rhine plain stretching flat to the east. It is one of the main stops on the German Wine Route, and it takes its wine very seriously, with a sense of humour attached.

  7. 7. Grünstadt

    Grünstadt does not make the postcards, and it does not seem to mind. It is a working wine town of about thirteen thousand at the northern end of the Palatinate Wine Route, with a pedestrian centre, a Saturday market and a few handsome half-timbered houses that survived the wars. The vineyards start right behind the railway, and beyond them the land rolls westward toward the Donnersberg, the highest hill in the Palatinate at just under seven hundred metres, a dome of forest that you can see from half the region.

  8. 8. Near Heidesheim

    Bockenheim marks the northern end of the Deutsche Weinstrasse, and if you have ridden it from the French border you have come about eighty-five kilometres through almost unbroken vineyards. The village has its own counterpart to the stone gate at the southern end: the Haus der Deutschen Weinstrasse, a building that straddles the road like a gatehouse, so you literally ride through it. It is a quieter monument than the one at Schweigen, and the village around it is a working wine place rather than a showpiece, with farmyards, cellars and a few taverns.

Why this ride

A 158 km round trip from Carlisle - roughly 2 h 43 in the saddle. On the way it passes Knockcross Roman temporary camp at Grey Ha, Wolsty South tower 13b, Burrow Walls Roman fort and back through Wolsty South tower 13b. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hadrian's Wall between Tarraby and Beech G

    Carlisle has been a border city for two thousand years, and the Romans were the first to make it one. On the eastern edge, around Tarraby, the line of Hadrian's Wall passes beneath modern housing, and for much of its length here there is nothing to see above ground except the occasional dip in a field or a bend in a lane that follows the old ditch. The great fort of Uxelodunum, now buried beneath Stanwix, was the largest on the whole wall, home to a thousand cavalrymen, which tells you how much weight the Romans put on this crossing of the river Eden.

  3. 3. Wolsty South tower 13b

    Hadrian's Wall gets the fame, but the Roman frontier did not simply stop at Bowness-on-Solway. Beyond the end of the wall, a chain of milefortlets and towers continued down the Cumbrian coast, watching the Solway Firth for raiders from the north. Wolsty South, tower 13b, was one of them, a small stone watchtower near Silloth on the low, windy coast. There is little to see today, often just a mark in the ground, but the site is part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire listed by UNESCO, and its position tells a story: from here, Roman soldiers looked across the water to the hills of Scotland.

  4. 4. Burrow Walls Roman fort

    On the northern edge of Workington, near the mouth of the river Derwent, a fragment of stone wall stands alone in a field, a jagged piece of masonry rising above the grass. It is part of Burrow Walls, a Roman fort that guarded the Cumbrian coast, one of the chain of defences that extended from the western end of Hadrian's Wall down the shore to watch for raiders crossing from Scotland and Ireland. Much of the fort's stone was reused in a medieval building on the site, and what survives today is modest, but it is part of the Hadrian's Wall World Heritage site and has a lonely charm, with views over the sea and the river.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Great Living Chola Temples

    The Cauvery delta is the rice bowl of Tamil Nadu, and for a thousand years it fed an empire. The Cholas, who ruled much of southern India from the ninth to the thirteenth century, built their power on the fields, the ports and the fleet that carried their armies as far as Sumatra, and they poured their wealth into temples. Three of these, the Brihadisvara at Thanjavur, the one at Gangaikonda Cholapuram and the Airavatesvara at Darasuram, are together a UNESCO World Heritage site, the Great Living Chola Temples, and the adjective is accurate: priests still perform rites in them daily.

  2. 2. The Airavatesvara Temple Complex

    Darasuram is a quiet town in the Kaveri delta, a few kilometres from Kumbakonam, and its temple is one of the gems of Chola architecture. The Airavatesvara temple was built in the twelfth century under Rajaraja II, and it is smaller and more delicate than the great temple of Thanjavur, as though the Cholas, at the height of their power, had decided to put their energy into detail. Every surface is carved: miniature friezes of dancers and musicians, the stories of the Shaiva saints, and narrow panels of gods and animals in fine, polished stone.

  3. 3. The Brihadisvara Temple Complex

    Rajendra Chola I was a king who named his capital after a river trip. After a military campaign that carried his armies as far as the Ganges, he founded a new city about the year 1025 and called it Gangaikonda Cholapuram, the city of the Chola who took the Ganges, and he built a great temple to Shiva at its heart. It stood for two and a half centuries as the capital of one of the great South Indian empires, which at its peak had a navy that reached Southeast Asia. Today the city has gone, but the temple remains, an enormous pyramid of granite rising about fifty-five metres above the surrounding fields, with a tower whose stepped profile is gently curved rather than straight, a style so refined that it feels almost organic.

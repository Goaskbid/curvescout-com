  1. 1. Quartiere Aja Piccola

    Alberobello is famous for its trulli, the little whitewashed stone houses with grey conical roofs that look as if they belong in a children's book. They stand in thousands across the Itria valley in Puglia, but in the town of Alberobello they come in two dense clusters on opposite hillsides, and the quarter called Aia Piccola is the quieter of the two. It holds around four hundred trulli, climbing a gentle slope, and unlike its showier neighbour across the valley, it is still mostly residential, with washing on lines, geraniums in pots and the occasional shop selling local cheese and wine. UNESCO listed the town in 1996.

  2. 2. Tarentum

    Taranto is a city between two seas. The old town sits on a small island between the Mar Grande, an open bay of the Gulf of Taranto, and the Mar Piccolo, an inner lagoon, connected to the mainland by bridges, including a swing bridge that opens to let warships pass into the naval base. Founded by Spartan colonists in the eighth century BC as Taras, it became one of the richest and most powerful cities of Magna Graecia, the Greek world in southern Italy, before falling to Rome.

  3. 3. The Via Appia from Mesochorum to Scamnum

    The last stretch of the Via Appia ran across this flat, sun-drenched part of Puglia toward Brundisium, the port from which Romans sailed to Greece and the East. The road, begun in 312 BC from Rome, was the queen of roads, and the whole route became a UNESCO site in 2024. The section between the stations the Romans called Mesochorum and Scamnum passes near Oria and Francavilla Fontana, and while little of the original paving survives here, the line of the road still shapes the landscape, with modern roads, field boundaries and farm tracks following its course.

  4. 4. The Appia Traiana along the Adriatic coast

    Between Ostuni and Brindisi, along the Adriatic coast of Puglia, ran the Via Appia Traiana, a road built by the emperor Trajan in the early second century as a faster alternative to the old Appian Way, carrying traffic to the port of Brundisium, the gateway to Greece and the East. In 2024, stretches of both roads were inscribed by UNESCO as Via Appia, Regina Viarum. Here the old route followed the coastal plain through olive groves, and traces of it, together with ancient settlements, can be found near the sea.

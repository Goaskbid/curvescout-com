  1. 1. Dolomiti Settentrionali Cadorine

    North of Cortina d'Ampezzo, the Dolomites change character. The soft meadows and pine woods of the Ampezzo basin give way to long grey screes, vertical walls and the pale towers that make the range so unmistakable, and the road toward Cimabanche runs through a valley framed by some of the most dramatic rock in Europe. This area belongs to the northern Cadore group in UNESCO's Dolomites listing of 2009, nine separate systems recognised for their landscape and their geology.

  2. 2. Sciliar-Catinaccio

    Watch the Rosengarten at sunset from the valley of Bolzano and you will understand the legend. As the sun goes down, the pale dolomite peaks glow pink, then red, then purple, a phenomenon the Ladin people call enrosadira. According to the old story, King Laurin of the dwarves had a magnificent rose garden here, and when he was captured by enemies who had spotted his roses, he cursed the garden so that no one would see it by day or by night. He forgot the twilight, and so, for a few minutes each evening, the roses bloom again on the rocks. Together with the Sciliar, or Schlern, the great flat-topped massif to the north, the Catinaccio forms one of the components of the Dolomites UNESCO World Heritage site, listed in 2009.

  3. 3. Le Colline del Prosecco di Conegliano e Va

    These are not soft, rolling wine hills. Between Conegliano and Valdobbiadene, the foothills of the Pre-Alps rise in steep hogback ridges, sharp crests of rock running east to west, with vines clinging to slopes so steep that much of the work is still done by hand. Grass-covered terraces called ciglioni hold the soil in place, and the result is a patchwork of vines, woods and small villages that UNESCO listed in 2019.

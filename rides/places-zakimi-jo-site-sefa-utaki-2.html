Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Zakimi-jô site

    The walls of Zakimi gusuku curve across the top of a hill in Yomitan like a ribbon of pale limestone, following the contour of the land in graceful arcs rather than straight lines. Built in the early fifteenth century by Gosamaru, a celebrated castle builder of the Ryukyu Kingdom, the fortress is small but beautifully made. Its arched stone gateways, held in place by a wedge-shaped keystone, are among the oldest stone arches in Okinawa. Zakimi is part of the UNESCO site of the Gusuku Sites and Related Properties of the Kingdom of Ryukyu, listed in 2000.

  2. 2. Gusuku Sites and Related Properties of the

    Okinawa was a kingdom of its own until the late nineteenth century, and the gusuku are what is left of its stone fortresses. The word means castle, but these are curving walls of pale coral limestone that follow the shape of a hill, with gateways like keyholes and view platforms over the sea. The Ryukyu Kingdom, which was unified under King Sho Hashi in 1429, traded between China, Japan, Korea and southeast Asia, and grew rich as a middle man, with Shuri as its capital. UNESCO listed the Gusuku Sites and Related Properties of the Kingdom of Ryukyu in 2000, among them Shuri Castle, the royal burial place of Tamaudun and the garden of Shikinaen.

  3. 3. Katsuren-jô site

    Katsuren castle stands on a limestone hill on the east coast of Okinawa, near the base of the Katsuren peninsula, and its curving walls step up the slope in a series of enclosures to a summit with sweeping views across the sea. It is one of the gusuku, the castles of the old Ryukyu kingdom and the warring lords who came before it, and it was inscribed in 2000 together with Nakijin, Zakimi, Nakagusuku, Shuri and several sacred groves.

  4. 4. Sêfa-utaki

    For the people of the old Ryukyu Kingdom, there was no holier place than Sēfa-utaki, a grove of forest and rock on the southeastern tip of Okinawa island. An utaki is a sacred site in the Ryukyuan religion, often a grove, a rock or a spring, where gods are believed to descend, and this one was the most important of all. The kingdom's highest priestess, the Kikoe-ōkimi, usually a woman of the royal family, was inaugurated here, and for centuries men were not allowed to enter.

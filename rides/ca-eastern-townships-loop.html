  1. 1. Sherbrooke

    Sherbrooke is the metropolis of the Eastern Townships, the hilly, lake-studded region of southern Quebec that was settled by American Loyalists after the Revolution and has kept an unusual blend of English and French culture. The city sits where the Magog River meets the Saint-François, and the falls on the Magog once powered its textile mills. It was named after Sir John Coape Sherbrooke, a British governor, and today about a hundred and thirty thousand people live here, making it the largest city in the region.

  2. 2. Magog

    Lake Memphremagog starts at Magog and runs south for some forty kilometres, long and narrow between wooded hills, until it crosses the border and ends in Newport, Vermont. Most of its length lies in Quebec, but the water does not care about borders, and neither does Memphré, the lake monster that local legend has been reporting for more than a century, a long dark shape in the water that has never been photographed convincingly. Magog itself is a town of about fifteen thousand at the northern tip of the lake, with a lakeside promenade, a beach and a lively main street.

  3. 3. Sutton

    A ski mountain, a main street lined with old wooden and brick buildings, art galleries and a couple of good coffee shops: Sutton is the kind of small Townships town that feels lived in rather than staged. It sits in the shadow of Mont Sutton, part of the Sutton Mountains, a northern extension of Vermont's Green Mountains and therefore of the Appalachians, and the US border lies just over ten kilometres south. About four thousand people live in the town and its surrounding countryside.

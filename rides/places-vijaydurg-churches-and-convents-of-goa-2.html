Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Vijaydurg

    Vijaydurg is a sea fort on the Konkan coast of Maharashtra, built on a rocky headland where the Vaghotan river runs into the Arabian Sea, and the setting alone is worth the ride. The fort was an old hill-fort of the Shilahara king Bhoja before Shivaji took it in the seventeenth century, rebuilt it, and made it one of the main bases of the Maratha navy. Later Kanhoji Angre, the admiral who held the coast against Portuguese, British and Dutch ships, used it as a harbour for his fleet, and the walls, bastions and triple line of defences look out over water that his ships once patrolled. They say there was an underwater wall to wreck enemy vessels that came too close, a story told at the gate with some pride.

  2. 2. Sindhudurg

    Shivaji built Sindhudurg on a rock. The fort stands on a small island just off Malvan, on the Konkan coast of Maharashtra, a great enclosure of black stone walls, about three kilometres around, built between 1664 and 1667 to guard against sea raiders and to project the power of the young Maratha state. Its walls rise straight from the surf, with bastions, gateways and hidden entrances, and inside lie temples, wells of fresh water, and the remains of old houses. A short boat ride from Malvan's jetty takes you across, and the view from the ramparts, over the blue Arabian Sea and the coconut-fringed coast, is superb.

  3. 3. Churches and Convents of Goa

    Goa's early Portuguese rulers built a capital that they hoped would rival Lisbon, and what is left of it, Old Goa, is a group of huge churches rising from tropical green. Albuquerque took the city in 1510, and for the next two centuries it was the centre of Portugal's empire in the East, with a population that may have rivalled London's. Then epidemics and a shifting river drove the people away, the capital moved down the coast, and the great buildings were left standing in a landscape of palms and rice fields. UNESCO listed the Churches and Convents of Goa in 1986.

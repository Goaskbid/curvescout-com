Why this ride

A 239 km round trip from Salamanca - roughly 4 h 07 in the saddle. On the way it passes Hermitage of San Segundo, Church of Santa María de la Cabeza, Convent of La Encarnación and Church of San Martín. The highest point is about 1,350 m.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Salamanca

    Salamanca glows. Its buildings are built from a golden sandstone that takes on a honey colour in low sun, and the effect, especially in the late afternoon in the Plaza Mayor, is memorable. The city stands on the Tormes river in western Spain, about 800 metres above sea level, and is one of the great university towns of Europe, with a student population that gives the old streets life well into the night.

  2. 2. Hermitage of San Segundo

    Outside the great medieval walls of Ávila, down by the river Adaja, a small Romanesque church stands among trees, quiet and often overlooked by the crowds heading for the walls and the cathedral. The Hermitage of San Segundo is dedicated to the first bishop of Ávila, according to tradition one of seven early missionaries sent to Spain, and it holds an alabaster statue of the saint at prayer, carved in the sixteenth century by the sculptor Juan de Juni. The building itself is simple and austere, with a beautiful carved doorway, and it is a lovely, peaceful place to visit. It is part of the UNESCO listing of the Old Town of Ávila with its Extra-Muros Churches.

  3. 3. Church of Santa María de la Cabeza

    Ávila is the highest provincial capital in Spain, at about 1,130 metres, and you feel it in the air even in summer. Its walls, begun in the late eleventh century and finished in the twelfth, run for around two and a half kilometres with 88 towers and nine gates, and they are among the best-preserved medieval city walls in Europe. The Church of Santa María de la Cabeza stands just outside the old town, close enough to the ramparts that a rider can park and walk in a few minutes, and it belongs to the group of Romanesque churches that UNESCO added to the old town's listing.

  4. 4. Convent of La Encarnación

    Teresa de Cepeda y Ahumada entered the Convent of the Incarnation in Ávila in 1535, when she was about twenty, and she stayed for twenty-seven years. The convent was large, comfortable and rather sociable, with visitors in the parlour and a lively social life among the nuns, and it was only after decades of illness, prayer and unease that she left in 1562 to found a small, strict house of her own, San José, the first of her reformed Carmelite convents. She became Saint Teresa of Jesus, one of the great writers of the Spanish language, and her cell and the rooms where she lived are still shown in the convent, a short distance outside the old walls.

  5. 5. Church of San Martín

    Ávila has a way of making you feel as though the walls are the only thing holding the past in place, but San Martín reminds you that the neighbourhoods around the churches were once lively parishes of their own. The city had many such parishes in the Middle Ages, each with its own church, its own priest and its own sense of rivalry with the next. Today they mostly sit quietly, built of the same grey-gold granite as the walls, and a walk from one to the next shows how the town's life was once organised.

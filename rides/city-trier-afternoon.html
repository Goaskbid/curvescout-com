Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Trier

    Trier claims to be the oldest city in Germany, and it can make a respectable case. The Romans founded it as Augusta Treverorum around the end of the first century before Christ, and by the fourth century it was one of the capitals of the empire, a city of emperors, with palaces, baths and a circus. Constantine the Great lived here for years before he moved east, and the city's buildings from this period are so impressive that UNESCO lists a group of them together with the cathedral and the Church of Our Lady.

  2. 2. City of Luxembourg: its Old Quarters and F

    Luxembourg City is built on a rocky plateau above the deep, green valleys of the Alzette and Pétrusse rivers, and for centuries it was one of the most powerful fortresses in Europe, nicknamed the Gibraltar of the North. Spanish, French, Austrian and Prussian armies all held it, and each added to its walls, bastions and tunnels. In 1867, after an international treaty, most of the fortifications were dismantled, but enough remains to make the old quarters and fortifications a UNESCO World Heritage site, inscribed in 1994.

  4. 4. Völklingen Ironworks

    Rust, soot and colossal machinery: the Völklingen Ironworks is a cathedral of heavy industry, and walking through it is an overwhelming experience. Founded in 1873 and developed by the Röchling family into one of the largest iron producers in Europe, the works produced pig iron from 1883 until 1986, when the blast furnaces were shut down. Instead of being demolished, the complex was preserved, and in 1994 it became the first industrial monument from the heyday of heavy industry to be listed by UNESCO. Today visitors can climb the blast furnaces, walk through the huge blowing engine hall, and explore the coking plant and the ore storage, much of which has been taken over by nature, with birch trees and wild plants growing among the steel.

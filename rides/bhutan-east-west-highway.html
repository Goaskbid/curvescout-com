Why this ride

496 km from Paro to Trashigang, about 6 h 42 of riding. It goes by way of Thimphu, Dochula Pass, Punakha and Trongsa. The highest point is about 4,000 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

For 51 km this is Bhutan East-West Highway, a road with a name of its own and a reason for it — expect company on a summer weekend. Early is better than fast.

Bhutan East-West Highway

The last of the trees go and the road runs out onto open ground. The summit sits at 3100 m, and what surrounds it is rock, weather and distance. Cold comes early up here and stays late. Ride it with something in reserve: the summit is not the end of the work, only the middle of it.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Paro

    Paro lies on a broad, flat valley floor at roughly twenty-two hundred metres in western Bhutan, which makes it the most civilised-looking place in a country of narrow gorges and steep forests. The Paro Chhu river runs through a patchwork of rice fields, and farmhouses with painted windows and carved wooden eaves sit among apple orchards, with pine-covered mountains on either side. The country's only international airport is here, and the approach, threading between peaks with the pilot flying by sight, is considered one of the trickiest in the world.

  2. 2. Thimphu

    Thimphu is a capital city with no traffic lights. The government has decided that the lights would spoil the town's character, and at the main crossing a policeman in white gloves directs the cars with elegant, slow gestures. The city of about a hundred thousand lies in a valley at around 2,300 metres, along the Wang Chhu river, with prayer flags fluttering on the hillsides and the great white and gold dzong, the fortress that serves as the seat of government and the home of the monastic body, standing beside the river. Most buildings are built in the traditional style, with painted wooden windows and sloping roofs, as required by law.

  3. 3. Dochula Pass

    Dochula Pass lies at 3,100 metres on the road between Thimphu and Punakha, and it is the first big climb eastward out of the capital. The road leaves the valley and winds up through forest of blue pine and rhododendron, in a long series of hairpin bends, to a saddle hung with thousands of prayer flags. Mist often fills the pass in the morning, giving the place a hushed, otherworldly look, and the temperature drops sharply, so a warm layer is needed even in summer.

  4. 4. Punakha

    Punakha sits at the meeting of two rivers, the Pho Chhu and the Mo Chhu, the father and mother rivers, and the great dzong of Punakha stands on the tongue of land between them, its whitewashed walls and gold roofs reflected in the water. It was built in the seventeenth century by Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal, the founder of the Bhutanese state, and served as the capital until the middle of the twentieth century. It remains the winter home of the monastic body. Reaching it involves crossing a long wooden cantilever bridge, with prayer flags overhead, and the approach is memorable.

  5. 5. Trongsa

    Trongsa is where Bhutan keeps its spine. The enormous dzong, sprawling down a ridge above the Mangde river gorge, was founded in the seventeenth century, and it is the ancestral seat of the royal family, whose first kings governed from here before ruling the whole country. Tradition says the crown prince still serves as governor of Trongsa before taking the throne. From the road, the building appears and disappears across the valley for a long time before you actually arrive, because the highway has to wriggle all the way round the hillside to reach it.

  6. 6. Bumthang

    Bumthang is not a single valley but a cluster of four, high in the middle of Bhutan, and it is regarded as the country's spiritual heartland. The main town, Jakar, lies at about 2,600 metres, and is a small place with a big dzong, the Yuedrel Tse, standing on the ridge above the town, as well as one of the country's oldest temples, Jambay Lhakhang, which is said to date from the seventh century. Other temples, such as Kurjey Lhakhang and Tamshing, hold a place in the history of Buddhism in Bhutan, and the valley is full of stories about the saint Guru Rinpoche, who is said to have meditated here.

  7. 7. Trashigang

    Trashigang is as far east as the highway goes in Bhutan, and it feels like it. The town clings to a steep hillside above the Gamri river, with its whitewashed dzong, a fortress-monastery from the seventeenth century, perched on a spur overlooking the valley, and houses stacked on the slopes in terraces. It is the administrative centre of one of the largest districts in the country, and the market on weekends draws people from the surrounding villages, many wearing the distinctive dress of their communities.

Recorded leg-by-leg notes

These source notes may contain earlier estimates. They are not turn-by-turn directions or current measured distances.

Paro to Thimphu

Paro → Thimphu is 28 km of rhythm rather than effort. Let it flow and let the view breathe.

Thimphu to Dochula Pass

Thimphu → Dochula Pass climbs about 3100 m in 14 km. This is the part worth slowing down for - look through the bends and use the pull-offs.

Dochula Pass to Punakha

Dochula Pass → Punakha drops about 3100 m over 22 km. Brakes and eyes forward; the views are behind the corners, not in them.

Punakha to Trongsa

Punakha → Trongsa is 78 km of getting across. Ride it tidily and keep the enthusiasm for what comes after.

Trongsa to Bumthang

Trongsa → Bumthang is 31 km of rhythm rather than effort. Let it flow and let the view breathe.

Bumthang to Trashigang

Bumthang → Trashigang is 103 km of getting across. Ride it tidily and keep the enthusiasm for what comes after.

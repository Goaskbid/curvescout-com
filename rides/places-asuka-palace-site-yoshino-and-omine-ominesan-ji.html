Why this ride

A 129 km round trip from Sakurai - roughly 2 h in the saddle. It goes by way of Yoshino and Ômine - Yoshimizu-jinja, Yoshino and Ômine - Ôminesan-ji and back through Yoshino and Ômine - Yoshimizu-jinja. It tops out around 800 m. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

The stops

  1. 1. Asuka Palace Site

    Start here.

    Few places in Japan can claim as much early history as the plain of Asuka, in the south of the Nara basin. In the seventh century, before Nara or Kyoto existed, the court moved from place to place, and for much of that century the centre of power was here. The Asuka Palace Site, also called Itabuki, was the residence of several sovereigns, and the building was reworked more than once. In 645, in the audience hall of this palace, the court strongman Soga no Iruka was cut down in front of the empress by Prince Naka no Oe and Nakatomi no Kamatari, an assassination that opened the way to the Taika reforms, the first attempt to build a centralised state on the Chinese model.

    Read the complete Asuka Palace Site write-up.

  2. 2. Yoshino and Ômine - Yoshimizu-jinja

    21 km in.

    Yoshino is the most famous cherry-blossom mountain in Japan, and it earns the fame. Tens of thousands of trees, planted over centuries as offerings by pilgrims, climb the ridge in four zones, so the bloom moves up the mountain through April like a slow pink tide. But Yoshino is older and stranger than a flower festival. It is the gateway to the Kii mountains, a vast tangle of steep forested ranges where mountain ascetics of the Shugendo tradition have walked for more than a thousand years, and the pilgrimage path of Omine runs along the high ridges to Kumano, passing places so sacred that women were long banned from entering. The Sacred Sites and Pilgrimage Routes in the Kii Mountain Range were inscribed by UNESCO in 2004.

    Read the complete Yoshino and Ômine - Yoshimizu-jinja write-up.

  3. 3. Yoshino and Ômine - Ôminesan-ji

    65 km in.

    Ōminesan-ji is a temple on the summit of Mount Sanjō, one of the peaks of the Ōmine range in the heart of the Kii peninsula, and it holds a special place in Japanese religion. It is a centre of Shugendō, the mountain ascetic tradition that blends Buddhism, Shinto and older mountain worship, and its practitioners, the yamabushi, undergo arduous training on the ridges. The temple is famous for another reason too: the mountain is still closed to women by tradition, one of the last such places in Japan, which has become a subject of ongoing debate.

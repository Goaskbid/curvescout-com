Why this ride

A 186 km round trip from Xi’an - roughly 3 h 12 in the saddle. On the way it passes Xingjiaosi Pagodas, Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor, Pits of Terracotta Army and back through Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor. The highest point is about 1,200 m.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Xian

    Xi'an was once among the largest cities in the world. Under the Tang dynasty, in the seventh and eighth centuries, it was called Chang'an, the eastern end of the Silk Road, and a million people lived within its walls, among them merchants from Persia and Central Asia, monks from India and envoys from Japan and Korea. Today the city is a big modern metropolis of more than six million people, but its old centre still has the shape of a walled city, with a massive Ming dynasty wall, about fourteen kilometres in circumference, running round it, and a Bell Tower and Drum Tower in the middle.

  2. 2. Xingjiaosi Pagodas

    When the monk Xuanzang returned to Chang'an in the seventh century after sixteen years of travel to India, he brought back hundreds of Buddhist scriptures, which he spent the rest of his life translating, and his journey later inspired the classic novel Journey to the West, with its Monkey King. After his death in 664, his remains were buried at Xingjiao Temple, on the slopes south of the city, and a five-storey brick pagoda was built over them in 669. Next to it stand two smaller pagodas for two of his disciples. The three pagodas, in a peaceful temple setting, are part of the Silk Roads World Heritage site, a tribute to one of the most famous travellers along that route.

  3. 3. Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor

    Beneath a large, tree-covered mound at the foot of Mount Li, east of Xi'an, lies the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, who unified China in 221 BC and became its first emperor. He ruled for only eleven years more, dying in 210 BC, but in that time he standardised weights, measures, writing and the axle width of carts, linked the northern walls into an early Great Wall, and conscripted hundreds of thousands of labourers for his projects. The tomb mound, which still rises about fifty metres, has never been opened. The ancient historian Sima Qian wrote that the burial chamber held a model of the empire with rivers of mercury, and modern soil tests have found high levels of mercury around the mound.

  5. 5. Pits of Terracotta Army

    In 1974 a group of farmers digging a well near the town of Lintong in Shaanxi struck something hard, and so began one of the great archaeological finds of the twentieth century. Below the farmland lay the Terracotta Army, thousands of life-size clay soldiers, horses and chariots, buried to guard the tomb of Qin Shi Huang, the man who unified China in 221 BC and became its first emperor. He ordered the burial complex to be started when he came to the throne, and tens of thousands of workers laboured on it for decades.

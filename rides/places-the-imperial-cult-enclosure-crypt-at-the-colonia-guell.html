Why this ride

A 192 km round trip from Tarragona - roughly 3 h 19 in the saddle. On the way it passes Triumphal Arch of Berá, Abric de Can Castellvi, Crypt at the Colònia Güell and back through Abric de Can Castellvi. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. The imperial cult enclosure

    When the Emperor Augustus died in AD 14, the people of Tarraco asked permission to build a temple to him, and Tiberius agreed. Tacitus records the request, and the rhetorician Quintilian preserves a joke from the emperor's lifetime: when the people of Tarraco reported that a palm tree had sprouted on his altar, Augustus replied that it showed how seldom they lit the fire on it. The imperial cult enclosure, the sacred precinct of the provincial capital, occupied the top of the hill, and what survives of it now lies under and beside the cathedral of Tarragona.

  2. 2. Triumphal Arch of Berá

    For centuries traffic on the main coastal road of Catalonia drove straight through a Roman triumphal arch. The Arc de Berà stands on the line of the Via Augusta, the great Roman road from the Pyrenees to Cádiz, about twenty kilometres northeast of Tarragona, and until the road was diverted around it in the late twentieth century, cars and trucks passed under its single span. It was built at the end of the first century BC, and an inscription records that it was erected according to the will of Lucius Licinius Sura, a figure whose identity historians still discuss. It is part of the UNESCO listing for the Roman remains of Tarraco.

  3. 3. Abric de Can Castellvi

    The Penedès is wine country, and the landscape around Vilafranca del Penedès is a patchwork of vineyards rolling away toward the Montserrat massif in the north and the limestone hills of the Garraf in the south. Abric de Can Castellví is a small rock shelter in this landscape, one of the many sites of Levantine and schematic rock art scattered across Catalonia and included in the UNESCO serial site of the rock art of the Mediterranean Basin on the Iberian Peninsula. Like many such sites, it is modest in appearance, a hollow in the rock where faint painted figures have survived thousands of years, and access is often limited to protect the art.

  4. 4. Crypt at the Colònia Güell

    Few people ride out of Barcelona expecting to find Gaudí in a mill village, but that is exactly what waits at Santa Coloma de Cervelló, a short hop up the Llobregat from Sant Boi. The Colònia Güell was a textile colony founded by the industrialist Eusebi Güell, who moved his cotton works out of the city in the 1890s and built housing, a school, shops and a theatre for the workers around the factory. He asked his friend Antoni Gaudí for a church. Gaudí spent about ten years on the design and its foundations, and when money and the Güell family's enthusiasm ran out around 1914, only the lower level had been finished. That crypt is what you come to see.

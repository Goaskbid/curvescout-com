  1. 1. Panama City

    Panama City is the only capital in the world with a rainforest inside its limits, and a canal at its door. The city curves round a bay on the Pacific side, with a skyline of glass towers that looks more like Miami than anything in Central America, and behind it the old quarter, Casco Viejo, is a peninsula of balconied houses, plazas and church towers rebuilt after the pirate Henry Morgan sacked the first settlement in 1671. The ruins of that first city, Panamá Viejo, still stand a few kilometres east, with a bell tower you can climb.

  2. 2. Near Colón

    West of the Panama Canal's Caribbean end, the land goes quiet surprisingly fast. You cross from the Colón side on the Atlantic Bridge, the long cable-stayed span that finally replaced the old swing bridge and the ferry over the canal entrance, and within minutes the container cranes give way to rainforest, old American base buildings and a narrow road heading north toward the mouth of the Chagres River.

  3. 3. The Colonial Transisthmian Route of Panamá

    The Camino Real, the royal road that Spain built across the Isthmus of Panama in the sixteenth century, is the reason this country matters in world history. Gold and silver from Peru were carried by ship to Panama City, loaded on mules and carried over the continental divide to the Caribbean coast, then shipped to Spain. The route had two arms: a paved mule road, the Camino Real, to Portobelo, and a mixed river-and-trail route, the Camino de Cruces, which used the Chagres River for part of the way. Together with the forts that guarded them, the Caribbean forts at Portobelo and San Lorenzo are on the UNESCO list, listed in 1980.

  4. 4. Castle of San Lorenzo

    The Castle of San Lorenzo stands on a cliff at the mouth of the Chagres River, on the Caribbean coast of Panama, and it was built to stop pirates. Gold and silver came across the isthmus on the Camino Real and the river, and this stone fort guarded the river mouth from the sea. Construction began in the late sixteenth century, and the fort, with its ditch, ramparts and cannon, was a serious piece of military engineering. It did not hold forever. In 1671 Henry Morgan and a force of buccaneers took it after a fierce fight in which the Spanish defenders resisted until their thatched roofs caught fire. Morgan went on to sack Panama City.

  5. 5. San Lorenzo

    Fort San Lorenzo stands on a cliff above the mouth of the Chagres River, and from the old walls you can see the Caribbean on one side and a green tangle of jungle on the other. It was built by the Spanish in the sixteenth century to guard the point where the river meets the sea, because the Chagres was the water road for the treasure caravans coming across the isthmus. A fort was only as good as its garrison, though, and in 1671 the Welsh buccaneer Henry Morgan took it, with a force of a few hundred men, before marching on Panama City itself.

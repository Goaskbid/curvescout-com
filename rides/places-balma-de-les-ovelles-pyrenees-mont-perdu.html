  1. 1. Balma de les Ovelles

    The Balma de les Ovelles, the Sheep's Shelter, lies in the high country where Catalonia meets Aragon, between the valleys of the Noguera Ribagorçana and the Noguera Pallaresa. This is the southern edge of the Pyrenees, a land of limestone ridges, deep gorges, reservoirs and scattered villages, many of them half empty. Rock shelters like this one, used by shepherds and their flocks for thousands of years, are found throughout the region, and some hold traces of prehistoric painting or occupation. The name tells you what it was most recently used for, and the landscape around still carries the sound of sheep bells.

  2. 2. Eglésia de Santa Maria de l'Assumpció

    Valleys in the high Pyrenees of Catalonia and Aragon are full of small Romanesque churches, and the area around the Alta Ribagorça is among the richest. They were built in the eleventh and twelfth centuries by craftsmen working in what is called the Lombard style, with plain stone walls, round arches and tall, slender bell towers decorated with blind arcades, and they stand in villages that often have only a few houses.

  3. 3. Eglésia de Santa Eulàlia

    The little Romanesque church of Santa Eulàlia stands at Erill la Vall, one of the villages of the Vall de Boí in the Catalan Pyrenees, and its tall, slim bell tower is a landmark from the road. Together with ten other churches and hermitages in the valley, it was listed by UNESCO in 2000, and the whole group is a small marvel. They were built in the early twelfth century, in a style borrowed from Lombardy, with stout stone walls, round arches and bell towers decorated with blind arcades. Inside, the frescoes that once covered the walls were taken down in the early twentieth century and are now in the museum in Barcelona, while replicas have been placed on site.

  4. 4. Cueva de Revilla

    Revilla is one of the most spectacularly placed hamlets in the Spanish Pyrenees, perched on a ledge above the Garganta de Escuaín, a deep gorge cut into the limestone of the Ordesa y Monte Perdido National Park. It looks down into the canyon from a terrace of meadows, with the high peaks behind. The Cueva de Revilla is a cave in the cliffs near the hamlet, and the area between Bielsa, Plan and Boltaña is some of the wildest country in Aragón.

  5. 5. Pyrénées - Mont Perdu

    Monte Perdido, the lost mountain, at 3,355 metres, is the highest limestone massif in Europe and the centrepiece of a cross-border World Heritage site shared by Spain and France. On the Spanish side, it gives its name to the Ordesa y Monte Perdido National Park, with deep canyons radiating out from the summit; on the French side, it looks down on the great cirques of Gavarnie and Troumouse.

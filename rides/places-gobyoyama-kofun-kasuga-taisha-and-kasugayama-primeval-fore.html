Why this ride

A 175 km round trip from Sakai - roughly 3 h 01 in the saddle. It goes by way of Buddhist Monuments in the Horyu-ji Area, Tachibana-dera Temple Site, Kasuga-Taisha and Kasugayama Primeval Fore and back through Tachibana-dera Temple Site. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Gobyoyama Kofun

    The Gobyoyama tomb is the resting place attributed to Emperor Richu, the seventeenth emperor in the traditional count, and it is a giant: a keyhole-shaped mound in Sakai more than three hundred metres long, one of the biggest in the whole country. It was built in the late fourth or early fifth century, and its builders moved an enormous amount of earth. Surfaces were covered with fitted stones, and rows of haniwa figures stood on the terraces, so that in its day the whole mound would have gleamed like a stepped pyramid.

  2. 2. Buddhist Monuments in the Horyu-ji Area

    If you want to stand in front of the oldest wooden buildings in the world, this is the place. Hōryū-ji, a Buddhist temple in the town of Ikaruga south-west of Nara, was founded by Prince Shōtoku in the early seventh century, and although it burned and was rebuilt not long after, its central buildings, including the main hall and the five-storey pagoda, date from around the end of the seventh century. They were among the first sites in Japan inscribed by UNESCO, in 1993.

  3. 3. Tachibana-dera Temple Site

    Asuka is where Japan as a state began to take shape, and Tachibana-dera sits right in the middle of it, among rice paddies and low hills in a small valley south of Nara. Tradition holds that Prince Shōtoku, the regent who promoted Buddhism and wrote Japan's first constitution in the early seventh century, was born here, and the temple claims to have been founded by him. The present buildings are much later, but the site is ancient, and the atmosphere of the quiet temple grounds, with their wooden halls, gravel courtyards and a curious stone carved with two faces, one good and one evil, is very special.

  4. 4. Kasuga-Taisha and Kasugayama Primeval Fore

    Kasugayama is a forest that has been protected since the ninth century, which is why it feels different from any other woodland in the region. Hunting and logging were banned here in 841, because the hill is sacred to the Kasuga shrine, and the result is a primeval stand of cedar, cypress, oak and evergreens on the slopes just east of Nara. Deer roam through it, as they do through the park below, and in autumn their calls echo through the trees. In the shrine's grounds, thousands of stone lanterns line the paths, donated by worshippers over the centuries, and thousands of bronze lanterns hang from the eaves, lit twice a year during the lantern festivals.

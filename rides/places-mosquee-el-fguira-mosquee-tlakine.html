Why this ride

A 142 km round trip from Erriadh - roughly 3 h in the saddle. The stops in between: Synagogue La Ghriba, Mosquée Sidi Zekri, Mosquée Moghzel and Mosquée Tlakine.

The stops

  1. 1. Mosquée El Fguira

    Start here.

    El Fguira stands in the south of Djerba, a long way from the bustle of Houmt Souk, in a part of the island where the farms are bigger and the roads quiet. The label puts it among the more remote of the mosques that make up the Djerba listing, and the journey is a pleasure in itself. The mosque is a small, whitewashed building with strong buttresses and a rounded roof, standing among olive trees and palms, and its shape is as simple as a child's drawing of a house. Like the others, it is still used for prayer, so it should be seen from outside and with respect.

    Read the complete Mosquée El Fguira write-up.

  2. 2. Synagogue La Ghriba

    16 km in.

    La Ghriba stands on the island of Djerba, just outside the village of Erriadh, and it is among the oldest Jewish places of worship in North Africa. The name means the Stranger, or the Marvellous, and the stories about its beginning stretch back to a priest who fled Jerusalem after the destruction of the First Temple, bringing a door or a stone. Whatever the truth of that, the building you see today is much later, a nineteenth-century structure with a whitewashed exterior and an interior of blue, turquoise and gold tiles, carved wood and low lamps that give the prayer hall an underwater glow.

    Read the complete Synagogue La Ghriba write-up.

  3. 3. Mosquée Sidi Zekri

    36 km in.

    Sidi Zekri is one of the many small religious buildings of Djerba, an island where it sometimes seems that every field has a whitewashed dome in it. The name suggests a local holy man, the sort of marabout who is buried under a domed tomb and whose tomb draws believers seeking help with illness or family troubles. Many such places are small and quiet, and are looked after by neighbours rather than by any big institution.

    Read the complete Mosquée Sidi Zekri write-up.

  4. 4. Mosquée Moghzel

    54 km in.

    Djerba is a flat, low island off the south coast of Tunisia, joined to the mainland by a Roman causeway and a ferry, and its landscape of olive groves, palms and whitewashed farmhouses is dotted with small mosques that look more like fortresses than places of prayer. The Moghzel mosque is one of them. Djerba has a long Ibadi tradition, a branch of Islam that is neither Sunni nor Shia, and many of the island's villages are Berber speaking, with architecture suited to hard times: thick walls, buttresses, small domes, and a plain whitewashed look that fits the sun and the sea.

    Read the complete Mosquée Moghzel write-up.

  5. 5. Mosquée Tlakine

    71 km in.

    Djerba is covered in small mosques, hundreds of them, and the visitor who sets out to find the quiet ones soon realises that you cannot ride more than a few kilometres without passing a whitewashed dome. Tlakine is one of those village mosques on the island's interior, set among olive and palm plantations between Houmt El Souk and the villages to the east. Most Djerbian mosques belong to the Ibadi tradition, a distinctive branch of Islam that survives in only a few places, including Oman, Djerba and parts of the Mzab in Algeria. The island's Ibadi community took refuge here when persecuted elsewhere, and built mosques that look more like fortresses than places of worship.

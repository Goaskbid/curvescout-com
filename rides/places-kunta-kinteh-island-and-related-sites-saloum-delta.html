Why this ride

From Lamin it runs 186 km to Lamin; allow about 7 h of riding. The stops in between: Six-Gun Battery, Saloum Delta and back through Six-Gun Battery. It stays on the island.

The stops

  1. 1. Kunta Kinteh Island and Related Sites

    Start here.

    In the brown tidal water of the River Gambia, some thirty kilometres from the Atlantic, a small low island of rock and scrub carries the ruins of a fort that mattered far beyond its size. For centuries Portuguese, Courlanders, English and French all fought to control this reach of the river, the gateway to the gold, ivory, wax and, above all, people that came down from the interior. The island was long called James Island, after a Stuart prince, and in 2011 its name was officially changed to Kunta Kinteh, after the ancestor in Alex Haley's novel Roots, who is said to have been taken from the nearby village of Juffureh. Whether or not the family story holds in every detail, the island stands for the whole trade, and the World Heritage listing covers the island and its related sites on the banks.

    Read the complete Kunta Kinteh Island and Related Sites write-up.

  2. 2. Six-Gun Battery

    41 km in.

    The Gambia is a long, narrow country that follows its river, and Banjul sits at the mouth where the river meets the Atlantic, on a low, sandy island that feels like a sandbar with ambitions. The city was founded by the British in 1816 as Bathurst, a base to suppress the slave trade, and the Six-Gun Battery is one of the military remains from that era. It forms part of the UNESCO site called Kunta Kinteh Island and Related Sites, which also includes Fort Bullen on the opposite bank at Barra, and together they tell the story of the Gambia river as a route of the Atlantic slave trade and of the later effort to end it.

    Read the complete Six-Gun Battery write-up.

  3. 3. Saloum Delta

    93 km in.

    The Saloum Delta is where the river Saloum and its arms dissolve into a maze of mangrove channels, sandbanks and low islands before meeting the Atlantic, about halfway between Dakar and the Gambian border. It is a flat, watery, shimmering place, where the horizon is a line of mangroves and the only things standing taller than a man are baobabs and the occasional village water tower. Locals call the narrow creeks bolongs, and they wind through the delta like veins.

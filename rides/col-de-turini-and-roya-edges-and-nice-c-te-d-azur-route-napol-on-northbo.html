Why this ride

453 km from Sospel to Bordighera, about 8 h 14 of riding. It goes by way of La Bollène-Vésubie, in Bordighera and Aisone. The climbs: Col de Turini and Cap-d'Ail. It tops out at about 1,950 m. It crosses France, Italy and Monaco - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules. It is a high-mountain day.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Sospel fuel gate

    Sospel lies in the deep valley of the Bévéra, a small river that cuts through limestone mountains about twelve kilometres inland from Menton, and it feels like a different country from the coast. The air is cooler, the light is softer, and the town has a pleasant, slightly sleepy character with colonnaded squares and painted houses. Its medieval bridge, the Pont-Vieux, carries a tower that was once used to collect tolls from the traders, and although it was blown up in the Second World War and rebuilt, it remains the town's postcard image. Rows of pale and ochre buildings crowd along the river.

  2. 2. Col de Turini

    Turini belongs to the Monte Carlo Rally more than to any map. For decades the rally's night stage over this pass has been one of the most famous in motorsport, the Nuit du Turini, when spectators light fires in the forest and cars appear out of the darkness with a wall of floodlights. At 1,607 metres, the col is not especially high, but it is wrapped in a dense forest of fir and larch, which is why the hairpins on both sides are so intimate, with rock walls and tree trunks flashing past close to your shoulder.

  3. 3. La Bollène-Vésubie food

    Rally fans know the name Turini the way footballers know Wembley, and La Bollène-Vésubie is the village at the foot of that legend. It sits on a spur above the Vésubie, a small cluster of stone houses in the lower reaches of the Mercantour foothills, with the road to the Col de Turini leaving the valley right beside it. The Monte-Carlo Rally used to send its cars up here in the dark, and the night stage over the Turini, with fans lighting fires along the hairpins, became one of motorsport's great spectacles.

  4. 4. Vésubie scenic roads

    Between Roquebillière and Saint-Martin-Vésubie, the valley road tightens and rises, and the scenery stops being merely pleasant. The river drops into a narrow gorge, the slopes steepen into dark chestnut and pine, and the villages that were built on spurs above the water start to look like they were fitted into the gaps. This is a landscape that rewards a rider who slows down, partly because the road demands it and partly because there is a great deal to look at.

  8. 8. Food stop near Annot

    Annot sits in the valley of the Vaïre in the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence, a medieval village of tall stone houses huddled beneath a backdrop of extraordinary sandstone boulders. These are the Grès d'Annot, huge blocks of sandstone scattered through the chestnut woods above the village, some of them with houses built right into them. The rock gives its name to a geological formation that is studied by geologists from around the world.

  9. 9. Photo stop near Saint-Raphaël

    Saint-Raphaël is the starting point of one of the most colourful roads in France, and the colour is its subject. The Esterel massif, a low range of volcanic rock behind the town, is made of red porphyry, which glows an almost unreal brick-red against the blue Mediterranean and the dark green of the pines. The town itself is a relaxed seaside resort of about thirty-five thousand people, with a promenade, a casino, a harbour and a handsome church in Neo-Byzantine style, Notre-Dame-de-la-Victoire, built in the nineteenth century.

  10. 10. Near Antibes

    On 1 March 1815, Napoleon, having escaped from exile on Elba, landed on the beach at Golfe-Juan, a few kilometres west of Antibes, with a small band of loyal soldiers, and began his march to Paris. The route he followed through the mountains of Provence to Grenoble is now the Route Napoléon, one of the classic motorcycle roads of France, and Antibes is where rides along it begin and end.

  11. 11. Cap-d'Ail

    Cap-d'Ail is the last French town before Monaco, and you can feel the principality pressing against it. The Basse Corniche winds in from Èze and Beaulieu between villas and pines, the rocks drop to clear water below, and then, almost without warning, you are among the towers of Fontvieille. Cap-d'Ail itself is quieter than its neighbour: a scatter of Belle Époque villas, gardens and newer apartments on the slopes above a small cape with coves and a little beach.

Why this ride

From Rome it runs 941 km to Parma; allow about 12 h 54 of riding. It goes by way of Siena and Florence. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Rome

    Rome is the end of the line for the Mille Miglia, or at least the halfway mark, because the old race ran out from Brescia to Rome and back again, a thousand Italian miles in all, which is where the name comes from. It ran from 1927 until 1957, mostly through towns and villages with the roads left open and crowds standing at the kerb, and the drivers flew down the Via Cassia and through the Apennine passes at speeds that make the whole thing seem barely believable now. It ended after a crash in 1957 that killed spectators, and was later revived as a rolling classic-car parade, which still comes through Rome every spring.

  2. 2. Siena

    Siena is the opposite of Florence in nearly every way that matters, and the Sienese would like you to remember it. It sits on three ridges in southern Tuscany, a compact medieval city of brick and stone in warm reddish-brown tones, and at its centre lies the Piazza del Campo, a vast shell-shaped square sloping down to a tall brick tower, the Torre del Mangia, about a hundred metres high. Twice each summer, on the second of July and the sixteenth of August, the square is covered with earth and ten riders from the city's districts, the contrade, race bareback around it in the Palio, a dangerous, chaotic event that lasts about ninety seconds and is felt by the whole city as a matter of honour.

  3. 3. Florence

    Florence was one of the great stops on the Mille Miglia, and it is easy to see why the race came through. Drivers arriving from Bologna had crossed the Raticosa and Futa passes in the Apennines, a long, twisting run of hairpins that tested both engines and nerves, then dropped down into the Arno valley to the city where Brunelleschi's red dome rises above a sea of terracotta roofs. The dome, completed in the fifteenth century over the cathedral, was a feat of engineering that was done without a conventional wooden frame, and it still defines the skyline.

  4. 4. Parma

    Parma sits on the Via Emilia, the straight Roman road that crosses the plain from Rimini to Piacenza, and today it is known above all for what is eaten there. Parmigiano-Reggiano and Prosciutto di Parma both come from this corner of Emilia, and a visit is not complete without a slice of each, accompanied by a glass of local sparkling red such as Lambrusco, which is sold everywhere. The pasta here is stuffed with spinach and ricotta, and the fried bread called torta fritta arrives with plates of cured meat.

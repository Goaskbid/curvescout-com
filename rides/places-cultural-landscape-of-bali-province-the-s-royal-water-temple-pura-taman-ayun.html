Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Cultural Landscape of Bali Province: the S

    Lake Batur lies in the bowl of an ancient volcanic caldera in the highlands of north-central Bali, and from the rim above it the view is unforgettable. The lake is crescent-shaped, with a young black cone, Mount Batur, rising from the caldera floor at about 1,700 metres, still steaming from vents on its flank. The road runs along the crater rim at Penelokan, a name that means the place to look, and on a clear morning the mist lies in the basin like milk in a bowl.

  2. 2. Subak Landscape of Pekerisan Watershed

    Bali's rice terraces look natural from a distance, but they are in fact a machine, an old and delicate piece of social engineering that shares water among thousands of farmers. The Pakerisan river runs south from the volcanic highlands near Tampaksiring, and along its length the fields, canals and temples form a landscape that UNESCO listed in 2012 as part of the cultural landscape of Bali, built on the subak system. Ubud, a sizeable town of twenty-eight thousand people, is about ten kilometres to the south-west.

  3. 3. Royal Water Temple Pura Taman Ayun

    Pura Taman Ayun means the temple of the beautiful garden, and it is a royal temple surrounded by a wide moat. The rajas of Mengwi built it in 1634, as the main temple of their small kingdom, and the broad lawns, canals and pavilions give it the air of a palace garden as much as a shrine. Across the inner courtyard rise the merus, shrines with tiered thatched roofs of black ijuk fibre, some with as many as eleven levels, which represent mountains and the homes of the gods. The temple lies in the middle of a district of rice fields, and it is part of the Subak cultural landscape that UNESCO listed in 2012, because the water for those fields is managed by village irrigation societies, the subak, which have been sharing it for a thousand years.

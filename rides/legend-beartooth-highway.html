Why this ride

US-212 to 3,337 m. Snowbanks in July. From Red Lodge it runs 77 km to the finish; allow about 1 h 24 of riding. The highest point is about 3,350 m. Beartooth Pass is open in summer only - check it is open before you set off. The line favours views over speed.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Red Lodge

    Red Lodge is the doorstep of the Beartooth Highway, and it knows it. A small town of just over two thousand people in south-central Montana, it sits at around five and a half thousand feet where the Beartooth Mountains meet the plains, and its main street of brick buildings, saloons, outfitters and cafes is lined with flower baskets in summer. Behind it, the huge granite wall of the range rises in a ragged line, still carrying snow in July.

  2. 2. Beartooth Highway point 2

    At the top of the Beartooth Pass, the world drops away. You stand at about 10,950 feet on a broad, treeless plateau of granite, tundra and tiny lakes, with snowfields clinging to the shaded slopes even in midsummer. The air is thin enough that you will notice it when you walk, and the sky is a deep, dark blue. In every direction, the rock is old: the Beartooth range is made of some of the oldest rock in North America, nearly three and a half billion years old in places, and the landscape has the bare, scoured look of something that has seen a very long time pass.

  3. 3. Beartooth Highway point 3

    This is the stretch where the Beartooth Highway shows off. After climbing out of the Rock Creek valley in a series of long switchbacks, the road emerges onto a vantage point with a big, open view back down the canyon. The Rock Creek Vista Point sits a little over nine thousand feet up, and from the railing you look down a deep glacial trough, a U-shaped valley carved by ice, with the road you have just climbed threading up its side and a tangle of peaks beyond.

  4. 4. Beartooth Highway point 4

    Cooke City is a tiny community at the end of the world, or so it can feel, wedged into a mountain valley just outside the north-east corner of Yellowstone National Park. It began as a mining camp in the 1880s, when prospectors found gold, silver and lead in the surrounding hills, and it was named for Jay Cooke, a financier who backed the Northern Pacific Railroad. The railway never came, however, and the boom faded, leaving a handful of log buildings and a small, hardy population.

  5. 5. Beartooth Highway point 5

    The northeast entrance to Yellowstone is one of the quietest of the park's five gates, a small station on a road that runs west along Soda Butte Creek into the Lamar Valley. Here you leave the high, bare country of the Beartooths and enter a land of rolling sagebrush, cottonwood and wide meadows, with mountains rising on every side. It feels very different from the crowded geyser basins to the south, and many visitors say it is the best part of the park.

Why this ride

A one-way ride of 88 km, Tula to Tarusa - roughly 1 h 42 in the saddle. It was put together for the scenery rather than the shortest line.

Roadbook

  1. Tula start

    Russians have a saying about carrying coals to Newcastle, and theirs is about taking your own samovar to Tula. The town has made samovars since the eighteenth century, and a museum of them stands in the center, with brass giants, tiny travelers' models and the elaborate urns used in merchant houses. Tula also made guns: Peter the Great founded an arms factory here in 1712, and the weapons works supplied the Russian army for centuries, so the old gunsmith's craft and the brass-tea tradition sit side by side. Add the gingerbread, spiced and printed with a pattern, and you have the three things Tula is known for.

    The Kremlin is the visual center, a red-brick fortress finished in the early sixteenth century on the bank of the Upa River, with towers, thick walls and a cathedral inside. It is more compact and less ceremonial than Moscow's, and it served as a frontier stronghold against Crimean Tatar raids. Inside the walls, a pair of museums, one for weapons, the other for local history, are worth an hour. The city has a long, rather industrial skyline, but the old streets near the river retain a handsome, provincial charm.

    Fourteen kilometers southwest lies Yasnaya Polyana, the estate where Leo Tolstoy was born in 1828, lived most of his life and wrote War and Peace and Anna Karenina. The house is preserved with his study and library, the park has birch alleys and ponds, and his grave, a simple grassy mound in a patch of woods, is deliberately unmarked by a cross or inscription, as he wished. It is one of the most moving places in Russia, and well worth the ride.

    Tula is a major city, so fuel, food and hotels are everywhere. The M2 highway links it to Moscow, about 180 kilometers north, and is fast but heavily used by trucks. The countryside to the south, near Kulikovo Field, where Dmitry Donskoy defeated the Mongol-Tatar forces in 1380, is open, rolling farmland with big skies. The best time is late spring or early autumn, when the light is soft and the roads are dry. Spring thaw and heavy winter snow make the side roads tricky, and summer heat can be intense on the open plain.

  2. Yasnaya Polyana scenic

    Yasnaya Polyana means clear glade, and the glade is where Leo Tolstoy was born in 1828, lived most of his life and wrote War and Peace and Anna Karenina. The estate lies a few kilometres south of Tula, in the open, rolling farmland of central Russia, and it is one of the most visited literary sites in the country. The house is plain, white and wooden-floored, full of books, and it feels like a place where a family really lived, with a large dining table, a piano and a study that still has the writer's desk.

    Around the house stretch the grounds that Tolstoy loved: orchards, an avenue of birches, ponds, and woods where he walked and rode every day. He ran a school for peasant children here, farmed, and in later life gave up much of his wealth and his title. When he died in 1910, at a railway station far from home, he was brought back and buried in the forest, in a simple grassy mound with no cross, no name and no marker. It is one of the most affecting graves in the world, reached by a path through the trees, and visitors walk to it in silence. During the German occupation of the region in 1941, the house was damaged and the contents had been evacuated, but the estate was later restored.

    The area around Tula has a long history of its own. The city was a centre of arms production from the days of Peter the Great, and also gave Russia its best-known samovars and spiced gingerbread, which are still sold in the old streets. The roads around the estate are a mixture of main highways, which carry a lot of freight on the way between Moscow and the south, and quiet lanes through fields and villages. The surface varies, and you should watch for potholes and for tractors.

    Fuel is easy to find in the towns nearby, including Kosaya Gora and Tula itself, which has all services. The estate has a cafe and a shop, and it is best to arrive early, because tour buses fill the car park by late morning. Set aside at least two hours to walk the grounds. Late spring, with the apple blossom out, and early autumn, with the birches turning, are the best times, and the estate is quiet in winter, when snow lies deep on the paths.

  3. Polenovo finish

    Polenovo is an estate on the high, wooded bank of the Oka River, and it is the Russian countryside of a painter's imagination. Vasily Polenov, a landscape artist of the late nineteenth century, bought the land in the 1890s and designed a house in a fanciful, medieval style, with a tower and wide terraces, where he lived and worked among fellow painters. The estate is now a museum-reserve, with the house, a church he designed and a gallery, and the surrounding landscape, a bend of the Oka with meadows and birch groves, looks very much as it did in his paintings.

    The view from the terrace is the point. The Oka curves far below, wide and slow, with willows along its banks, and the far shore rolls away in soft blue distance, the kind of scene Russian painters tried to capture. In summer the meadows are full of wildflowers, in autumn the birches turn gold, and in winter the river freezes, with snow-covered banks. The nearby town of Tarusa, a gentle riverside settlement, became a refuge for writers and artists, and the poet Marina Tsvetaeva spent her childhood summers here.

    The ride is relaxed. From Serpukhov or Tarusa, narrow country roads wind through villages, fields and pine woods, past wooden houses with carved window frames and wells. The surface varies, with good asphalt on main roads and patchy, sometimes broken pavement on the lanes, and the ferry or bridge crossings over the Oka can mean waits. Weekends in summer bring crowds of visitors to the museum, so early morning or a weekday is best, and rain turns unpaved lanes to mud quickly.

    There are no big services at Polenovo itself, just a cafe near the entrance, so fill up in Tarusa, Serpukhov or Protvino. The best months are late May to September, with September particularly lovely, when the light is soft and the woods are turning. Winters are cold and snowy, and the estate is open year-round, but the roads are icy. Take your time, walk through the park, and watch the sun go down over the river.

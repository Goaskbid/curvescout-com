Why this ride

133 km round trip from Hadrian's Wall and vallum between the fiel, about 2 h 07 of riding. It runs out through Knockcross Roman temporary camp at Grey Ha, Wolsty South tower 13b and Burrow Walls Roman fort and comes back by another road.

The stops

  1. 1. Hadrian's Wall and vallum between the fiel

    Start here.

    Carlisle was a Roman town before it was an English one. Luguvalium grew beside the Eden, and just across the river at Stanwix stood the largest fort on Hadrian's Wall, home to a cavalry regiment of a thousand men. The wall itself ran along the north side of today's city, and though houses and roads cover it, its line can be traced by plaques and fragments, and the wall and its Vallum are part of the UNESCO listing. A stretch of the ditch and the foundations of milecastles are visible beyond the suburbs.

    Read the complete Hadrian's Wall and vallum between the fiel write-up.

  2. 2. Knockcross Roman temporary camp at Grey Ha

    26 km in.

    The Solway Firth is shallow and wide on its Scottish side, and between Annan and Gretna the land is flat and green, with sheep and cattle, field boundaries lined with hawthorn, and long views across the water to the Lake District hills.

    Read the complete Knockcross Roman temporary camp at Grey Ha write-up.

  3. 3. Wolsty South tower 13b

    55 km in.

    Hadrian's Wall gets the fame, but the Roman frontier did not simply stop at Bowness-on-Solway. Beyond the end of the wall, a chain of milefortlets and towers continued down the Cumbrian coast, watching the Solway Firth for raiders from the north. Wolsty South, tower 13b, was one of them, a small stone watchtower near Silloth on the low, windy coast. There is little to see today, often just a mark in the ground, but the site is part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire listed by UNESCO, and its position tells a story: from here, Roman soldiers looked across the water to the hills of Scotland.

    Read the complete Wolsty South tower 13b write-up.

  4. 4. Burrow Walls Roman fort

    79 km in.

    On the northern edge of Workington, near the mouth of the river Derwent, a fragment of stone wall stands alone in a field, a jagged piece of masonry rising above the grass. It is part of Burrow Walls, a Roman fort that guarded the Cumbrian coast, one of the chain of defences that extended from the western end of Hadrian's Wall down the shore to watch for raiders crossing from Scotland and Ireland. Much of the fort's stone was reused in a medieval building on the site, and what survives today is modest, but it is part of the Hadrian's Wall World Heritage site and has a lonely charm, with views over the sea and the river.

    Read the complete Burrow Walls Roman fort write-up.

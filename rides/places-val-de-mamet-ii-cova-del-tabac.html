Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Val de mamet II

    In 1412 nine delegates met in the little Aragonese town of Caspe to decide who should be king of Aragon after the death of Martin the Humane without an heir, and the choice they made, Ferdinand of Antequera of the Castilian royal house, changed the course of Spanish history. The Compromise of Caspe is the thing most people know about the town, which sits on the Ebro seventeen kilometres from here, and it gives this dry, wide, thinly peopled corner of Aragon an unexpected sense of having once been at the centre of things. Val de Mamet is a quiet barranco in the hills to the east, on the border country between Aragon and Catalonia.

  2. 2. Pintures Rupestres d'Alfés

    South of Lleida the irrigated orchards give way abruptly to dry, open steppe, and the little village of Alfés sits on the edge of that change. The Pintures Rupestres d'Alfés are rock paintings in a small sandstone shelter near the village, schematic designs in red pigment, mostly abstract lines, dots and simplified figures, that belong to the later, more symbolic phase of prehistoric art in eastern Spain, and they are included in the UNESCO listing of Mediterranean Basin rock art. They are modest to look at, but there is something powerful about seeing marks left by people thousands of years ago in such an open, wind-swept place.

  3. 3. Cova del Tabac

    Above the village of Camarasa, where the Segre comes out of the pre-Pyrenean hills onto the plain of Lleida, a cave in the limestone holds some of the most interesting rock paintings in Catalonia. The Cova del Tabac, the tobacco cave, is a wide shelter with schematic paintings, simplified human figures, animals and symbols in red, and it is one of the sites included in the UNESCO listing of Mediterranean rock art on the Iberian Peninsula. A short walk leads up from the road to the shelter, which also offers a fine view over the valley, and on a clear day the Pyrenees rise white on the northern horizon.

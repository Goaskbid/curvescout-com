Why this ride

167 km from Mainhardt to Mainhardt, about 3 h of riding. The stops in between: Watchtowers WP 9/101 to 9/131 including th, Fort site and civil settlement of Lorsch, Watchtowers WP 12/39 and Watchtowers WP 12/60 to 12/68.

The stops

  1. 1. Watchtowers WP 9/70 to 9/99a including the

    Start here.

    Mainhardt sits on a ridge in the Swabian-Franconian Forest, and the Roman frontier ran right through it, on its way south in one of the longest straight lines ever drawn across European landscape. Here, the Upper Germanic Limes runs for some eighty kilometres from Walldürn to near Welzheim almost perfectly straight, ignoring hills and valleys, as if surveyors had laid a ruler on the map. The watchtowers numbered in this section stood along that line in the forest, and Mainhardt had a fort, whose site is marked in the village, with a small museum.

    Read the complete Watchtowers WP 9/70 to 9/99a including the write-up.

  2. 2. Watchtowers WP 9/101 to 9/131 including th

    23 km in.

    There is a stretch of the Roman frontier in southern Germany that runs almost perfectly straight for about eighty kilometres, from Walldürn in the Odenwald to near Welzheim in the Swabian-Franconian Forest, ignoring hills and valleys alike, as if the surveyors had simply drawn a line on a map. The watchtowers numbered WP 9/101 to 9/131 stand near the southern end of that line, in the forests around Kaisersbach and Welzheim, where the Limes finally turns.

    Read the complete Watchtowers WP 9/101 to 9/131 including th write-up.

  3. 3. Fort site and civil settlement of Lorsch

    44 km in.

    Lorch, in the Rems valley east of Stuttgart, sits on a historic seam. Just outside town, the Upper German Limes, which ran as a palisade and ditch from the Rhine, met the Raetian Limes, the stone wall running east to the Danube, and the Romans built a fort here to watch over that junction and the valley road. The fort lay beneath what is now the old town centre, and its outline, together with traces of the civilian settlement that grew around it, is marked in places in the streets. It is part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire World Heritage site, and a reconstructed watchtower stands on a hill nearby.

    Read the complete Fort site and civil settlement of Lorsch write-up.

  4. 4. Watchtowers WP 12/39

    61 km in.

    Iggingen is a small village on the plateau above the Rems valley, a few kilometres east of Schwäbisch Gmünd, and like many villages in this part of Württemberg it has a Roman frontier running through its fields. The site labelled Watchtower WP 12/39 sits on the Raetian Limes, the stone wall that marked the edge of Roman Raetia, and from here the line runs eastwards towards Aalen, the largest Roman cavalry fort north of the Alps, now home to an excellent Limes museum.

    Read the complete Watchtowers WP 12/39 write-up.

  5. 5. Watchtowers WP 12/60 to 12/68

    83 km in.

    The Roman frontier wall known as the Limes ran for more than five hundred kilometres across what is now southern Germany, from the Rhine to the Danube, and near Aalen it passed through the Kocher valley and over the hills of the Ostalb. Along its length, watchtowers were built at regular intervals, numbered by archaeologists in sequence: WP 12/60 to 12/68 are a group of tower sites near Hüttlingen, mostly now visible as low mounds or reconstructed foundations. The Upper Germanic-Raetian Limes was inscribed by UNESCO in 2005 as part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire.

    Read the complete Watchtowers WP 12/60 to 12/68 write-up.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Bath

    The Romans came to Bath because of the water. Hot springs rise here at roughly forty-six degrees, and the legions built a sacred pool and a bathing complex around them in the first century, dedicating the place to a local goddess whom they merged with Minerva and calling it Aquae Sulis. The great bath is still there, green and steaming, below a Victorian terrace of statues. Over a thousand years later the Georgians remade the city in pale golden stone, the architect John Wood and his son building the Circus and the sweeping Royal Crescent, so that the place looks like a stage set for a Jane Austen novel. Austen herself lived here for several years and was not entirely happy about it.

  2. 2. Avebury and Associated Monuments

    Avebury is the place where a village grew up inside a stone circle. The henge, a huge circular bank and ditch enclosing the largest stone circle in Britain, was built around four and a half thousand years ago, and the village, with its church, its manor house and its pub, sits partly within it, with a road running right through the middle. You can walk among the stones, touch them, sit beside them, and watch the sheep grazing around them, which is a very different experience from the controlled visits at Stonehenge, its more famous partner on the World Heritage list.

  3. 3. Stonehenge and Associated Monuments

    Stonehenge looks smaller than people expect and stranger than the photographs, a ring of grey stones standing alone on a swell of chalk grassland with the sky pressing down. It sits on Salisbury Plain, a high, open expanse of downland that was a holy landscape long before the stones. The monument went through phases from about 3000 BC, with the great sarsen blocks raised around 2500 BC, hauled, it seems, from the Marlborough Downs some thirty kilometres to the north. The smaller bluestones came from the Preseli Hills in west Wales, about two hundred kilometres away, which remains one of the most astonishing facts in European prehistory.

  4. 4. Stonehenge

    Nobody quite knows why Stonehenge was built, and that is part of its power. Begun around 3000 BC as a circular ditch and bank, it was remodelled over the following thousand years, and the great sarsen stones, some weighing more than twenty tonnes, were raised around 2500 BC. The sarsens came from the Marlborough Downs, about twenty-five kilometres north; the smaller bluestones came from the Preseli Hills in west Wales, more than two hundred kilometres away, and how they were moved remains one of the great puzzles of prehistory. The monument is aligned with the sunrise at the summer solstice and the sunset at the winter solstice, and thousands gather here at those times. With Avebury and the surrounding landscape, it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Kootenay Rockies Loop: Banff to Golden

Why this ride

A 250 km one-way ride from near Banff, about 4 h 04 of riding. It goes by way of Kootenay National Park and Radium Hot Springs. The highest point is about 2,150 m. It suits an unhurried touring pace.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Banff

    Banff in the morning has a particular feel: cold air coming off the glacier-fed Bow River, mist clinging to Mount Rundle, and the smell of pine and woodsmoke from kitchens. The town sits in the heart of the Canadian Rockies at about 1,400 metres, hemmed in by peaks, and it exists because of hot springs. In the 1880s railway workers found warm water at Cave and Basin, and the government made the area a reserve that grew into Canada's first national park.

  2. 2. Kootenay National Park

    There is a place on this road where the colour of the ground changes from grey to rust. Kootenay National Park, on the western side of the Continental Divide, is the quieter neighbour of Banff, and Highway 93 South threads through it for about ninety kilometres from the Vermilion Pass to the hot springs at Radium. The stretch around Marble Canyon is the heart of it, with a deep, narrow limestone gorge and, close by, the Paint Pots, where iron-rich springs stain the earth ochre, and where the First Nations once gathered pigment to trade.

  3. 3. Radium Hot Springs

    You smell Radium Hot Springs before you see it, or so it seems: faint sulphur, wood smoke, hot tarmac. The village sits where the Kootenay valley opens to the broad Columbia, and the road from the north drops through Sinclair Canyon, a dramatic notch in red rock, so narrow that the highway squeezes through with barely a shoulder. It is the best entrance to any town on the loop.

  4. 4. Golden

    Golden sits in the Columbia Valley at the meeting of the Kicking Horse and Columbia rivers, in the shadow of two great ranges, the Rockies on the east and the Purcells and Selkirks on the south and west. The town is small, about four thousand people, but it is the largest settlement for a hundred kilometres in any direction, and it acts as a hub for riders on the Trans-Canada Highway. It has been a railway town since the 1880s, when the Canadian Pacific Railway came through the Kicking Horse Pass, and it still has the feel of a place where trains are part of the landscape, with long freights rumbling through the valley day and night.

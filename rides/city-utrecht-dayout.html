Why this ride

A 431 km round trip from Utrecht - roughly 7 h 26 in the saddle. The stops in between: Fort along the Pampus, Tiel Inundation Canal, Works along the IJ before Durgerdam and Seventeenth-Century Canal Ring Area of Ams. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Utrecht

    Utrecht is the middle of the Netherlands in a very literal way, and it feels it: trains from everywhere come through the station, and everything else is arranged around a canal that sits lower than the street. The Oudegracht has wharves and cellars cut into its banks, once warehouses for goods unloaded straight from boats, now cafes with tables at water level. Above it all stands the Dom Tower, a little over a hundred metres of Gothic stone, which you will see from the ring road long before you arrive.

  2. 2. Fort along the Pampus

    Out in the open water of the IJmeer, a few kilometres off the old fortified town of Muiden, a low circular fortress sits on an artificial island. This is Pampus, one of more than forty forts that make up the Defence Line of Amsterdam, a ring of fortifications built around the city in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The idea was simple and very Dutch: in case of attack, large areas of land around the city would be flooded to a depth that was too shallow for boats and too deep for soldiers to wade, and the forts would guard the roads, railways and dykes that crossed the flooded zone. Pampus guarded the approach to Amsterdam by water. The line was listed by UNESCO in 1996.

  3. 3. Tiel Inundation Canal

    Tiel is a river town on the Waal, the main branch of the Rhine in the Netherlands, in the middle of the Betuwe, a region of orchards, dykes and meadows between the Waal and the Lower Rhine. Just outside the town, a wide, straight channel runs through the fields, crossed by bridges and lined by dykes. This is the Tiel inundation canal, built in the late nineteenth century as part of the New Dutch Waterline, the defensive system of controlled flooding that protected the heart of Holland, and it is now part of the UNESCO-listed Dutch Water Defence Lines.

  4. 4. Works along the IJ before Durgerdam

    East of Amsterdam, where the IJ widens into the IJmeer and the city gives way to water and sky, the old dike village of Durgerdam sits on a strip of land between the sea wall and the polders. Its wooden houses, painted in strong colours and leaning on each other along the dike, look out over a lake that was once the Zuiderzee, open to the North Sea until the Afsluitdijk closed it in 1932. The works along the IJ here belong to the Defence Line of Amsterdam, now part of the Dutch Water Defence Lines World Heritage Site.

  5. 5. Seventeenth-Century Canal Ring Area of Ams

    Nobody rides a motorbike into the Amsterdam canal ring for the curves. You come because in the early seventeenth century, the city at the height of its Golden Age planned an extension of startling ambition: three main canals, the Herengracht, Keizersgracht and Prinsengracht, laid out in concentric arcs around the medieval core, lined with tall, narrow merchants' houses with gabled roofs and hoisting beams. Work began in 1613, and the result, a UNESCO site since 2010, is still one of the most admired examples of urban planning in the world.

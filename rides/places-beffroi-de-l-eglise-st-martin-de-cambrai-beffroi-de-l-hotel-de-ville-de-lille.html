Why this ride

A 276 km round trip from Cambrai - roughly 4 h 46 in the saddle. The stops in between: Cités de la Solitude, Chevalement de la fosse Dutemple, Ensemble minier de la Belleforière and Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Lille. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Beffroi de l’église St-Martin de Cambrai

    Cambrai's belfry is the tower of the church of Saint-Martin, a stout, plain-faced tower that rises over the roofs of a town of about thirty-six thousand people in the flat, open Cambrésis. Belfries in northern France and Belgium were symbols of civic freedom, built by town councils to hold the bells and the charters, and a group of them is on the World Heritage list. This one has a more modest charm than the giants of Bruges or Ghent, but it still announces the town from a long way off, across fields of sugar beet and wheat.

  2. 2. Cités de la Solitude

    On the edge of Waziers, near Douai, the cités de la Solitude are rows of brick houses built by a mining company for its workers, laid out in neat streets with small gardens. Such company towns, the cités minières, were the backbone of life in the coal basin of northern France, providing housing, schools, churches and shops for miners and their families, and they are among the most important elements of the Nord-Pas-de-Calais Mining Basin, listed by UNESCO in 2012. The name Solitude seems almost ironic for a place built to house so many people close together.

  3. 3. Chevalement de la fosse Dutemple

    A steel tower with a big wheel at the top: once you have seen one headframe, you start to spot them all across the north of France, but each one marks a place where men went down into the dark. The headframe of the Fosse Dutemple, between Valenciennes and Petite-Forêt, is one of the survivors, kept as part of the UNESCO-listed Nord-Pas de Calais mining basin. Under it, cages once dropped miners hundreds of metres to the coal seams; now it stands as a monument, its pulleys still against the sky.

  4. 4. Ensemble minier de la Belleforière

    Roost-Warendin, Waziers and Râches sit just north of Douai, where the old Scarpe floodplain met the coal seams, and the Belleforière mining ensemble is one of the clusters that shows how a pit site was organised. Shafts, buildings, workers' housing and spoil heaps were laid out together, with the railway linking them all, and in a few places the ensemble survives well enough that you can read it on the ground. Part of the regional mining landscape listed by UNESCO in 2012, it is a good example of how coal companies planned whole villages around their extraction.

  5. 5. Beffroi de l’Hôtel de Ville de Lille

    Lille's town hall belfry is a child of the twentieth century. After the old town hall was lost, the architect Émile Dubuisson built a new one in the 1920s and early 1930s in a working-class district south of the centre, and its belfry, rising about 104 metres, is the tallest civic belfry in Europe. Built of reinforced concrete and brick in an Art Deco reinterpretation of the Flemish tradition, it is part of the UNESCO-listed Belfries of Belgium and France, a group that celebrates the towers built by towns to symbolise their freedom and civic pride.

Why this ride

A 147 km round trip from Antwerpen - roughly 2 h 32 in the saddle. The stops in between: Béguinage de Lier, Flemish Béguinages, Stadhuis en Belforttoren and Grand Béguinage de Mechelen.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Plantin-Moretus House-Workshops-Museum Com

    In 1555 a French bookbinder called Christophe Plantin set up a printing press in Antwerp, then one of the richest cities in Europe, and his business became the largest printing house of its time, printing Bibles, atlases, scientific works and books in many languages. His son in law, Jan Moretus, and the Moretus family carried on the business for three centuries, and their home and workshops on the Vrijdagmarkt, preserved almost intact, are now a museum. It holds the two oldest surviving printing presses in the world, a huge collection of type, woodblocks and copper plates, a library and portraits painted by Rubens, a friend of the family. In 2005 it became the first museum listed as a World Heritage Site.

  2. 2. Béguinage de Lier

    Through a gateway off a busy street in Lier, you step into another world. The béguinage is a small walled neighbourhood of narrow cobbled lanes, whitewashed brick houses and a baroque church, where for centuries communities of beguines lived. These were religious women who chose a life of prayer, work and service without taking the permanent vows of nuns, living independently in their own houses but sharing a communal life. The béguinages of Flanders, about a dozen of them, were listed by UNESCO in 1998, and the one in Lier, with its quiet lanes and doorways topped with statues of saints, is one of the most charming.

  3. 3. Flemish Béguinages

    In the thirteenth century, a remarkable movement spread across the Low Countries. Women, often unmarried or widowed, chose to live together in communities devoted to prayer and work, without taking the permanent vows of nuns, keeping their property and the freedom to leave. They were called béguines, and their walled settlements, the béguinages, became towns within towns. The Groot Begijnhof of Mechelen, the great béguinage, is one of thirteen in Flanders listed by UNESCO, and at its height it housed well over a thousand women, making it one of the largest communities of its kind.

  4. 4. Stadhuis en Belforttoren

    Lier's Grote Markt is one of those squares that makes you slow down without being asked. In the middle stands an elegant eighteenth-century town hall in pale stone, and grafted onto its side is something much older: a slim Gothic belfry from the fourteenth century, its four corner turrets giving it the look of a castle chess piece. Together they belong to the group of Belgian and French belfries listed by UNESCO, symbols of the moment when medieval towns won their freedoms and built towers to prove it.

  5. 5. Grand Béguinage de Mechelen

    Mechelen's Grand Beguinage is a small town inside a town, and the first thing that hits you on walking through its gate is the silence. Behind the walls are cobbled lanes lined with small gabled houses, a quiet church, a green square and a canal arm of the Dijle river. For centuries this was the home of the beguines, women who lived a religious life without taking formal vows, supporting themselves by weaving, lace-making, nursing and teaching. The movement began in the Low Countries in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and at its height there were hundreds of beguinages. Mechelen's was one of the largest, and most of what stands today dates from the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. It was listed by UNESCO in 1998 as part of the group of Flemish beguinages.

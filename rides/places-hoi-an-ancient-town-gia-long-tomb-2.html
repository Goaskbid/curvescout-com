Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hoi An Ancient Town

    Hội An is the one old port in Vietnam that was neither flattened by war nor remodelled in concrete, and that is why people tolerate the crowds. The town lies on the Thu Bồn river a short way from the sea, about twenty-five kilometres south of Da Nang. From the fifteenth to the nineteenth centuries it was a trading hub where Chinese, Japanese and later European merchants waited for the monsoon winds to turn. They left their marks in the architecture: yellow-walled Chinese assembly halls, narrow merchant houses with wooden beams, and the Japanese Covered Bridge, a small humped span that has become the town's emblem. The silt in the river eventually killed the harbour, and Da Nang took over the shipping. The decline saved the old streets, and UNESCO listed the town in 1999.

  2. 2. My Son Sanctuary

    My Son is a valley ringed by green hills in Quang Nam province, and the first thing that strikes you is the quiet. After the traffic of Da Nang and the tourist streets of Hoi An, a muddy path leads into a hollow where red-brick towers lean out of the trees. The temples were built by the Cham, a seafaring Hindu kingdom that ruled much of central Vietnam for over a thousand years, and who used this valley as a place for royal ceremonies and worship of Shiva.

  3. 3. Gia Long Tomb

    Emperor Gia Long, who founded the Nguyen dynasty and reunified Vietnam in 1802, chose a spot for his tomb about eleven kilometres from Hué, and he chose it far from the city on purpose. The Thien Tho hills rise south-west of the capital, and his burial ground lies tucked among them, a long, quiet complex of terraces, stone courtyards and guarding statues of mandarins and elephants. He died in 1820. His queen is buried beside him, which was unusual enough that guides still mention it.

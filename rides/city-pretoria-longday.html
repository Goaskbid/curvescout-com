Why this ride

A 297 km round trip from Pretoria - roughly 5 h 07 in the saddle. On the way it passes 16 June 1976 - The Streets of Orlando West, Walter Sisulu Square, Sharpeville Graves site A and Sharpeville Graves site B. The highest point is about 1,650 m. A steady touring day rather than a sprint.

Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Pretoria

    Pretoria is South Africa's administrative capital, and it gets less attention than Johannesburg and Cape Town, which is part of its appeal. In October the city is painted purple by thousands of jacaranda trees, introduced from South America in the late nineteenth century, and the fallen blossom carpets the pavements. It sits in a warm valley under the Magaliesberg ridge, a little over fifty kilometres from Johannesburg, and its centre mixes Afrikaner history, colonial buildings and a lively black African present.

  2. 2. 16 June 1976 - The Streets of Orlando West

    Orlando West looks ordinary at first: tidy brick houses, kids in school uniform, taxis hooting at every corner, smoke from braai stands curling over the street. It is in Soweto, about fifteen kilometres southwest of central Johannesburg, and on the morning of 16 June 1976 these were the streets where thousands of schoolchildren marched against being taught in Afrikaans. Police opened fire. Among the first killed was twelve-year-old Hector Pieterson, and the photograph of him being carried by an older student went around the world.

  3. 3. Walter Sisulu Square

    On 26 June 1955, some three thousand delegates gathered on a dusty open field in Kliptown, one of the oldest settlements of what became Soweto, for the Congress of the People. Police surrounded the meeting on its second day, but not before the crowd had adopted the Freedom Charter, the document that opens with the words that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white. Its ideas went on to shape the country's post-apartheid constitution. The field is now Walter Sisulu Square, named after the anti-apartheid leader and mentor of Nelson Mandela, and the charter's clauses are carved into stone around a monument at its centre.

  4. 4. Sharpeville Graves site A

    On 21 March 1960, a crowd of several thousand people gathered at the police station in Sharpeville, a township south of Johannesburg, to protest against the pass laws, which forced Black South Africans to carry documents and restricted their movement. The police opened fire. Sixty-nine people were killed, many of them shot in the back as they ran, and many more were wounded. The massacre became a turning point: it brought the apartheid regime worldwide condemnation, led to the banning of the African National Congress and the Pan Africanist Congress, and pushed the liberation movement towards armed struggle. Today, 21 March is a public holiday, Human Rights Day.

  5. 5. Sharpeville Graves site B

    On the morning of 21 March 1960, several thousand people gathered at the police station in Sharpeville, a Black township beside Vereeniging in what is now Gauteng, to protest against the pass laws that controlled where they could live and work. By early afternoon the police had opened fire, and sixty-nine people lay dead, many shot in the back as they ran. The massacre made headlines around the world, led to the banning of the African National Congress and the Pan Africanist Congress, and pushed the liberation movements toward armed struggle. South Africa now marks the date as Human Rights Day, and the graves of the victims in the nearby Phelindaba cemetery are part of the memorial landscape, together with the police station and a small museum.

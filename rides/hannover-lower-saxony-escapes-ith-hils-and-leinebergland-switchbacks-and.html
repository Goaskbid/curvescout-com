Waypoint introductions and full write-ups

  1. 1. Hannover south gate

    South of Hannover the flat plain starts to fold, and the small town of Pattensen, on the Leine, marks the place where the ride leaves the city for the hills. The land here is the Calenberger Land, a fertile farming country of wheat and sugar beet, with big brick farmhouses and low ridges on the horizon. Beyond it rise the limestone ridges of the Leinebergland, the Ith and the Hils, long, forested crests with cliffs and old quarries, and they are the reason the ride exists.

  2. 2. Coppenbrügge Ith road

    The Ith is the sort of ridge that makes a rider look twice at the map. It is a long, narrow limestone wall about twenty kilometres from end to end, rising steeply from the farmland to a crest of nearly four hundred and fifty metres, and it runs northwest to southeast like a great fold in the earth. The slopes are covered with old beech forest, and along the crest are cliffs and rock outcrops that attract climbers, with caves and potholes in the limestone below. The villages at its foot, Coppenbrügge among them, look up to it with a faint air of respect.

  3. 3. Eschershausen Hils road

    Eschershausen is a small town at the foot of the Hils, and its main claim to fame is a writer. Wilhelm Raabe, one of the great German novelists of the nineteenth century, was born here in 1831, and his books about small-town life, with their humour and melancholy, are still read, particularly the Chronicle of Sparrow Alley and The Hunger Pastor. A modest museum in the town honours him, and the place has a quiet pride in its son. You do not need to have read a word of his work to enjoy the town, but the connection adds some weight to the plain streets.

  4. 4. Alfeld food stop

    Alfeld has a secret that surprises many visitors: a factory building that changed the history of architecture. On the edge of the town stands the Fagus Works, a shoe-last factory designed by a young Walter Gropius in 1911, with great sheets of glass wrapped around the corners of the brick structure, so that the building seems to float. It was one of the first buildings to use a curtain wall of glass, and it is a forerunner of the Bauhaus style that Gropius would found in the 1919. The works still produce shoe lasts and are a World Heritage site, with a visitor centre and guided tours that explain how the place worked.

  5. 5. Elze fuel gate

    Elze is a modest town on the Leine, between Hannover and Hildesheim, and it exists in a rider's day for one good reason, which is that it has petrol stations. That is not meant as an insult. A small, level-headed Lower Saxon town of nearly ten thousand people, set in the broad river valley with farmland on one side and wooded hills on the other, Elze does what a fuel gate should do, quickly and without drama. Pumps sit along the main roads, a supermarket and a few bakeries are in the centre, and you can be on your way again in ten minutes.

  6. 6. Marienburg castle view

    The castle of Marienburg looks as if a Hollywood studio had been told to build the perfect German fairy-tale palace, and in a sense it was an act of devotion: King George V of Hanover, who was blind, had it built in the 1850s and 1860s as a birthday present for his wife, Queen Marie, on a forested hill above the Leine valley. The architect, Conrad Wilhelm Hase, packed it with towers, turrets, pointed windows and steep roofs in the neo-Gothic style, and the result is a skyline of such romantic excess that visitors often assume it is medieval.

  8. 8. Hannover north edge

    The northern fringe of Hannover is where the city gives up and the Wedemark begins, and the landmarks that matter are small. Isernhagen and Langenhagen sit side by side, the first a strung-out village-suburb with a great many gardens and horse paddocks, the second a proper town that happens to host the city's airport. The sandy soil is why pine and birch woods start creeping in as you head north.

  9. 9. Celle fuel gate

    Celle is a town that looks like it has been preserved in a jar. More than four hundred half-timbered houses, with carved beams, painted inscriptions and crooked upper storeys, line the streets of the old centre, and the whole place has survived with very little damage because it escaped bombing in the Second World War. About seventy-one thousand people live here, on the banks of the Aller, at the southern edge of the Luneburg Heath.

  10. 10. Uelzen Hundertwasser station sight

    Uelzen's railway station is the most unexpected thing in Lower Saxony. Friedensreich Hundertwasser, the Austrian artist who hated straight lines, redesigned it for the Expo 2000 world's fair, and the result is a riot of golden onion domes, wobbly floors, ceramic columns in every colour and trees growing from the roof. Walking through the building is free, and you do not need a train ticket.

  11. 11. Lüneburg food stop

    Lüneburg got rich on salt, and you can still see that in the brick. For centuries brine from under the town was boiled into the white salt that was shipped to Lübeck and used to preserve the herring of the Baltic, and the money from this trade paid for the Gothic town hall, the gabled merchant houses and the old crane by the Ilmenau. The Hanseatic League was good to Lüneburg. The streets are lined with stepped brick gables, many of them leaning a little because of subsidence from the salt mines below, which gives the whole old town a gently drunken look.

  12. 12. Harburger Berge forest

    South of Hamburg, on the far bank of the Elbe, a ridge of low, wooded hills rises to a little over a hundred metres, and Hamburgers treat it as their mountains. The Harburger Berge are what is left of a glacial moraine, covered in beech, oak and pine, with patches of heath and sandy soil. The highest point, the Hasselbrack, reaches 116 metres, which makes it the highest natural point in the whole city state of Hamburg, a fact that Hamburgers recite with a mixture of pride and self-mockery.

  13. 13. Undeloh heath road

    Ask a German about Undeloh and he will tell you about the heather. The village lies in the middle of the Lüneburg Heath, one of the largest areas of heathland left in central Europe, and it is the quiet gate to the nature reserve that surrounds the hill of Wilsede. Thatched farmhouses sit under old oaks, there is a small church with a wooden tower, and in the main street horse-drawn carriages wait for tourists, which gives the place the look of a postcard from about 1925. Part of the track beyond the village is closed to motor traffic, so the last kilometres to Wilsede are for walkers, cyclists and the carriages. The bike stays where you leave it.

  14. 14. Back to Hannover

    North of Hannover, near Langenhagen and Isernhagen, the city turns into a district of villas, horse paddocks and big gardens, and the sky fills with the sound of aircraft. Hannover Airport lies in Langenhagen, and the approach and departure paths pass low over the roads. The suburban landscape is flat, tidy and a little anonymous, but after a long day among hills and forests, it is exactly what is needed for a gentle landing.

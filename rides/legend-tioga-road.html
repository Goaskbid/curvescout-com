  1. 1. Near Yosemite Valley

    Half Dome is the mountain everyone recognises, even if they have never been to California: a great granite dome, sliced clean through on one side, rising almost 5,000 feet above the floor of Yosemite Valley. The only way up is on foot, a long, hard hike from the valley, finishing with a climb up a pair of steel cables bolted into the rock, and a permit system limits the number of people who can make the attempt each day.

  2. 2. Near Yosemite Valley

    The label points at Yosemite Valley, but up here on the Tioga Road you are far above it, in the high country of the park where the granite lies bare and polished and the air at two and a half thousand metres smells of pine resin and warm rock. This stretch, between Olmsted Point and Tenaya Lake, is where the road runs across the glacier-scoured heart of the Sierra Nevada, and for many riders it is the finest part of the whole crossing.

  4. 4. Tioga Road point 4

    Tuolumne Meadows is the largest subalpine meadow in the Sierra Nevada, a long, flat sweep of grass at 8,600 feet with the Tuolumne River winding through it and a ring of granite domes and peaks around the edge. Lembert Dome rises on one side like a bald head, and the spires of Cathedral Peak stand on the south, with snow lingering in their gullies into July. The meadow is lovely in the evening, when the alpenglow touches the peaks and the river is silver.

  5. 5. Tioga Road point 5

    Tioga Pass, at 9,943 feet, is the highest highway pass in California and the eastern gate of Yosemite. The entrance station sits just below the top, in a high alpine landscape of granite, scattered pines and small lakes, with the snow-streaked ridges of the Sierra crest rising on every side. Beyond the pass, the road plunges down the eastern escarpment in a series of long, steep descents toward Mono Lake, losing nearly 3,200 feet in 12 miles.

  6. 6. Tioga Road point 6

    The road from Tioga Pass drops into Lee Vining in a headlong rush, and the first thing you see is Mono Lake spread out below like a pool of molten silver. It sits in a basin at 6,400 feet, with no outlet, so the water that flows in from the Sierra streams leaves only by evaporation, and the lake has become strange. It is two and a half times as salty as the ocean and highly alkaline, with a texture that feels slippery on the skin, and it supports no fish, but trillions of brine shrimp and alkali flies.

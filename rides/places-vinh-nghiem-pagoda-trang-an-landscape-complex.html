  1. 1. Vinh Nghiem Pagoda

    In the countryside of Bắc Giang province, in northern Vietnam, about fifty kilometres north-east of Hanoi, the Vinh Nghiem Pagoda stands by the Thương river, surrounded by rice fields and villages. It is one of the oldest and most important Buddhist centres in Vietnam, associated with the Trúc Lâm school of Zen Buddhism, founded in the thirteenth century by King Trần Nhân Tông, who abdicated to become a monk.

  2. 2. Central Sector of the Imperial Citadel of

    In 1010 the Vietnamese king Lý Thái Tổ moved his capital to the banks of the Red River and named it Thăng Long, Ascending Dragon, after he is said to have seen a dragon rising from the water. The city grew up around the royal citadel, and for most of the next eight centuries it was the political heart of Vietnam. The Central Sector of the Imperial Citadel of Thăng Long, in the middle of modern Hanoi, preserves layers of this history, from the foundations of palaces excavated beneath the ground to buildings of the Nguyễn dynasty and the French colonial era. It was listed by UNESCO in 2010, the year Hanoi celebrated its thousandth anniversary.

  3. 3. Trang An Landscape Complex

    The first view of Trang An catches people out. You step into a rowing boat, an old woman or man takes the oars, often with feet as well as hands, and the boat glides into a cave in a limestone cliff, comes out the other end into a hidden lagoon ringed by green peaks, and slides into another cave, and another, for a couple of hours. The Trang An Landscape Complex, in Ninh Bình province, is a maze of karst towers, flooded valleys, caves and rice fields, and it was listed by UNESCO in 2014 for both natural and cultural value.

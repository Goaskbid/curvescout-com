Why this ride

Leave the city behind and spend the day on roads with texture. 474 km round from Walnut Grove - plan on about 8 h 37 of riding. The stops in between: near Squamish, near Nanaimo and Richmond. The climbs: Vancouver / British Columbia and Birch Bay. It tops out at about 1,400 m. It crosses from Canada into United States - carry ID and check vignette or toll rules.

Roadbook

  1. Fuel stop in Walnut Grove fuel

    Walnut Grove is a suburban neighbourhood in the north-west of the Township of Langley, close to where Highway 1 crosses the Fraser Valley. It is a tidy, residential place with shopping centres, parks and easy access to the freeway, making it a convenient start and fuel stop for rides heading out of the Vancouver area.

    The Golden Ears Bridge, opened in 2009, connects Langley with Maple Ridge and Pitt Meadows across the Fraser River just to the north. Nearby, the historic village of Fort Langley offers a charming detour, with the reconstructed Hudson's Bay Company fort and a lively main street.

    The riding out of Walnut Grove depends on your direction. Highway 1 heads east up the Fraser Valley towards Abbotsford and Hope, gateway to the mountain roads of the interior. To the west lies the city and the ferry terminals for the Sunshine Coast and Vancouver Island. Local roads are mostly flat and well maintained, but traffic can be heavy, especially at rush hour.

    Fuel, coffee and food are abundant in Walnut Grove, with several service stations near the highway interchanges. It is a good place to meet a group, top up and grab a quick bite before setting off. The ferry ride to the Sunshine Coast, a short drive away, is a wonderful way to start a coastal adventure.

  2. Vancouver / British Columbia return pass

    Midway between Squamish and Whistler, the Sea to Sky Highway climbs out of the fjord country into the heart of the Coast Mountains, and this stop lies near the Daisy Lake area, about twenty kilometres south of Whistler. The lake, a long, narrow reservoir the colour of milky jade, lies below the road, with the Black Tusk, a jagged volcanic spire of about 2,300 metres, rising above it in the Garibaldi range. Nearby Brandywine Falls drops about seventy metres in a single plume, and a short walk from the highway leads to a viewing platform.

    The highway was widened and improved for the 2010 Winter Olympics, and now it is a fast, well-surfaced road with passing lanes and long, flowing bends. It is a magnificent ride, with the sea on one side and mountains on the other, and there is plenty to see, from the Stawamus Chief, a huge granite dome at Squamish, to the glaciers on the high peaks. Traffic is heavy on weekends, so start early and avoid Friday evening and Sunday afternoon.

    Whistler, with nearly nine thousand permanent residents, is a resort village with fuel, supermarkets, cafes, restaurants and hotels, and Pemberton, further north, is a quieter farming community known for its potatoes. The label mentions Vancouver, but the city is about a hundred kilometres to the south. Fuel can be found in Squamish and Whistler, and it is wise to fill the tank before heading north.

    The road can be affected by weather. In winter, snow and ice make driving hazardous, and chain requirements apply to many sections. In summer, afternoon thunderstorms and wildfire smoke are possible. Autumn brings fog and cool nights, and spring has snowmelt and waterfalls. Whatever the season, the mountains are impressive, and the air has a clean, resinous smell of fir and glacier water.

  4. Photo stop near Nanaimo photo

    Ask a Canadian about Nanaimo and the first thing you will hear about is the Nanaimo bar, a no-bake square of crumb base, custard buttercream and chocolate that turns up in cafes across the country and is unapologetically sweet. The city itself, with about eighty-five thousand people, is the second largest on Vancouver Island and the main ferry hub to the mainland, with boats to Horseshoe Bay and Tsawwassen near Vancouver. For many riders it is where the island begins or ends.

    Nanaimo began as a coal town. In the 1850s, the Hudson's Bay Company opened mines here, and for a century coal was the city's livelihood, with shafts reaching under the harbour. The Bastion, a white wooden blockhouse built by the company in 1853 to protect its workers, still stands near the waterfront and is one of the oldest buildings in British Columbia. The harbour today is lively, with seaplanes, ferries and pleasure boats, and a long promenade for walking. In summer, the city hosts a bathtub race across the Strait of Georgia, a gloriously silly event that has been going since the 1960s.

    The main road north, Highway 19, is a fast, divided highway that runs up the island to Campbell River, efficient but dull. For a better ride, take the Old Island Highway, Highway 19A, which follows the coast through towns like Parksville and Qualicum Beach, with views over the strait to the mountains of the mainland. It is slower, with more traffic and lower speed limits, but far more interesting. West of Nanaimo, roads lead into the hills and to lakes in the forest, and Highway 4 heads across the island from Parksville to Port Alberni and the wild west coast at Tofino.

    Nanaimo has every service: fuel, motorcycle shops, restaurants and hotels. The Old City Quarter and the waterfront have good cafes, and Newcastle Island, a short foot ferry ride from the harbour, is a provincial park with trails and beaches. The climate is mild, with dry summers and wet winters, and the ferries can be busy on summer weekends, so book ahead if you are heading to the mainland.

  5. Near Richmond finish

    The point lies on the western edge of Richmond, out where the city gives way to the mudflats and marshes of the Fraser River delta. Richmond is built almost entirely on flat delta land, protected by dykes from the river and the sea, and the western shore, along Sturgeon Bank, is a wide expanse of mud, eelgrass and tidal channels, important for migrating birds. On a clear day, the view across the Strait of Georgia to the mountains of Vancouver Island is wide and calming, and the sunsets are often spectacular.

    At the south-western corner of Richmond lies Steveston, an old fishing village at the mouth of the Fraser, where boats still sell fresh fish and prawns directly from the dock. The Gulf of Georgia Cannery, once one of the largest salmon canneries in British Columbia, is now a national historic site and museum, telling the story of the fishing industry and the many Japanese Canadian, Chinese and Indigenous workers who made it run.

    Richmond also has one of the most vibrant Asian food scenes in North America. The city has a large Chinese Canadian population, and its malls and restaurants serve some of the best dim sum, noodles and seafood outside Asia. In summer, the night markets are a lively, crowded experience with food stalls of every kind.

    As a starting point for the Sunshine Coast ferry ride, Richmond is practical but requires a trip through Vancouver. The route north crosses the city, the Lions Gate Bridge or the Second Narrows, and then heads west along the North Shore to Horseshoe Bay, where ferries leave for Langdale on the Sunshine Coast. Traffic in the Vancouver area can be heavy, so allow plenty of time and leave early. Richmond has fuel and services everywhere. The weather on the coast is mild but often wet, especially from October to April, so good rain gear is essential.

  6. Point Roberts scenic

    When the boundary between the United States and British North America was fixed along the 49th parallel in 1846, nobody seems to have noticed, or cared, that the line would cut off the tip of the Tsawwassen peninsula south of Vancouver. The result is Point Roberts, a small piece of Washington State, about thirteen square kilometres, that can only be reached by land by passing through Canada. To get there from the rest of the United States, you cross into Canada, ride about forty kilometres through British Columbia, and cross back into the US. Bring your passport, and expect four border crossings for a round trip.

    The community is quiet and slightly surreal: a few streets of houses, a marina, a golf course, some shops and a lot of forest, with water on three sides. Many of the homes belong to Canadians who use them as holiday cottages, and the local economy depends heavily on visitors from the north. When the border was closed during the pandemic, Point Roberts was cut off from the outside world for well over a year, and the community struggled.

    The views from the shore are wide and beautiful. From Lighthouse Marine Park, at the south-western tip, you look out over the Strait of Georgia to the Gulf Islands and Vancouver Island, and in summer, orcas are sometimes seen passing close to shore. The sunsets over the water are spectacular. Monument Park, at the north-western corner, marks the start of the land border, with a stone obelisk on the bluff.

    Next door in Canada, Tsawwassen is the terminal for the BC Ferries to Victoria and the Gulf Islands, and you can watch the ferries come and go from the shore. The roads in Point Roberts are short and quiet, with little traffic, and they make a pleasant loop. There are a few fuel stations, a grocery store and some cafés. Border queues can be long on summer weekends.

  7. Birch Bay pass

    Birch Bay is a wide, shallow, crescent-shaped bay on the coast of Whatcom County, just south of the Canadian border, and at low tide the water retreats a long way, exposing vast flats of sand where children dig for clams and look for sand dollars. In summer, the incoming tide flows over the sun-warmed sand and the water becomes noticeably warmer than elsewhere in the area, making it one of the more pleasant places to swim in the chilly waters of the Salish Sea.

    The town of Birch Bay is a laid-back resort community, with beach houses, holiday rentals, a few restaurants and ice cream shops along the shore road, and a state park with campsites, forest and beach at the southern end. On clear days, the view across the water stretches to the islands and the mountains of Vancouver Island, and Mount Baker rises to the east, a great white cone above the farmland.

    A few kilometres north, in Blaine, the Peace Arch stands on the border between the United States and Canada, a white monument built in 1921 to celebrate the long peace between the two countries, in a park that straddles the border. The nearby Semiahmoo Spit is a long, narrow sandbar with a resort, a marina and views across Drayton Harbor.

    For riders on the Sunshine Coast ferry ride, Birch Bay is a pleasant pause on the coastal route between the border and Bellingham. The roads here are quiet and flat, with views of the water, though the shore road can be busy in summer. Interstate 5 runs a few kilometres inland, carrying heavy traffic to and from Vancouver. Blaine and Birch Bay have fuel and food. If you are crossing the border, check the wait times, which can be long, especially on weekends and holidays, and have your documents ready.

  8. Back to Sunshine finish

    Walnut Grove is not the sort of name that appears on a postcard, and that is part of its charm as the last stop of the day. The point lies in the Township of Langley, in the lowlands of the Fraser Valley east of Vancouver, where the land flattens into a broad plain of farms, greenhouses and new subdivisions. A few kilometres to the east, the little town of Fort Langley preserves the old Hudson's Bay Company post, founded in 1827, where in 1858 the colony of British Columbia was proclaimed after the gold rush brought thousands of miners up the river.

    The Fraser itself is the great fact of the landscape. It comes out of the mountains with a load of silt, runs wide and brown between its banks, and has laid down the flat, fertile delta that makes this one of the richest farming areas in western Canada. In the fall, salmon run up the river in huge numbers, and eagles gather on the sandbars and cottonwoods to feed on the carcasses. To the north, the Golden Ears and the North Shore mountains stand in a line, and on a clear morning their snowy tops are the first thing you see.

    The riders who end their day here have usually come back from the ferry at Horseshoe Bay, and the Sunshine Coast, with its green ridges and long inlets, is already behind. The road home is mostly the Trans-Canada Highway, wide, busy and fast, with commuter traffic that moves in big surges and plenty of trucks, and the more interesting route is along the river by Fort Langley, along back roads with farm stands, hedgerows and the odd tractor. Rain is the main hazard: the Fraser Valley collects weather from the Pacific, and winter days can be wet and grey for weeks.

    Fuel and food are no trouble. Walnut Grove has stations, supermarkets and a number of cafes and chain restaurants, and Fort Langley has bakeries and small places for a proper meal. In summer, look for blueberry and strawberry stands along the roadside, selling fruit picked that morning. May and June, or September, are the most pleasant months to ride, with long daylight and warm, dry roads, and Fort Langley is worth an hour of your time before you go home.

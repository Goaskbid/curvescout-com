Roadbook

  1. Puerto Montt start

    Puerto Montt is where the Carretera Austral starts, though few people would call it a pretty town. It is a busy port and salmon-farming centre on the Reloncaví Sound, with a grey waterfront, fish stalls and a rain record to match, and it was founded in 1853 by German colonists brought in under the direction of Vicente Pérez Rosales. Across the water, if the clouds allow, the cone of Osorno volcano shows up white against the sky, and the lakeside towns to the north, Puerto Varas and Frutillar, still show their Bavarian-style wooden houses.

    The harbourside quarter of Angelmó is the place to go before leaving. Stalls and tiny restaurants sell smoked mussels, fresh shellfish and woollen goods, and you can try curanto, a southern Chilean dish of shellfish, meat and potatoes traditionally steamed in a pit. From the jetties, ferries leave for the islands and the long fjords of the south, a reminder that for much of Chilean Patagonia the sea was the only road until the highway was built.

    Heading out, the Carretera Austral, Ruta 7, begins as an ordinary paved road, passing farmland and forest to the ferry at La Arena, which crosses to Puelche. Further south, a second ferry connects Hornopirén to Caleta Gonzalo, running through fjords and mountains. Ferry timetables are tight and reservations may be needed in summer, so check before you commit. Rain is frequent all year, and the great temperate rainforest depends on it. The driest and warmest months are January and February. Pack full waterproofs, because showers can come suddenly even then.

    Puerto Montt is a big city of over a hundred and fifty thousand people, with fuel stations, motorcycle dealers, supermarkets and good hotels, so it is the place to sort out spares, tyres and supplies. Puerto Varas, seventeen kilometres away, is a more relaxed base. Fill up and buy extra snacks and water before the ferries, and keep cash handy for ticket offices, which do not always take cards.

  2. Church of Colo sight

    Colo is one of the quiet places of Chiloé that most visitors never reach, a small settlement on the eastern side of the big island, with a wooden church on a rise above the water. It belongs to the family of churches that the Jesuits and local carpenters created in the colonial and early republic periods, and which UNESCO protects together. Their charm is simple. A front porch of columns, a tower with an octagonal top and a roof like an upturned boat. Inside, the nave is a hull of curved timbers, the ceilings painted with stars or sky, the altars carved by local hands. They were built without nails in some cases, using wooden pegs, and the big planks were cut by axe.

    What I can say is that the churches of this part of the island are linked to a communal tradition, with annual festivals and a body of people who maintain the buildings, called fiscales, who look after the keys and the services. If the church is open, step in quietly, and if it is closed, the porch and the setting are rewarding enough, with the green hill, the sea beyond and the smell of woodsmoke from the nearby farms.

    The road from Dalcahue, a market town across the water from the island of Quinchao, runs south through farmland and woods, along the coast and over low rises. The asphalt is good in places, and in others replaced by gravel, with rain turning ruts into puddles and mud in winter. Traffic is light and mostly local, with farm trucks, cattle and the occasional horse. Distances are modest, but weather decides everything, and it is wise to plan extra time. Summer, from December to March, is the driest and warmest period, though you should still pack waterproofs.

    Dalcahue, twenty-four kilometres away, has a ferry terminal, fuel, a Sunday craft market with woollens and baskets, and several seafood restaurants, so fill up there. Beyond it, supplies thin out and shops are small. Cash is useful, and a flask of something hot is welcome. Respect private land and farm gates, as many of the small chapels sit on or near working property, and a smile and a greeting go a long way with the people of the island.

  3. Church of Tenaún sight

    Out on the eastern coast of Chiloé, where the island looks across a channel at the smaller islands of the inner sea and, on a clear day, the snowy volcanoes of the mainland, the village of Tenaún gathers around a wooden church that looks almost too bold for its setting. The façade is painted deep blue, scattered with white stars, and topped not with one but with three towers. It is one of the sixteen churches of Chiloé on the World Heritage list, masterpieces of a unique tradition of wooden architecture.

    The churches were born from the encounter between Jesuit missionaries, who arrived in the seventeenth century, and the island's own people, Huilliche and mestizo, who were skilled boatbuilders and carpenters. Built entirely of local timber, with shingles of larch or cypress cladding the walls, they use joinery techniques borrowed from shipbuilding, and many interiors look like upturned hulls. The Franciscans later continued the mission, and the communities themselves have maintained the churches through the generations, often repairing them with their own hands.

    Riding in Chiloé is a slow, rewarding experience. The main road runs down the island from the ferry at Chacao through Ancud and Castro to Quellón, paved and generally good. Side roads like the one to Tenaún are often gravel, with steep, short climbs, sharp bends and potholes, and they can be very slippery after rain, which is frequent. The weather is famously changeable, with mist, drizzle and wind sweeping in from the Pacific, so good rain gear is essential. Summer, from December to March, brings the best chance of sunshine.

    The island has a strong identity, with its own myths and legends, including the Trauco, a forest creature blamed for unexplained pregnancies, and the Caleuche, a ghost ship that sails the channels. The landscape is green and rolling, with patches of native forest, small farms and fishing villages. Look out for black-necked swans, dolphins and sea lions along the coast.

    Dalcahue, about twenty-three kilometres away, is the nearest town with fuel and services, and its Sunday market is great for woollen goods and seafood. Castro, the island's capital, is further south, known for its colourful palafitos, houses on stilts over the water. Try curanto, a feast of shellfish, meat and potatoes cooked in a pit with hot stones and nalca leaves.

  4. Church of San Juan sight

    San Juan is a tiny village on a sheltered inlet on the east coast of Chiloé, the big green island off the coast of southern Chile, and its wooden church stands right by the water, painted white with a tall tower, looking out over the boats moored in the channel. It is one of the sixteen Churches of Chiloé on the World Heritage list, a unique school of wooden architecture that grew out of the Jesuit missions of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and was continued by the Franciscans and the islanders themselves. The churches were built by local carpenters using native woods, with techniques borrowed from boat building, and many of them are covered in wooden shingles. Inside, the vaulted ceilings often look like the hull of an upturned ship.

    Chiloé has its own culture, distinct from mainland Chile, with myths of witches, ghost ships and forest spirits, such as the Trauco and the Pincoya, and a tradition of community work called minga, in which neighbours help each other, sometimes even moving entire houses across the land or the water with oxen and boats. The landscape is soft and green, with rolling hills, patches of native forest, pastures and fjords, and it rains a lot, in every season.

    To reach the island, you take a ferry across the Chacao channel from Pargua, a short crossing with frequent departures, often with dolphins in the water. The Pan-American highway, Route 5, runs down the middle of the island from Ancud to Castro and on to Quellón, where it ends. The road to Dalcahue and San Juan branches off towards the east coast, winding through hills and villages, paved on the main routes, with gravel on the side roads. Rain, wind and mud are constant companions, and the roads can be slippery, so good waterproofs are essential.

    Dalcahue, about thirteen kilometres away, is a small town with fuel, shops and a famous Sunday market of crafts and food, and Castro, the island's capital, has the palafitos, wooden houses on stilts over the water, and everything you need. Try curanto, the traditional dish of Chiloé, in which seafood, meat, potatoes and dumplings are cooked in a pit in the ground, covered with nalca leaves and heated with hot stones, a feast for many people.

  5. Church of Dalcahue turn

    Chiloé is a damp, green island off the coast of southern Chile, and its wooden churches are its soul. The Church of Dalcahue stands on the waterfront of a small town on the eastern side of the island, facing the inner sea and the islands of the archipelago, with a long arcaded porch along its front and a tall tower rising above. Built entirely of wood, with shingles cut from native trees, it is one of sixteen churches inscribed in 2000 as the Churches of Chiloé.

    The churches reflect a meeting of cultures. Jesuit missionaries arrived in the seventeenth century and set up a circuit of missions around the islands, and later Franciscans continued the work. The local people, Indigenous Huilliche and mestizo islanders, built the churches using their skills as boat builders and carpenters, joining timbers with wooden pegs and covering walls in shingles. The result is a unique style, simple yet elegant, with vaulted ceilings that look like the inverted hull of a ship.

    Dalcahue is famous for its Sunday market, where islanders sell woollen goods, wooden crafts, potatoes, seafood and baskets. The town also has palafitos, houses on stilts over the water, and a small ferry crosses to the island of Quinchao, where the church at Achao is one of the oldest in the group. Chiloé's cuisine is distinctive: curanto, a feast of shellfish, meat, potatoes and dumplings cooked in a pit with hot stones and covered with leaves, is the most famous dish, and there are many potato varieties grown only on the island.

    Riding in Chiloé is not about speed. The main road, Ruta 5, runs down the island from the ferry at Chacao to Quellón in the south, while smaller roads lead to villages and churches, often unpaved and muddy. Rain is frequent all year, sometimes for days, and the wind can be strong. The landscape is lush and green, with rolling hills, forests and sea views. Summer, from December to February, is the driest season.

    Dalcahue has fuel and basic services, and Castro, fifteen kilometres away, is the island's capital, with more shops, restaurants and accommodation. The ferry from the mainland at Pargua takes about half an hour. Bring good waterproofs.
